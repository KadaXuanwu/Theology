# Description
Creation alone does not leave every person "[[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse|without excuse]]". The verdict needs a knowledge some peoples show no sign of having, or a moral premise the world does not supply. [Romans 1:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:20&version=NIV) says God's eternal power and divine nature have been clear from what he made, so people are without excuse. [Romans 1:21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:21&version=NIV) gives the ground: "although they knew God", they did not glorify him or give thanks. The verse names power and divinity, not a person, a name or a moral demand.

[[Lari Launonen]] reports that many theologians apply the passage to "all cognitively mature and healthy humans" (p. 264).[^launonen-2025] [[Joseph Fitzmyer]] calls the knowing a "vague, unformulated knowledge or experience of God" that Paul thinks "pagans could not help but have" (p. 281, quoted by [[Richard Alan Young|Young]] at p. 705).[^young-2000] On that reading a people's record can test the verse.

The mind brings a readiness to see agents and purpose, and culture supplies which beings, as [[Humans Are Primed to See Agents and Purpose]] sets out.[^norenzayan-2016] Among foragers, spirits and gods have "only limited powers and circumscribed concerns about human morality" (p. 3).[^norenzayan-2016] [[Justin Barrett]] argues that "some kind of super-knowing creator god" comes naturally. A chapter he co-wrote says it does not follow that a God outside time and dependent on nothing comes naturally, and cites "some evidence suggesting to the contrary".[^visala-2018]

Some peoples have no maker or ruler of the world in their record. In the 33 forager societies of [[Peoples, Duda & Marlowe 2016]], animism is coded in all, and a maker or ruler of all things in 13, active in 5.[^peoples-2016] The other 20 are "absent or not reported".[^lightner-2023] The Pirahã have no creation myth, by the account of [[Daniel Everett]] and of those who dispute his wider claims.[^everett-2005][^nevins-2009] [[Jason Marsh]] argues from such evidence that nonbelief of this kind is more expected if there is no God than if there is (p. 360). He notes it "at the very least poses a problem" for reading Romans 1 as making all nonbelief culpable (p. 373).[^marsh-2013]

For people with no such awareness the verdict needs a further premise. Universal suppression ([Romans 1:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:18&version=NIV)) is weighed in [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]], and Launonen finds that version "seems hopeless", though evidence supports a partial one (pp. 275–276).[^launonen-2025] [[Evan Fales]] presses a related point against [[Alvin Plantinga]] at pp. 361 and 364. In Fales's reading, unbelief is explained by a damaged faculty which is itself identified by belief in Christian doctrine, so nothing independent is left to show the faculty is there.[^fales-2003] An inborn corruption comes, as Launonen puts it, "according to theological tradition, from Adam" (p. 274).[^launonen-2025] In Plantinga's model, damage to the sense of God is "like other disease and handicaps", "not necessarily sin on the part of the person" (p. 214, in a note).[^plantinga-wcb]

# Based On
- [Romans 1:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:20&version=NIV) what is seen in what God made is "his eternal power and divine nature".
- [Romans 1:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:18&version=NIV) people suppress the truth.
- [Romans 1:21–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:21-23&version=NIV) the target is idol worship, so Paul is arguing with religious people, not atheists. [[Mark A. Seifrid|Mark Seifrid]] finds the charge aimed at "those who worship idols" (p. 116), and Launonen reads Paul as "attacking polytheism rather than atheism" (p. 264).[^seifrid-1998][^launonen-2025] A people who worship nothing therefore tests the later extension to everyone.
- Paul's argument echoes [Wisdom of Solomon 13:1–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Wisdom%20of%20Solomon%2013:1-9&version=NRSVUE), a parallel Joseph Fitzmyer draws at his note on 1:20 and Young calls "striking" (p. 700).[^fitzmyer-1993][^young-2000] There is "no question of direct quotation" (pp. 51–52).[^sanday-headlam-1895] Wisdom says people who mistake creation for God could not "know the one who exists" (13:1); Paul says they knew. It grants that they "are little to be blamed, for perhaps they go astray while seeking God and desiring to find him" (13:6). Then it rules that "not even they are to be excused" (13:8).
- [[John Calvin|Calvin]]'s *Institutes* I.3 argues the innate sense of God from universal religious practice. The 1845 English text quotes no scripture, and its authorities are ancient writers.[^calvin-institutes] Fales makes the cross-cultural test at p. 361: the view "would indicate decided unawareness of the data provided by ethnography".[^fales-2003]
- [[Peoples, Duda & Marlowe 2016]] the forager numbers, from 33 societies chosen by how they get food.[^peoples-2016] The code counts a being "believed to have created all reality and/or to be its ultimate governor", even a remote one.[^lightner-2023]
- [[Kelemen 2004]] the purpose bias in children's reasoning about nature. [[Deborah Kelemen|Kelemen]] describes "a nonhuman 'somebody'" that "may be elaborated by a particular religious culture" (p. 297). She draws this "tentatively" and calls cross-cultural research "limited" (p. 299).[^kelemen-2004] London children from mostly Christian and Muslim families named "God, Jesus, Allah" as maker of natural things.[^kelemen-diyanni-2005]
- Seeing purpose is not yet seeing design, on one reading of the developmental work: "Humans are not intuitive creationists".[^decruz-2017] Under time pressure, non-religious adults more often judge natural things "purposefully made by some being", a being the task left "deliberately non-specific".[^jarnefelt-2015]
- [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]] people not fighting God who still do not believe.
- [[Daniel Everett]] reports that the Pirahã have no creation stories or myths, and that pressed on it they say only "Everything is the same" (pp. 632–633). "Nothing was created" is his gloss.[^everett-2005] [[Andrew Nevins|Nevins]], [[David Pesetsky|Pesetsky]] and [[Cilene Rodrigues|Rodrigues]] dispute his explanation and his claim of no myths at all, but not the missing creation myth, on which they report that [[Marco Antônio Gonçalves|Gonçalves]] agrees (p. 392).[^nevins-2009]
- Source checks still open on this node are tracked in the repo under `Verification/`.

# Countered By
- [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] is the claim this one is aimed at.
- The strongest counter is Calvin's own reading. Reason "cannot ascertain who or what sort of being God is", though the light of creation is "sufficiently clear". Knowing that some Deity, "whoever he may be", is owed worship is enough: "we cannot pretend any excuse".[^calvin-romans] The fault is in the person: "the dullness which prevents us is within" (*Institutes* I.5.15).[^calvin-institutes] Launonen builds the same culpability case on a different model, from [[C. Stephen Evans]]'s principle that a good God's evidence would be "widely accessible but also easy to resist" (p. 271). Nonbelievers who find the question important and hold their view with doubt "arguably have the responsibility to carefully consider the evidence" (p. 273).[^launonen-2025] Young grants at p. 704 that the awareness "does not fully disclose the Creator", then relocates the culpability.[^young-2000] It is enough to prompt someone to ask, so the failure is never asking rather than answering wrongly. He does not discuss cross-cultural numbers.
- Seifrid denies that what creation gives is thin: it is "full and sufficient for the creature to worship him rightly" (p. 119).[^seifrid-1998]
- [[C. K. Barrett]] and [[C. E. B. Cranfield]], as [[James Barr]] quotes them, deny that Paul is teaching a knowledge of God that reason reaches from the world. For Cranfield creation yields no natural knowledge "independent of God's self-revelation in his Word", but "simply the excuselessness of men in their ignorance" (p. 41).[^barr-1993] To Barr both read as "confessional assertions" (p. 43).[^barr-1993] Data on belief cannot touch this reading; only the moral premise can.
- [[Kelly James Clark]] and Barrett argue that the sense of God "may be reliable in producing belief in a divinity". By their own count its reliability for belief in Yahweh alone "is less than 1/2" (p. 187).[^clark-barrett-2010]
- [[Max Baker-Hytch]] argues that believing in "a personal first cause of the universe" gives knowledge of "the God of monotheism if He exists", even unawares (pp. 131–132, 231).[^baker-hytch-2014]
- These thin readings pay a price: one that animism, polytheism and no creation story all satisfy predicts no outcome, as [[No Outcome Counts Against It]] sets out.
- [[Thomas Aquinas]] reads the verse as knowledge reasoned from God's effects, held by the wise among the Gentiles, whose later ignorance came from their fault (cap. 1 lect. 6–7).[^aquinas-romans] Elsewhere he holds that reason alone brings few to it, late, and with error.[^aquinas-scg]
- [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]] moves the evidence to after the commitment, and [[Religious Experience Is Evidence for God]] offers evidence of another kind. [[Hell Is Self-Chosen Separation]] answers only the fairness half.

# Limits
- The case turns on what Paul means by "although they knew God" in [Romans 1:21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:21&version=NIV). If he means a knowledge every people holds, it would show in their record. Then a people with no maker or ruler of the world in its record counts against the verse. If he means an awareness that can leave no trace in belief, no record counts against it, and the case rests on the moral premise below. On Aquinas's reading, where the wise knew and later ignorance came from their fault, it rests there too. Barr reports New Testament specialists divided on whether Romans 1 teaches any natural knowledge of God (pp. 40–41).[^barr-1993] Opening the major commentaries on 1:21 would show which reading they hold. On the first reading, a test of design intuitions in a small society with no creator in its record would settle the facts. So would recoding from primary ethnography the 20 forager societies coded "absent or not reported". Counting thanks and offerings would not, since knowing God and giving no thanks is what 1:21 describes.
- The moral premise is that ignorance a person did not cause excuses, and no study supplies it. The verse's verdict is excuselessness for failing to glorify and thank, not a penalty for a wrong guess. Aquinas states the rule for this very passage: ignorance excuses only when it is not itself caused by fault (cap. 1 lect. 7).[^aquinas-romans] So the test, as in [[Invincible Ignorance Excuses Unbelief]], is whether the inquiry a person skipped was open to them. Where belief needs a desire for God, Launonen finds culpability "falls back on" whether humans answer for "the morally bad character with which they are born" (p. 274).[^launonen-2025] Wisdom of Solomon 13:6 concedes the mitigating half of the premise and overrides it anyway. Someone who perceives a power owed honour and gives it none falls under the verdict on an ordinary moral premise.
- The forager evidence has its own problems, kept in [[Peoples, Duda & Marlowe 2016]]. Its reconstruction leaves open a single inactive creator among ancestral hunter-gatherers. The paper does not code missionary contact.[^peoples-2016] It takes 28 of its 33 societies from the Standard Cross-Cultural Sample.[^peoples-2016] That sample's codes for such a being rest on sources with a median date of 1920 (p. 562).[^lightner-2023]
- [[Matthew Braddock]] questions whether prehistoric humans really were natural nonbelievers, since "the empirical jury is still out on what prehistoric humans believed about gods". He argues that even if it was prevalent, "it would not be very surprising given theism", and suggests that "widespread belief in a high god" is "a problem for naturalism".[^braddock-2022] In the full Standard Cross-Cultural Sample, 100 of the 168 societies coded have a maker or ruler of all things, 47 of them inactive.[^dplace-sccs]
- The Pirahã are not irreligious. They claim direct experience of spirits (p. 622).[^everett-2005] Everett says the word is his, for beings that to the Pirahã are "broadly human, a different species of jungle hominid".[^everett-2009] What is missing is a creation myth, which is narrower than no God-belief. A disputed fragment has a being, Igagai, make animals, a moon and rivers and remake the world, with no origin given for him (p. 392).[^nevins-2009][^everett-2009] They are also one small society and a contested ethnography. Illustration, not proof.
- The argument tests what people take from the world, not the world, and does not reach whether the world is evidence for a creator. Barrett and his co-author hold that the cognitive research "should be understood as methodologically agnostic, not methodologically atheistic".[^visala-2018]
