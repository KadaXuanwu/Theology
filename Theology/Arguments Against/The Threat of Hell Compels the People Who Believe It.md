# Description
A free will defence of hell that also treats hell as a threat God issues defeats its own purpose for the people who believe the threat. [[Richard Swinburne|Swinburne]]'s version holds both halves: if the freedom to reject God is real, God "must after a finite time take no for an answer", and the pain of hell is a "threat" and "a more powerful deterrent to the hard-hearted".[^swinburne-1998] [[Michael J. Murray|Murray]] supplies the mechanism. A threat compels in proportion to its strength and to how imminent the threatened person takes it to be, and God keeps the threat of hell epistemically ambiguous so that the human response to him stays morally significant, a choice among live options rather than one narrowed to a single option by fear.[^murray-1993]

The texts command love of God, back the command with an eternal penalty God is named as imposing, and command fear of him as its imposer. [Deuteronomy 6:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%206:5&version=NIV) commands love with all the heart, soul and strength, [Matthew 25:46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2025:46&version=NIV) has the condemned "go away to eternal punishment", and [Matthew 10:28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010:28&version=NIV) commands fear of "the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell".

A conditional penalty announced by the one who will carry it out is a threat, not a warning of a natural consequence. Murray draws that line himself, between a robber's gun and a blizzard outside the door, treats God's penalties as the robber's kind, and rates the threat of damnation "equal to the strongest imaginable threat".[^murray-1993]

A believer convinced of that threat who chooses God partly to avoid it meets [[Robert Nozick|Nozick]]'s conditions for coercion: a credible threat from an agent, compliance, and avoiding the threatened consequence as part of the reason.[^sep-coercion][^nozick-1969] Murray himself writes that those "aware of such threats and are convinced of their veracity are in a state where their freedom is at risk", which creates "some degree of compulsion by another in the context of the threat. Specifically, it is compulsion by God".[^murray-1993] He reserves "clearly would be compelled" for creatures "confronted by such obvious threats", has God calibrate general revelation to "the least wanton individual", and never squares that with the compulsion he has just placed on everyone convinced of the threat.[^murray-1993] The compulsion comes from the sanction and not from knowing that God exists: [[Thomas Talbott|Talbott]] separates a revelation that changes a mind from coercion of a will, and [[J. L. Schellenberg|Schellenberg]] holds at pp. 109–111 that an already formed belief, even in God's existence, can be shut out and that believing does not carry obeying with it.[^talbott-sep][^schellenberg-1993]

[[Aristotle]] and [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] class acts done from fear as mixed and count them voluntary, since the will moves toward them, unlike compulsion, where the will does nothing.[^aristotle-ne][^aquinas-st-i-ii-6] The Stanford encyclopedia's survey of coercion separates whether a threat leaves the agent's freedom intact from whether the choice under it is voluntary, and reports a test on which a choice made because there is no acceptable alternative is not voluntary at all, whatever it does to freedom.[^sep-coercion][^olsaretti-2004] For a believer convinced of the threat, hell is no acceptable alternative, so the choice is the will's own act on Aquinas's test and a forced one on that test.

What a threat produces is fear and compliance, and in the covenant texts that compliance is what "love" names. Deuteronomy's love is covenant loyalty, in [[William L. Moran|Moran]]'s words "a love that can be commanded", the loyalty vassal treaties demanded under curse.[^moran-1963] It does not produce affection: [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]] holds that love as an inclination cannot be commanded, only practical love, the willing of another's good.[^kant-groundwork] The tradition itself denies that fear driven devotion is charity. Aquinas calls fear that turns to God to escape punishment servile and its servility "contrary to charity"; [[Augustine]] calls fear the bristle that draws in the thread of charity; [1 John 4:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%204:18&version=NIV) sets fear, which "has to do with punishment", against perfect love.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-19][^augustine-1john]

So for the believer who takes the texts at their word, the arrangement said to keep the response free places it under a compulsion its own defender names and does not measure, and produces loyalty under fear, which the texts call love and the tradition says is not charity.

# Based On
- [Deuteronomy 30:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2030:15-20&version=NIV) makes loving God the condition of life against "death and destruction". [Matthew 25:41](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2025:41&version=NIV) sends the cursed "into the eternal fire"; [2 Thessalonians 1:8–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Thessalonians%201:8-9&version=NIV) names "everlasting destruction" for those who do not obey the gospel; [Revelation 20:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation%2020:15&version=NIV) throws into the lake of fire anyone not written in the book of life, and [Revelation 21:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation%2021:8&version=NIV) puts "the unbelieving" there, in "the second death". Whether aiōnios in Matthew 25:46 means endless is disputed, see [[No Finite Life Deserves Infinite Punishment]]; the lake of fire and the second death do not turn on that word.
- Moran, at pp. 80–82, cites the Assyrian succession oath of 672 BC, which orders the vassals to love the crown prince as themselves, and at p. 83 the treaty curse of a brazen heaven and iron ground that [Deuteronomy 28:23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2028:23&version=NIV) repeats.[^moran-1963][^barcina-2016]
- Schellenberg adds at pp. 129–130, from [Romans 7:15–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%207:15-19&version=NIV), that Paul's own conviction did not stop his bad actions;[^schellenberg-1993] Talbott's example is the Damascus road.[^talbott-sep]
- Swinburne's *Providence and the Problem of Evil* calls his free will defence libertarian in its second chapter; the pain of hell as a "threat" that "so often has started the hard-hearted on the road to sanctity" is at pp. 205–206, and "take no for an answer" is endnote 21 to chapter 11.[^swinburne-1998]
- Self determination theory classes acting to avoid a threatened punishment as controlled regulation, set against "regulation based on coercive forces".[^deci-2000] A 1993 study found religious practice from guilt or fear of disapproval went with more anxiety and depression, and practice from personal value with less, in two of four Christian samples;[^ryan-1993] a Belgian Catholic sample replicated the well being pattern.[^neyrinck-2006] All of it is cross sectional, none measures love of God as the outcome or hell as the predictor, and the one longitudinal study models meaning in life, not the movement from fear to identification.[^kerasha-2026]

# Countered By
- Augustine, Aquinas and the Council of Trent, the Catholic council that met from 1545 to 1563, give the strongest answer: fear of punishment is a first stage charity replaces and never the love itself; charity is infused by God and "cannot be natural or acquired by the natural powers";[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-24] God moves the free will to accept grace and the will still moves;[^aquinas-st-i-ii-113] and, in Trent's decree, a person under grace "is also able to reject it", "the fear of divine justice" is a step by which people "are profitably agitated" toward hope, and calling the fear of hell that drives a person to God's mercy a sin is anathema.[^trent-1547] Aquinas adds that the fear of punishment itself can remain alongside charity; only its servility, acting as if moved from outside, cannot.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-19] The threat leaves the power to refuse intact; [[Alex R. Gillham|Gillham]], at p. 249: "even those who believe that damnation in Hell will result from sin often refuse the offer proposed".[^gillham-2020] So there is coerced fear and no coerced love, and the freedom hell protects, the power to refuse, is not the kind a resistible threat removes.
- Gillham's paper, written in defence of the view that hell exists but no one is ever sent there, runs on Nozick, for whom coercion must succeed, so "the frequency with which many of us sin therefore entails that God's use of Hell is not coercive" (p. 247), and on [[Harry G. Frankfurt|Frankfurt]], for whom a threat coerces only when overbearing (pp. 248–249).[^gillham-2020] Neither reaches the believer who does comply from fear: for that one agent Nozick's conditions are met, which Gillham grants ("It would be coercive if one were to presuppose Nozick's account of coercion", p. 249), and the encyclopedia notes that "few besides Frankfurt have taken such overburdening to be a necessary condition for coercion".[^gillham-2020][^sep-coercion]
- [[Hell Is Self-Chosen Separation]] reads the same passages as describing what refusal is, not a penalty God adds; the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* calls hell "definitive self-exclusion from communion with God" and says God "predestines no one to go to hell".[^catechism] If so there is no sanction and the argument loses its target. Against it stand the texts' surface, "Depart from me, you who are cursed, into the eternal fire", and Swinburne's own account of the pain of hell as a threat God leaves in place.[^swinburne-1998]
- [[God Can't Accept Sin]] derives the separation from God's nature rather than from a penalty he chose; the same reply applies.

# Limits
- Everything turns on what the free will defence means by freedom. If it means a range of live options that another's threat can narrow, as Murray uses it, a believer convinced of the threat is under what Murray himself calls some degree of compulsion by God, and Murray declines to say whether that degree defeats the freedom hiddenness is meant to protect, setting the relation between threats and freedom aside "for the purposes of this essay".[^murray-1993] If it means the power to consent to God or to refuse him, as Aquinas and the Council of Trent use it, no threat short of force removes it, and people who believe in hell refuse anyway. [[Jerry Walls|Walls]]'s *Hell: The Logic of Damnation* and Swinburne's 1983 essay, read for what each says that freedom is, would settle how far the argument reaches.[^walls-1992][^swinburne-1983]
- A second question is open: whether devotion that begins in fear becomes a love that would survive the threat's removal. Augustine and Trent say it does; the nearest psychology on fear driven religious motivation runs the other way in direction; no study has measured it.[^augustine-1john][^trent-1547][^ryan-1993][^kerasha-2026] Whether commanded action and habit can produce affection indirectly is open; [[Belief Isn't Directly Chosen]] holds the parallel question for belief.
- Reach. The reductio lands on Murray's compulsion premise; his calibration reply never meets it.[^murray-1993] It reaches Swinburne so far as he calls his theodicy libertarian and the pain of hell a deterrent threat, though he grounds the loss of God in a choice God must in the end let stand.[^swinburne-1998] It reaches Walls only as the Stanford encyclopedia reports him, grounding hell in a settled self deception God will not strip away rather than in a threat.[^talbott-sep][^walls-1992] It does not reach the Augustinian line, Augustine and [[John Calvin|Calvin]] as the encyclopedia groups them, for whom salvation is wholly grace, nor Aquinas, for whom charity is infused; hell there was never a means to free love.[^talbott-sep][^aquinas-st-ii-ii-24] The encyclopedia classes the free will theodicy of hell as an Arminian position that accepts libertarian free will, and [[C. P. Ragland|Ragland]] writes that "the free will view relies on incompatibilism".[^talbott-sep][^ragland-iep] Against the Augustinian and Thomist accounts a residue stands: the fearful believer's consent is coerced and voluntary at once, and no source says whether a mixed act stays voluntary at infinite stakes.
- The argument borrows a premise from the position it attacks and has no standalone force; it carries no premise that a loving God would not threaten. In print it is raised and answered by Gillham, and stands as an unreconciled premise in Murray.[^gillham-2020][^murray-1993] Murray's paper is 33 years old; [[Travis Dumsday|Dumsday]] still called the freedom reply "one prominent line of reply" in 2010.[^dumsday-2010]
- "Love" has four senses here: covenant loyalty, practical love, affection and charity. The argument holds in the first; the tradition itself grants that a threat produces neither of the last two.[^moran-1963][^kant-groundwork][^aquinas-st-ii-ii-24]
