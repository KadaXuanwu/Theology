# Description
A free will defence of hell that also treats hell as a threat God issues narrows, for believers in the threat, the free choice it keeps God hidden to protect. What it offers them in return is a move from fear to doing good for its own sake, which no study found has measured for fear of hell. [[Richard Swinburne|Swinburne]]'s version holds both halves. If the freedom to reject God is real, God "must after a finite time take no for an answer". He also calls the pain of hell a "threat" and "a more powerful deterrent to the hard-hearted".[^swinburne-1998]

For [[Michael J. Murray|Murray]], a threat compels in proportion to its strength and to how imminent the threatened person takes it to be. God keeps the threat of hell epistemically ambiguous so that the response to him stays a morally significant choice among live options, not one narrowed by fear.[^murray-1993] For Swinburne, if God allocates afterlife fates by how we live, then other things being equal belief in God "will deprive us of that serious choice between good and bad".[^swinburne-1998]

The texts command love of God, and the New Testament names an eternal penalty that God, or Christ as judge, imposes. [Deuteronomy 6:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%206:5&version=NIV) commands love with all the heart, soul and strength. [Matthew 25:46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2025:46&version=NIV) has the condemned "go away to eternal punishment". [Matthew 10:28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010:28&version=NIV) commands fear of "the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell". An 1897 commentary reports that most commentators take that One to be God, and itself reads "the devil whose agent he is".[^bruce-1897]

A conditional penalty announced by the one who will carry it out is a threat, not a warning of a natural consequence. Murray draws that line himself, between a robber's gun and a blizzard outside the door. He treats God's penalties as the robber's kind and rates the threat of damnation "equal to the strongest imaginable threat".[^murray-1993]

A believer convinced of that threat who chooses God partly to avoid it meets the six conditions of [[Robert Nozick|Nozick]]'s account of coercion, as the Stanford encyclopedia simplifies them. In summary: an agent who makes a credible threat in order to stop an act, compliance, and avoiding the threatened consequence as part of the reason.[^sep-coercion][^nozick-1969] Murray himself writes that those "aware of such threats and are convinced of their veracity are in a state where their freedom is at risk". That creates "some degree of compulsion by another in the context of the threat. Specifically, it is compulsion by God".[^murray-1993] He reserves "clearly would be compelled" for creatures "confronted by such obvious threats" and has God calibrate general revelation to "the least wanton individual". He does not say how the compulsion he has just placed on everyone convinced of the threat stays below the level that would defeat their freedom.[^murray-1993] The compulsion comes from the sanction and not from knowing that God exists. [[J. L. Schellenberg|Schellenberg]], though, holds at p. 124 that if punishment "is seen as something in the future, its deterrent effect must be greatly reduced".[^schellenberg-1993]

[[Aristotle]] and [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] class acts done from fear as [[Mixed act|mixed]] and count them voluntary, since the will moves toward them, unlike compulsion, where the will does nothing.[^aristotle-ne][^aquinas-st-i-ii-6] The Stanford encyclopedia reports a test on which a choice made because there is no acceptable alternative is not voluntary, whatever it does to freedom.[^sep-coercion][^olsaretti-2004] For a believer who chooses God because hell is the only alternative, the choice is the will's own act on Aquinas's test and a forced one on that test.

What a threat produces is fear and compliance. On [[William L. Moran|Moran]]'s reading Deuteronomy's "love" is covenant loyalty, "a love that can be commanded", tied to "fear and reverence" and shown in "unqualified obedience". It is the allegiance Assyrian loyalty oaths demanded under curse.[^moran-1963] A 2019 survey adds that scholars also suggest God's "passionate love for Israel demands a reciprocal emotional response".[^grant-2019] In the tradition, Aquinas calls fear that turns to God to escape punishment [[Servile fear|servile]], and its servility "contrary to charity".[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-19] [[Augustine]] calls fear the bristle that draws in the thread of charity.[^augustine-1john] [1 John 4:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%204:18&version=NIV) sets fear, which "has to do with punishment", against perfect love.

# Based On
- [Deuteronomy 30:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2030:15-20&version=NIV) makes loving and obeying God the condition of life in the land, against "death and destruction". [Matthew 25:41](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2025:41&version=NIV) sends the cursed "into the eternal fire", and [2 Thessalonians 1:8–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Thessalonians%201:8-9&version=NIV) names "everlasting destruction" for those who do not obey the gospel. [Revelation 20:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation%2020:15&version=NIV) throws into the lake of fire anyone not written in the book of life, and [Revelation 21:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation%2021:8&version=NIV) puts "the unbelieving" there, in "the second death". Whether aiōnios in Matthew 25:46 means endless is disputed, see [[No Finite Life Deserves Infinite Punishment]]; the lake of fire and the second death do not turn on that word.
- Moran, at pp. 80–82, cites the Assyrian succession oath of 672 BC, which orders those who swear it to love the crown prince "like yourselves".[^moran-1963][^parpola-1988][^barcina-2016]
- Swinburne's *Providence and the Problem of Evil* calls his free will defence [[Libertarian free will|libertarian]] in its second chapter. At p. 205 the threat of the pain of hell "through the centuries so often has" started the hard-hearted "on the road to sanctity".[^swinburne-1998]
- [[Self determination theory]] classes acting to avoid a threatened punishment as controlled regulation.[^deci-2000] A 1993 study found religious practice from pressure to win approval went with more anxiety and depression in both Christian samples that measured them. Practice from personal value went with less.[^ryan-1993] That study is cross sectional.

# Countered By
- The strong counter is Swinburne's own. Threats serve "as a preliminary to children learning to do good for its own sake", after which "threats are less needed".[^swinburne-1998] He warns that preaching "the horrors of Hell" can make people seek heaven "for the wrong reason" and so be "unsuited for Heaven". God therefore leaves heaven less than "completely obvious", but "would deprive no one of Heaven who seeks to do the good for its own sake".[^swinburne-2005] He claims no more for the move from fear than that, once a habit of good acts begins, doing good for the better reason "becomes a serious possibility".[^swinburne-1998]
- Augustine, Aquinas and the Catholic Council of Trent give a second answer: fear of punishment is a first stage charity replaces and never the love itself.[^augustine-1john][^trent-1547] The fear of punishment itself can remain alongside charity; only its servility, acting as if moved from outside, cannot.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-19] Trent anathematised the claim that the will moved by God "cannot refuse its consent, if it would".[^trent-1547] It called it false that contrition from fear of hell is "extorted and forced, not free and voluntary". Such sorrow, it said, is "a gift of God, and an impulse of the Holy Ghost".[^trent-1551] Aquinas and most modern theology reading Trent differ on whether [[Attrition|attrition]] must be perfected by charity for the sacrament to justify.[^deletter-1955] So on this answer fear of punishment may be servile, love cannot be, and the freedom hell protects, the power to refuse, is not the kind a resistible threat removes.
- [[Alex R. Gillham|Gillham]] defends the view that hell exists but no one is ever sent there. For Nozick coercion must succeed, so "the frequency with which many of us sin therefore entails that God's use of Hell is not coercive" (p. 247). For [[Harry G. Frankfurt|Frankfurt]] a threat coerces only when overbearing (pp. 248–249).[^gillham-2020] For the believer who does comply from fear, Gillham writes at p. 249 that on Nozick's account God's use of hell "would be coercive". He answers that an offer meeting Nozick's six conditions "appears insufficient" to limit freedom "to an objectionable degree".[^gillham-2020] The Stanford encyclopedia notes that "few besides Frankfurt" make overbearing a necessary condition.[^sep-coercion]
- [[Hell Is Self-Chosen Separation]] reads the same passages as describing what refusal is, not a penalty God adds. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* calls hell "definitive self-exclusion from communion with God" and says God "predestines no one to go to hell".[^catechism] For [[Jonathan Kvanvig|Kvanvig]], hell is "the only alternative compatible with what they have chosen", and God's "primary motivation is always love".[^kvanvig-1993] That removes Nozick's first condition, an aim to deter, though Kvanvig's God still sends people to hell. The voluntariness test still applies, since it also covers choices forced by circumstances.[^kvanvig-1993][^sep-coercion] Against this reading stand the texts' surface, "Depart from me, you who are cursed, into the eternal fire", and Swinburne's account of hell as a threat.[^swinburne-1998]

# Limits
- Everything turns on what the free will defence wants the freedom for. If it wants each response to God to be a serious choice among live options, a believed threat narrows it, as Murray says and as Swinburne says of God's hiddenness.[^murray-1993][^swinburne-1998] Murray sets aside "for the purposes of this essay" which of three accounts of threats and freedom is right. Later he writes that unless the threat's imminence or the person's wantonness can be controlled, the morally significant use of free will "would be precluded".[^murray-1993] If the defence wants only that a life keep the power to refuse God and to come to do good for its own sake, a resistible threat leaves that standing. Trent holds that the will moved by grace can refuse, and as Gillham writes, people who believe in hell often refuse anyway.[^trent-1547][^gillham-2020] Swinburne holds both.[^swinburne-1998][^swinburne-2005] [[Jerry Walls|Walls]]'s *Hell: The Logic of Damnation* and Swinburne's 1983 essay would settle which one each defender means.[^walls-1992][^swinburne-1983]
- A second question is open: whether devotion that begins in fear becomes a love that would survive the threat's removal. Augustine says it does, and Trent's decree on justification puts the beginning of love after fear and hope.[^augustine-1john][^trent-1547] The nearest psychology is indirect. In one 2026 study, students whose religious practice was mainly controlled more often moved to more autonomous profiles than to the amotivated one. It did not measure fear of hell, and no study found measures fear of hell turning into love of God.[^kerasha-2026-transitions]
- Reach. The [[Reductio ad absurdum|reductio]] lands on Murray's compulsion premise; his calibration reply does not say where the convinced believer falls.[^murray-1993] It reaches Swinburne so far as he calls his theodicy libertarian and the pain of hell a deterrent threat. He grounds the loss of God, though, in a choice God must in the end let stand.[^swinburne-1998] It does not reach the Augustinian line, Augustine and [[John Calvin|Calvin]] as the encyclopedia groups them, for whom salvation is wholly grace.[^talbott-sep] Nor does it reach Aquinas, for whom charity is infused; hell there was never a means to free love.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-24] The encyclopedia classes the free will theodicy of hell as an [[Arminianism|Arminian]] position that accepts libertarian free will, which [[C. P. Ragland|Ragland]] ties to [[Incompatibilism|incompatibilism]].[^talbott-sep][^ragland-iep] Against the Augustinian and Thomist accounts a residue stands. The fearful believer's consent is coerced and voluntary at once, and no source says whether a mixed act stays voluntary at infinite stakes.
- The argument borrows a premise from the position it attacks and has no standalone force; it carries no premise that a loving God would not threaten. Gillham answers it in print.[^gillham-2020] [[R. Zachary Manis]] makes it at pp. 207–212: for one who "genuinely believes" in hell, "Refusal is psychologically impossible".[^manis-2015] That rests on a premise he calls controversial, that freedom needs the ability to do otherwise. He adds that Murray's solution "appears to offer no help" to such a believer. A warning in place of a threat may not coerce, he grants, but it is "far less clear" that it leaves free choice intact.[^manis-2015] Murray's 2001 restatement answers only for people indifferent enough to threats to receive a clear revelation and stay free. It does not take up the believer convinced of hell through teaching.[^murray-2001]
