# Description
There is no coherent reason a just and all-powerful God would require sacrifice, whether animal offerings under the old covenant or the death of Jesus, as a precondition for forgiveness. A God able to forgive freely could simply do so; demanding blood first makes the mechanism look like an arbitrary or invented ritual rather than a moral necessity.

# Based On
- [Leviticus 17:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%2017:11&version=NIV) atonement by blood.
- [Hebrews 9:22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%209:22&version=NIV) without shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.
- The comparative point strengthens it and needs sourcing: substitutionary blood offering is standard across ancient Near Eastern religion, which is what makes it look like inherited practice rather than moral necessity.

# Countered By
- [[God Can't Accept Sin]] the requirement follows from what God is rather than from a rule he chose, so it is not arbitrary even though it is not negotiable.
- [[The Covenant Changed with Christ]] the animal system is presented as provisional from the start and is ended, which answers the "why keep doing it" half but not the "why any of it" half.
- The king and the lashes, the popular form of the penal substitution answer. Gold keeps going missing from a king's treasury, so he sets ten lashes for the thief. The thefts continue, he raises the count to twenty, then thirty, then forty, and at forty it is lethal. The thief turns out to be his daughter. A just king owes her the lashes and a loving father cannot let her take them, so at the hour of punishment he takes them in her place and dies. It circulates as an anonymous sermon illustration and needs a source for who tells it and where. The move behind it has a documented ancient form: Valerius Maximus writes that Zaleucus of Locri, under whose own law an adulterer lost both eyes, had one of his own eyes put out and one of his convicted son's, so the law got its full count and the son kept his sight ([*Memorable Doings and Sayings* 6.5.ext.3](https://www.attalus.org/translate/valerius6b.html)).
- The illustration answers the "why not simply forgive" half and concedes the rest. A ruler who holds a penalty in force and absorbs it himself is not waiving anything, which is [[God Can't Accept Sin]] put in a story. But the king sets ten, then forty. The count is his own decree, so what the story actually shows is a ruler treating a number he picked as binding on him, which is the arbitrariness the argument alleges rather than a reply to it. The law it borrows the scene from works the other way round. Theft there is repaid by restitution, double for a live animal and up to fivefold for a slaughtered ox ([Exodus 22:1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2022:1-4&version=NIV)), and flogging is capped at forty with the count set by what the crime deserves, not by how often the judge has been defied ([Deuteronomy 25:1–3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2025:1-3&version=NIV)).
- Two more places it does not reach. No court treats a sentence as served because a volunteer took it, so justice in the story means that someone suffered rather than that the guilty answered, and that is the thing in dispute rather than something the story establishes. And the king stays dead, while the death it stands for is followed by a resurrection on the third day ([1 Corinthians 15:3–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2015:3-4&version=NIV)), so the cost the illustration makes vivid is not the cost the doctrine claims.
- Needs the standard atonement theories written up. Penal substitution, satisfaction ([[Anselm]]) and Christus Victor give different answers here and the argument currently treats them as one target.

# Limits
- The Bible itself says the animal sacrifices never worked ([Hebrews 10:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%2010:4&version=NIV)). That is a point for the argument on the ritual half, but it also means the tradition is not claiming the mechanism was transactional in the way the argument assumes.
- "A God able to forgive freely could simply do so" assumes forgiveness has no cost, which is the exact point in dispute. The argument asserts the conclusion rather than establishing it.
