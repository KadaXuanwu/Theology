# Description
God's requirement of a death before he forgives rests on his choice alone. He could have forgiven without one, and no reason given shows that a death does what a lesser costly act could not, unless it rests on a setting he chose. The Hebrew Bible records forgiveness with no offering named ([Numbers 14:19–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers%2014:19-20&version=NIV), [Psalm 32:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2032:5&version=NIV)). Its law lets someone who cannot afford two birds atone with flour, in the same words of atonement and forgiveness the blood offerings use ([Leviticus 5:11–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%205:11-13&version=NIV)).[^peres-2020] [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] holds that God could have freed humans from sin without [[Satisfaction|satisfaction]] and "would not have acted against justice".[^aquinas-st-iii-46]

Where philosophers attach conditions to forgiveness, they fall on the wrongdoer: repentance, confession and a commitment to moral change.[^thurow-sep] The Mishnah holds that the Day of Atonement does not atone for a wrong against another person until the wrongdoer has pacified him.[^mishnah-yoma] If God can forgive without a death, the death is a means he chose. [[Steven L. Porter|Steven Porter]], who defends [[The Penalty Falls on a Substitute|penal substitution]], sets the test such a means must pass.[^porter-2004] A voluntary sacrifice of life, he writes, "is not a morally valuable act unless there is some good purpose that can only or best be achieved by means of it".[^porter-2004] The settings are Israel's sacrificial sign and an order of justice that demands satisfaction, which Aquinas says "depends on the Divine will".[^aquinas-st-iii-46]

# Based On
- [Leviticus 17:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%2017:11&version=NIV) gives the law's reason for atonement by blood, inside a ban on eating blood ([Leviticus 17:10–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%2017:10-14&version=NIV)). [Hebrews 9:22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%209:22&version=NIV) states the rule, "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness", after saying that "the law requires that nearly everything be cleansed with blood". The same letter says the blood of bulls and goats cannot "take away sins" ([Hebrews 10:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%2010:4&version=NIV)).
- One reading takes the flour offering as a concession given "by the grace of YHWH at his discretion".[^kim-2013] On that reading blood stays the rule, and the exception rests on God's choice.[^kim-2013]
- That God could have saved humans otherwise is held by [[Augustine]] and Aquinas.[^augustine-trin][^aquinas-st-iii-46] [[John Owen]] called the contrary claim "false and erroneous" in 1648, and by 1653 argued for "the indispensable necessity of the satisfaction of Christ".[^owen-1648][^owen-1653][^trueman-1998] [[Richard Swinburne|Swinburne]] says God "could have" forgiven on "some minimum amount of repentance and apology".[^swinburne-2013] [[Robert Merrihew Adams]] agrees with [[John Hick|Hick]] that the Bible portrays a God who "can and does forgive freely".[^adams-1997]
- The Racovian Catechism of the Polish Socinians, first printed in 1605, pressed a charge of injustice in its revised Latin text.[^racovian] If one drop of Christ's blood would have sufficed, as its opponents said, a God who had him "so severely tormented" would be unjust.[^racovian] [[Hastings Rashdall]] contrasted [[Anselm]], who "insisted on an absolute, demonstrable necessity for Christ's death", with a later view.[^rashdall-1919] On it "the Father's arbitrary will and pleasure" was "the sole reason for His acceptance of the sacrifice".[^rashdall-1919] Porter, testing Swinburne's account, finds that whatever further goods the cross served as reparation and penance, "it will always seem that they could be achieved equally well without Christ's death".[^porter-2004]
- Israel's offerings shared their general form with its neighbours': grain offerings and the inspection of animals for blemishes were "widespread in the ancient Near East".[^ej-cult][^pardee-2002] At Ugarit animals make up 54 percent of recorded offerings, which were thought to feed the gods.[^pardee-2002]

# Countered By
- The strong counter is that the choice has reasons. Augustine holds that no other way was "more appropriate", since this one shows "at how great a price God rated us".[^augustine-trin] Aquinas holds the passion more fitting than deliverance "simply by God's good-will".[^aquinas-st-iii-46] It shows God's love, sets an example, merits grace, binds people more firmly against sin and lets a man overcome the devil and death.[^aquinas-st-iii-46] [[Joshua C. Thurow|Joshua Thurow]] argues that within Israel's sacrificial setting the blood of a pure victim could communicate that sin was washed away. Any means that was not a sacrifice, he holds, would have left doubt whether atonement was adequate, so the cross was "at least extremely fitting" if not necessary.[^thurow-2021] He suggests God took up animal sacrifice because it deals with the felt "stain" of guilt as well as with reparation.[^thurow-2021] God may then choose to accept these sacrifices, he writes, "because he loves humans and wants to give them a way to atone for their sins that they will understand".[^thurow-2021] His account assumes that it is "wise or at least permissible to voluntarily sacrifice oneself", and Jesus, he notes, "isn't forced".[^thurow-2021] Making "a symbol of oneself" is wise, he holds, when the context offers no "equally good ways".[^thurow-2021] On this view the argument shows only that the cross was not necessary, which Augustine and Aquinas already hold.[^augustine-trin][^aquinas-st-iii-46]
- Other reasons for a cost do not rest on Israel's rites. [[William Lane Craig|Craig]] supposes that a free pardon "would have taken on the appearance of 'cheap grace'" and won fewer people to salvation.[^craig-2019] [[Hugo Grotius]], as Craig reads him, held that God as "the supreme Ruler of the world, could have remitted our sins without punishing Christ".[^craig-2019] God's penal law, Grotius held, could be relaxed, but "could not be relaxed easily, or upon slight cause".[^grotius-1617] Porter writes that he is "prone to agree" that God "could forgive our sins without exacting such loss".[^porter-2004] Exacting it, he adds, "takes human sin seriously", and a voluntary substitute may bear it.[^porter-2004]
- A further counter denies that God required the death at all. Adams reads the cross as an episode in which God's representative "does not kill. Rather he is killed".[^adams-1997] His readings, he says, do not presuppose "that anything needed to be done to make God more willing or more able to forgive".[^adams-1997] Swinburne writes that "a perfect life need not end in a death by execution, but in so many human societies that may well happen".[^swinburne-2013] On a recent approach to sacrifice, the crucifixion is "an execution of a criminal".[^eberhart-2023] Rashdall held that the death "came to Him in the discharge of His Messianic task".[^rashdall-1919]
- [[God Can't Accept Sin]] and [[The Penalty Falls on a Substitute]] deny that God can simply waive sin, which must be dealt with or its penalty borne. Each says it does not explain why the remedy had to be a death. Anselm argues that to remit sin by compassion alone "is nothing else than not to punish", and that "satisfaction or punishment must needs follow every sin".[^anselm-cdh] For the payment he proposes a sinless man's voluntary death, the one gift such a man does not already owe God.[^anselm-cdh] In his *Dissertation on Divine Justice* Owen turned the charge into an argument.[^owen-1653] If God could forgive freely, no "sufficient reason" could be given for laying sins "so easily remissible" on his Son.[^owen-1653] Whether a penalty can pass to someone else is itself disputed.[^thurow-sep]

# Limits
- The argument stands or falls on whether any reason makes a death better than a lesser costly act without leaning on a setting God was free not to adopt. Thurow's own principle is that the fittingness of an atoning act "depends on context", and he calls his account of why God took up sacrifice at all "partial, somewhat speculative".[^thurow-2021] A published reason for a death that needs no such setting, and survives [[John McLeod Campbell|Campbell]]'s objection below, would settle it against the argument. So would a case that a reason resting on a chosen setting is reason enough.
- Some reasons need no such setting. Augustine's and [[John Calvin|Calvin]]'s, that so costly a gift shows how much God loves, faces an objection Campbell put in 1856.[^augustine-trin][^calvin-john] A man, he wrote, "will not actually lay down his life merely to shew his love", with nothing to make it necessary.[^campbell-1856] Aquinas's reason that the passion binds people against sin, and Craig's cheap grace, concern its effect on people; neither argues that a lesser costly act would have less effect.[^aquinas-st-iii-46][^craig-2019] Aquinas's fifth reason, that "as man deserved death, so a man by dying should vanquish death", rests on death as what sin deserves.[^aquinas-st-iii-46] [[Eleonore Stump|Stump]], as Craig reports her, ties Christ's incarnation and atonement to God's indwelling in human beings, but holds that union with God does not require the passion.[^craig-2019]
- Why blood and why a death are separate questions: a specialist reference entry holds that "killing is not essential to sacrifice", since grain could serve.[^gane-2022] An entry by a proponent of a more recent approach says Israelite sacrifice "does not work through killing".[^eberhart-2023] Thurow reaches a death from the sign of blood by holding that the life must be given for the blood to stand for life.[^thurow-2021]
- The pardons in Numbers 14 and 2 Samuel 12 keep a cost. The pardoned generation may not enter the land ([Numbers 14:20–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers%2014:20-23&version=NIV)), and David is told his child will die ([2 Samuel 12:13–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Samuel%2012:13-14&version=NIV)).
- The *Encyclopaedia Judaica* entry on the cult says the burnt offering "with its blood rites" was "rarely used" outside Syria and Palestine until late antiquity.[^ej-cult] A Mesopotamian rite gives a piglet, flesh for the sick man's flesh and blood for his blood, so that a disease demon's threat passes to it.[^schramm-2008] The text names no sin, and its editor reads it as no offering to the demons.[^schramm-2008] The Ugaritic sacrificial texts never mention the blood or fat of the victims, which their editor says may reflect their genre.[^pardee-2002] One study argues that Israel's blood rite shares its origin with a Hurro-Hittite rite for removing the effects of sin.[^feder-2011] Thurow holds that "expiatory animal sacrifices are widespread throughout ancient civilizations".[^thurow-2021] For their power to communicate outside Judaism he cites a 1981 study that could not be read for this node.[^thurow-2021][^hengel-1981]
- That Israel shared the form of its rites shows the setting was one among others; it does not show the setting lacks a reason. One Jewish philosophical reading holds that God allowed the sacrificial worship of the age to continue and "transferred" it to his own service.[^maimonides-guide]
- Hick's essay stating the argument, and the atonement appendix of *The Case Against Christianity*, could be read only in fragments.[^hick-1994][^martin-1991]
