# Description
There is no coherent reason a just and all-powerful God would require sacrifice, whether animal offerings under the old covenant or the death of Jesus, as a precondition for forgiveness. A God able to forgive freely could simply do so; demanding blood first makes the mechanism look like an arbitrary or invented ritual rather than a moral necessity.

# Based On
- [Leviticus 17:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%2017:11&version=NIV) atonement by blood.
- [Hebrews 9:22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%209:22&version=NIV) without shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.
- The comparative point strengthens it and needs sourcing: substitutionary blood offering is standard across ancient Near Eastern religion, which is what makes it look like inherited practice rather than moral necessity.

# Countered By
- [[God Can't Accept Sin]] the requirement follows from what God is rather than from a rule he chose, so it is not arbitrary even though it is not negotiable.
- [[The Covenant Changed with Christ]] the animal system is presented as provisional from the start and is ended, which answers the "why keep doing it" half but not the "why any of it" half.
- The king and the lashes, a parable rather than a case, and the popular form of the penal substitution answer. Gold keeps going missing from a king's treasury. He decrees ten lashes for whoever is taking it, the thefts go on, so he raises the count to twenty, then thirty, then forty, and forty is past what a person survives. The thief is caught and it is his daughter. He is just, so the lashes are owed. He loves her, so he cannot watch her take them. At the hour of punishment he steps down, takes her place at the post and dies under the count she earned. Nothing in it depends on whether it happened. It is there to make one thing visible: a ruler who cancels his own sentence has made his word worth nothing, and the only way left to spare the guilty is for someone else to absorb what they owed.
- What the parable carries. It answers the "why not simply forgive" half, by showing that forgiving and keeping the penalty in force are not both free, and it puts [[God Can't Accept Sin]] in a form anyone can follow in a minute. It does not reach the "why blood" half, which is what this argument is actually about. The king chooses ten and then forty, so on the question of whether a penalty is picked or fixed, the story sits on the argument's side rather than against it, and it says nothing about why the price should be a death.
- Needs the standard atonement theories written up. Penal substitution, satisfaction ([[Anselm]]) and Christus Victor give different answers here and the argument currently treats them as one target.

# Limits
- The Bible itself says the animal sacrifices never worked ([Hebrews 10:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%2010:4&version=NIV)). That is a point for the argument on the ritual half, but it also means the tradition is not claiming the mechanism was transactional in the way the argument assumes.
- "A God able to forgive freely could simply do so" assumes forgiveness has no cost, which is the exact point in dispute. The argument asserts the conclusion rather than establishing it.
