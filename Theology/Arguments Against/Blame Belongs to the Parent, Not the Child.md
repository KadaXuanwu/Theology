# Description
Where [[Augustine]], [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] and [[John Calvin|Calvin]] have God withhold the heart that loves him, their teaching binds his children to a father's claim and frees God from a father's duty. Scripture blames Eli because he "failed to restrain" his sons ([1 Samuel 3:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%203:11-14&version=NIV)), and it lays duties on fathers ([Ephesians 6:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians%206:1-4&version=NIV)). It also calls God Father to claim what his children owe him and to reproach their failure ([Malachi 1:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi%201:6&version=NIV), [Deuteronomy 32:4–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2032:4-6&version=NIV)). Augustine holds that to have faith and love "belongs to the grace of believers", and "in some the will is prepared by the Lord, in others it is not prepared".[^augustine-praed] Aquinas writes that God "does not give some the assistance, whereby they may avoid sin, which assistance were He to give, they would not sin".[^aquinas-st-i-ii-79] Calvin puts the fault wholly on those who lack it.[^calvin-institutes] If the title Father carries a father's office as well as his claim, God stands where Eli stood, with a power Eli lacked.

# Based On
- Eli rebuked his sons ([1 Samuel 2:22–25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%202:22-25&version=NIV)), and the charge is that he did not restrain them ([1 Samuel 3:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%203:13&version=NIV)). A critical commentary reads Ephesians 6:4 as marking that "the obligation was not all on the side of the children".[^abbott-1897]
- A critical commentary on Deuteronomy lists among the uses of Israel's sonship that Israel "is reproached accordingly with its unfilial disposition".[^driver-1896]
- The understanding mind is God's to give: "to this day the Lord has not given you a mind that understands" ([Deuteronomy 29:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2029:2-4&version=NIV)). The Second Council of Orange, a council of bishops held at Orange in 529, says "To love God is entirely God's gift".[^orange-529] Aquinas says those passed over "cannot acquire grace", by a "conditional" impossibility only. Each sin still "comes from the use of his free-will" and so "is rightly imputed to him as guilt". Mercy to some and justice to others is his reason for the scheme, but which ones God chooses "has no reason, except the divine will".[^aquinas-st-i-23]
- Calvin writes that the first man "falls by his own fault". Those passed over "cannot impute this corruption to God", the corruption of nature that is "the true source" of their condemnation.[^calvin-institutes]
- [[John Wesley]] charged that predestination has God condemn people "for continuing in sin, which, for want of that grace he will not give them, they cannot avoid".[^wesley-free-grace]
- Scripture runs the parent's standard up to God: "how much more will your Father in heaven give good gifts to those who ask him" ([Matthew 7:9–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207:7-11&version=NIV)). [[Nicholas Hadsell]] argues that if procreators incur duties by causing their children to exist, "then a fortiori God also incurs duties towards the offspring as well".[^hadsell-2025] [[Richard Swinburne|Swinburne]] holds God to the parents' limits, "Parents who totally neglect their children have no right to impose suffering on them", though they bind God "far less".[^swinburne-2025]

# Countered By
- The strong counter is Aquinas's. A failure to prevent wrong is charged only to someone who stops steering "while able and bound to steer", and on Aquinas's account God is not bound.[^aquinas-st-i-ii-79] He pays each thing its due, "yet He Himself is not the debtor, since He is not directed to other things, but rather other things to Him".[^aquinas-st-i-21] What people owe God rests on his "excellence" and the "benefits received", with parents as a lesser case.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-101] [[Henry Longueville Mansel|Henry Mansel]] held that man's rule of right and wrong implies "subjection to the authority of a superior". God's rule is one "implying no higher authority, and determined absolutely by His own nature".[^mansel-1866] Hadsell reports the modern objection that duties to creatures would "require seeing God as a moral agent" or are "incompatible with one of God's perfections", citing [[Brian Davies]] and [[Mark C. Murphy|Murphy]]. His own fallback has God's parental duties owed "to himself rather than to the child".[^hadsell-2025]
- Its cost is that it holds only where God stands outside every order of obligation. [[John Stuart Mill|Mill]] wrote in 1865 that to call God's goodness different in kind from man's is to say "that God may possibly not be good".[^mill-1865] Aquinas derives what God pays creatures from what is due to his own wisdom and goodness, and counts as due to a thing "what is ordered to it".[^aquinas-st-i-21] The question then returns as whether the withholding fits God's goodness. Calvin holds that it agrees with "an equity, indeed, unknown to us, but most certain".[^calvin-institutes]
- [Deuteronomy 32:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2032:6&version=NIV) and [Malachi 2:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi%202:10&version=NIV) join Father to Creator, and [Acts 17:28–29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%2017:22-29&version=NIV) calls all people God's offspring. Aquinas reads Deuteronomy 32:6 as making God Father of "the rational creature" by "the likeness of His image".[^aquinas-st-i-33] A thesis on the Old Testament's father language reads divine paternity in terms of election and covenant "rather than primarily in general creation".[^hiles-2001] Calvin takes Acts 17:28 of all people, God's gifts "attesting that he is our Father". He also holds that no one could call God Father with confidence "were we not gratuitously adopted".[^calvin-institutes] On the covenant reading, [Deuteronomy 30:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2030:1-6&version=NIV) promises the circumcised heart after Israel returns (30:2), so within the covenant the charge narrows to the time before the promise is kept. Outside it, the charge rests on whether creating brings a parent's duties.
- The prodigal son's father, often read as a picture of God, acts: he runs to the son and goes out to the elder ([Luke 15:20–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2015:11-32&version=NIV)).[^burke-2013]
- Wesley held that "no man sins because he has not grace, but because he does not use the grace which he hath".[^wesley-85] He thought grace working irresistibly in general would leave man "no longer a moral agent", though he allowed "exempt cases".[^wesley-63] The Catholic Council of Trent condemned in 1547 the view that all the called but the predestined "receive not grace, as being, by the divine power, predestined unto evil".[^trent-1547] Aquinas also writes that "God is ready to give grace to all", and that only those "who offer an obstacle" lack it. The fallen cannot avoid that obstacle "unless they be helped in advance by grace", and it is still "regarded as their fault".[^aquinas-scg-iii]
# Limits
- The argument stands or falls on one of two premises, and the covenant and creation readings of the title leave open which. One is whether calling God Father puts him under a father's duties or only gives his children a child's duties to him. The other is whether being the source of someone's being and goods, which grounds both debts, makes the source answerable for withholding what only he could give.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-101] No parent can give a child the heart itself, so on this argument's own reading no human case matches the second exactly. Hadsell, who carries a parent's duties over to God, holds that God "may not have" a parent's duty to prevent a child dying of cancer. He calls the content of divine duties "a bit of a mystery".[^hadsell-2025] One account of parental duty holds that a grandparent's "mere causal connection" to a grandchild is "not sufficient". In general, it holds, we "don't hold people responsible for just any situation to which they have causally contributed".[^weinberg-2018] Whether a maker is a remote or a proximate cause in that sense is not settled there. A published account of which parental duties pass to a maker who could prevent the refusal would settle it.
- No study found measures blame when a child does not love a parent. In stereotypes of adults who cut off a parent, the child is most often "childish/immature" or "ungrateful" and the parent "abusive", and the child "is not solely blamed".[^rittenour-2018] A Swiss city sought kinship support from a father toward welfare paid for his son, who had broken contact at about fifteen because he felt nothing for him. The Federal Supreme Court held that demanding the father should have been more present "geht zu weit", so it did not put the son's lost feeling on the father.[^bger-2002]
- Scripture faults Eli for failing to restrain his sons ([1 Samuel 3:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%203:11-14&version=NIV)) and tells fathers to train their children and not to embitter them ([Ephesians 6:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians%206:1-4&version=NIV), [Colossians 3:21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians%203:20-21&version=NIV)). No text found tells a father to make a child love him. The argument holds where failing to love God means a failure of covenant loyalty, the reading [[The Threat of Hell Compels the People Who Believe It]] carries.
- Eli is the strongest text and the least settled. The same account says his sons did not listen "for it was the Lord's will to put them to death" ([1 Samuel 2:25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%202:22-25&version=NIV)). Whether that will caused the refusal was not checked in a modern commentary read in full.
- On this node's reading, the sources put at most a share of the fault on the father, and the Swiss court none. A defender of lifelong parental duty holds that a grown child's "independent responsibility" can limit that duty but not remove it.[^weinberg-2018]
- The argument reaches only the line on which God withholds a grace that would end the refusal. Where no grace can end a free refusal, as in Wesley's general case, it becomes [[Demanding Belief Without the Proof]], which turns on [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]]. Where the inability is traced to [[Original sin|original sin]] alone, it becomes [[God Punishes Humans for a Flaw He Built into Them]]. Aquinas calls the withholding "deserved through a previous sin".[^aquinas-st-i-ii-79] His line stays in reach, since he rests the choice among the fallen on the divine will alone.[^aquinas-st-i-23]
- The commentaries on Deuteronomy and Ephesians date from 1896 and 1897, and were not checked against a modern commentary read in full.
