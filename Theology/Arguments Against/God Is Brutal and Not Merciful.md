# Description
Scripture has the LORD order whole peoples killed without pity, infants included, so either those orders are not his or he is not merciful toward those peoples. That second option needs one more premise, which [[Wesley Morriston|Morriston]] defends: that it is highly unlikely God had a morally sufficient reason for issuing them.[^morriston-2009] [Deuteronomy 20:16–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2020:16-17&version=NIV) orders Israel to "not leave alive anything that breathes" in the cities of the nations it is to inherit. In [1 Samuel 15:2–3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:2-3&version=NIV) Samuel brings the LORD's order to "put to death men and women, children and infants". Saul loses the kingship for carrying it out only in part ([1 Samuel 15:22–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:22-23&version=NIV)).

The parallel command in [Deuteronomy 7:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%207:2&version=NIV) and [7:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%207:16&version=NIV) forbids Israel favour and pity. Their Hebrew roots are the ones scripture uses of God's grace in [Exodus 34:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2034:6&version=NIV) and his pity for Nineveh in [Jonah 4:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jonah%204:11&version=NIV).[^bdb][^morales-2026] Scripture also says the LORD "has compassion on all he has made" ([Psalm 145:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%20145:9&version=NIV)). The reasons the texts give include keeping Israel from other gods ([Deuteronomy 20:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2020:18&version=NIV)) and punishing what Amalek did when Israel came out of Egypt ([1 Samuel 15:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:2&version=NIV)). Morriston argues that these are bad reasons for a perfectly good God, and that the second has infants killed for an offence centuries old.[^morriston-2012] A right to take life, he adds, is not a reason to use it.[^morriston-2012] [[John Calvin|Calvin]] rests the commands' justice on the one who gave them. Done by Joshua "of his own accord", he writes, the killing would carry "the guilt of detestable cruelty".[^calvin-joshua]

# Based On
- [Deuteronomy 7:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%207:2&version=NIV) puts the seven peoples of 7:1 under the [[Herem|herem]], and its last words read literally "show them no favor".[^morales-2026] The same root recurs in [Joshua 11:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%2011:20&version=NIV), where the LORD hardens the cities' hearts so that they are destroyed "without mercy".[^morales-2026] The Jewish Publication Society's translation glosses the people God pities in Jonah 4:11 as infants, "not held responsible for their actions".[^njps]
- Commentators read "anything that breathes" in Deuteronomy 20:16 as the human population. They argue from the word's usage and from [Joshua 11:14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%2011:14&version=NIV), where the people are killed and the cattle kept as plunder.[^driver-1895][^lilley-1993] The order in [1 Samuel 15:3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:3&version=NIV) names ʿôlēl and yônēq, "child" and "suckling" in the classic Hebrew lexicon, and the Septuagint has the same pair.[^bdb][^nets-2007] A critical commentary reads it as extermination "to the last man, woman, child, and animal".[^mccarter-1980]
- The Talmud gives Saul the protest that even if the adults had sinned, the children had not, and has a heavenly voice answer him with [Ecclesiastes 7:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ecclesiastes%207:16&version=NIV).[^yoma-22b]

# Countered By
- The strong counter is [[Scripture Can Be God's Word Without Reporting God's Speech]]: these commands record how Israel's writers portrayed God's will, not what God willed. They then count against [[The Bible Only Speaks Truth|inerrancy]] and not against God.[^morriston-2009] Morriston concludes that, failing better reasons for inerrancy, a Christian should deny that God gave them.[^morriston-2009] [[Randal Rauser]] argues that God did not command the killing.[^rauser-2009] [[Eric A. Seibert|Eric Seibert]] reads 1 Samuel 15 as political propaganda for David against Saul and concludes that "God never issued this genocidal decree".[^seibert-2009] He judges each portrayal of God by whether it fits the God Jesus reveals, and holds that not everything in scripture is authoritative.[^seibert-2009] [[Eleonore Stump]] argues that rejecting a revealed text on moral grounds would mean giving up the idea of revelation in these texts at all.[^stump-2011][^morriston-2012] Morriston answers that correction can run from moral intuition to text as well as the other way.[^morriston-2012] Biblical writers, he adds, already corrected one another, as [Ezekiel 18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel%2018&version=NIV) does with punishment across generations.[^morriston-2012]
- Where the commands are held to be God's, the question is whether he had a good reason.[^morriston-2012] Stump argues that the Amalek story could be literally true in a world like ours where Christianity's central claims also hold, for reasons beyond those the text gives.[^stump-2011][^morriston-2012] [[Richard Swinburne|Swinburne]] holds that God, as giver of life, had the right to order the Israelites to kill the Canaanites, to keep Israel from "lethal spiritual infection".[^swinburne-2011][^morriston-2012] [[Mark C. Murphy|Mark Murphy]] argues that God could not have wronged the ancient peoples, since he and they do not share an order of justice.[^murphy-2011][^morriston-2012] Morriston answers that the texts state their reasons and the reasons are bad. On his account, trusting a reported command to kill against its content would also cover a modern one.[^morriston-2012]
- In a sermon on 1 Samuel 15, Calvin raises the nursing children himself. He answers that God is judge of the whole world and that by [Exodus 20:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2020:5&version=NIV) he visits the fathers' sins on sons in whom enough vice always clings. His judgments, Calvin adds, are just even when their reason is hidden.[^calvin-1886] [[Paul Copan]] once argued that death would be a mercy to the children, since they would be ushered into God's presence.[^morriston-2009] Morriston calls such scenarios "intrinsically implausible".[^morriston-2009]
- [[Stephen N. Williams|Stephen Williams]] argues that the slaughter of the Canaanites is conceivable as God's command only if given "with an immeasurably heavy heart".[^williams-2012] The account in Joshua, he adds, gives no ground for such a command now.[^williams-2012]
- [[Conquest Texts Use Ancient War Hyperbole]] meets the victory reports in Joshua, and its own Limits name the commands as the hard case. Copan applies it to the commands as well. He reads "man and woman" as stock language that can stand even where no women or children were present, and holds that this could apply to the Amalekites.[^copan-2009] For the case where women and children were the explicit objects of the command, he keeps a fallback.[^copan-2009] Morriston had answered Copan's earlier version of the hyperbole case: a smaller death toll does not change what was ordered.[^morriston-2009]
- A 2026 study of the herem reads [Joshua 11:19–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%2011:19-20&version=NIV), where only Gibeon made peace, as showing that mercy stayed open to any city that asked for it.[^morales-2026] Some medieval Jewish commentators read the offer of peace in [Deuteronomy 20:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2020:10&version=NIV) as reaching the Canaanite cities too. The standard reading confines it to distant cities, as verse 15 says.[^morales-2026] Copan adds that carrying out the herem did not mean refusing mercy, as Rahab's case shows ([Joshua 6:25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%206:25&version=NIV)).[^copan-2009] None of this reaches 1 Samuel 15:3.
- Whether Deuteronomy's herem aimed at expelling the peoples or at killing them is disputed.[^stern-1991][^weinfeld-1991] A reading of the two as one policy still has the conquest cost many lives, presumably women and children among them.[^ford-2017]
- The texts that tie the judgment to conduct ([Leviticus 18:24–25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%2018:24-25&version=NIV), [Deuteronomy 9:4–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%209:4-5&version=NIV)) answer for the Canaanite adults, and [[God Delays Judgment to Allow Repentance]] answers why it came late. [Leviticus 18:28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%2018:28&version=NIV) warns Israel of the same expulsion. The Pontifical Biblical Commission, a Catholic body of biblical scholars, reads the law of extermination non-literally.[^pbc-2014] It still defends the law, on the ground of that conduct, as a duty of justice like executing a capital offender.[^pbc-2014] Morriston finds the evidence of incorrigible wickedness "nonexistent".[^morriston-2009] The Amalek order rests on an attack by the people's forebears generations before.[^morriston-2012][^seibert-2009]
- [[The Covenant Changed with Christ]] answers whether the commands still bind, not whether a merciful God gave them. [[Augustine]]'s answer, that one God commanded war in the prophets' time and forbade it to the apostles, keeps the commands as God's.[^augustine-faustum]

# Limits
- Whether these commands show anything about God depends on whether a command scripture gives in the LORD's name, through Moses or through Samuel, reports what God actually willed. History does not decide it. Two archaeologists who differ on much else agree that the conquest did not happen as Joshua tells it, and one calls this "accepted by all".[^finkelstein-mazar-2007] Scholarship has come to read Deuteronomy's ban as a utopian law, written centuries after the wars it describes.[^weinfeld-1993][^collins-2003] Morriston holds that a finding that the killings did not happen as the texts describe "does nothing to remove" the problem.[^morriston-2012] A teaching on scripture that addresses the point would settle it. The evangelical Chicago Statement on Biblical Hermeneutics of 1982 denies that any saying reported in scripture was invented, which would keep these commands as God's.[^icbi-1982] *Dei Verbum*, the Second Vatican Council's constitution on revelation, says the Old Testament contains "some things which are incomplete and temporary".[^dei-verbum] It does not say whether a command like this can be the writer's and not God's. The Pontifical Biblical Commission's study is, by its own preface, not an official statement of Church teaching.[^pbc-2014]
- The title's verdict, that God is brutal and not merciful, needs a further premise: that the command texts and not the mercy texts show God's character. None of the sources cited here supplies one. On the argument's own premise the mercy texts report the same God, so what follows is the dilemma, not the verdict.
- Mercy as leniency presupposes a deserved penalty, on the usual philosophical analysis.[^ransome-2008] In that sense the killing of blameless infants is a charge of injustice, not of a want of mercy. Morriston argues it as a charge against God's goodness.[^morriston-2012] The argument stands on mercy as pity, the sense of the words for pity and sparing in Deuteronomy 7:16 and 1 Samuel 15:3.[^bdb] One account extends mercy to anyone in need who has no claim to kind treatment. The study that reports it rejects it, holding that innocent sufferers have such a claim.[^ransome-2008]
- [Deuteronomy 20:16–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2020:16-17&version=NIV) names the towns of six peoples, and a study of the herem holds that its universal application "was never credibly demanded".[^stern-1991] For Deuteronomy the claim is every person in the towns taken. The order in 1 Samuel 15:3 carries it at full strength. One reading takes that order out of the late dating and ties its formula to an older ban of Saul's time.[^weinfeld-1993]
