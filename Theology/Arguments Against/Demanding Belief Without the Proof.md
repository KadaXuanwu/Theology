# Description
God holds people responsible for believing something he gives them no clear evidence for, and by scripture's own account the evidence is his to give. The signs, and the capacity to take them up, are his to give, and he gives them unevenly.

[[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] holds that everyone must explicitly believe that God exists, citing [Hebrews 11:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%2011:6&version=NIV).[^aquinas-de-veritate] The Council of Florence's 1442 decree for the Copts shuts everyone outside the Catholic Church out of eternal life.[^florence-1442] The Reformed Westminster Confession denies that anyone not professing the Christian religion can be saved in any other way, however diligently they live by the light of nature. It saves elect persons who are incapable of being outwardly called.[^westminster-confession]

Scripture gives signs so that people will believe ([Exodus 4:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%204:5&version=NIV)), and says the signs do not settle it. [Deuteronomy 29:2–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2029:2-4&version=NIV) recalls what Israel saw and says the Lord has not given them a mind that understands. Aquinas concludes that of those who see the same miracle some believe and some do not, so belief needs an inner cause from God.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-6]

Thomas and Paul are shown what others are not, and the risen Jesus is seen by chosen witnesses, not by all the people ([Acts 10:40–41](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%2010:40-41&version=NIV)). At [Matthew 11:21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2011:21&version=NIV) Jesus says Tyre and Sidon would have repented had they seen his miracles, and [[Augustine]] takes that at its word.[^augustine-de-dono] Some who lack belief never refused it, the [[Non-resistant non-belief|non-resistant non-belief]] that [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]] sets out. The argument concludes that these traditions make the lack decide salvation while its deciding cause lies with God. If condemning someone for a lack he did nothing to bring about is unjust, the argument holds that they act unjustly.

# Based On
- [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]] carries the existence claim, that there are people who do not resist God and still do not believe. [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] closes off the answer that they were resisting anyway.
- [Numbers 14:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers%2014:11&version=NIV) signs performed, belief in God not produced, and God states it as a complaint rather than as the design.
- [1 Kings 18:38–39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Kings%2018:38-39&version=NIV) fire falls in front of a crowd, and the people respond.
- [John 20:27–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2020:27-28&version=NIV) Thomas is offered what he asked for, and no touching is narrated. [[Raymond E. Brown|Brown]] writes at p. 1046 that Thomas comes to belief without probing the wounds, and traces the touching to later writers.[^brown-1970]
- Paul has seen Jesus ([1 Corinthians 9:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%209:1&version=NIV)), and Christ appeared to him with the verb used of the other appearances ([1 Corinthians 15:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2015:8&version=NIV)).[^gieniusz-2019] He was persecuting the church when God revealed his Son in him ([Galatians 1:13–16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%201:13-16&version=NIV)), so he is hostile when the evidence arrives.
- [John 12:37–40](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2012:37-40&version=NIV) has people not believing after many signs, and at 12:39 "they could not believe". Brown reads it at p. 485 against Deuteronomy 29:2–4.[^brown-1966]
- Aquinas raises the complaint against himself: hearing is not in anyone's power unless someone speaks.[^aquinas-de-veritate] [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] asks whether a God who let doubt stand for millennia, threatening dreadful consequences, can be good.[^nietzsche-daybreak]
- [[Belief Isn't Directly Chosen]] carries the premise the argument needs, and the limit on it. A defender of the freedom reply can grant that belief is not chosen and still hold that people control which evidence they seek out.
- [[Daniel Speak]] argues from [[Ought implies can|ought implies can]] that God will not condemn those who became unbelievers without negligence.[^speak-2007] [[Robert Merrihew Adams|Robert Adams]] holds that blame can fit involuntary beliefs but only voluntary acts are rightly punished, and leaves God open.[^adams-1985]
- [[Unbelief Clusters by Country, Not by Motive]] carries the demographic version of the argument, [[Stephen Maitzen]]'s, and the replies to it.

# Countered By
- The strong counter is that guilt follows the light a person was given. Aquinas and the Second Vatican Council's *Lumen Gentium* (1964) hold that the faultless are not condemned, and Robert Adams that unbelief is no sin where God has not spoken. Aquinas writes that God would give what is needed for believing to someone raised in the woods who follows natural reason, if nothing on his side impedes.[^aquinas-de-veritate] He counts unbelief in those who never heard as a penalty, not a sin. He holds that they are damned for other sins that cannot be forgiven without faith.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-10] *Lumen Gentium* 16 offers salvation to the faultlessly ignorant who sincerely seek God, moved by grace, as [[Invincible Ignorance Excuses Unbelief]] sets out.[^lumen-gentium] Adams writes at p. 9 that merely not believing God exists is not the sin of unbelief if God never spoke to you.[^adams-1984] [John 15:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2015:24&version=NIV) ties guilt to the works seen. [James 2:19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=James%202:19&version=NIV) has the demons believe and shudder. Read as a claim about what God is after, it makes allegiance rather than agreement the goal, and more proof would not deliver it. [[Max Baker-Hytch]] cites James 2:19 at pp. 658–659 for the narrower point that belief that God exists is not sufficient for saving faith. Saving faith also takes trust, love and dispositions to act.[^baker-hytch-2025] James is contrasting faith with works, not discussing evidence for God.
- [[Blaise Pascal]] grants that God could have revealed himself so plainly that the most hardened could not doubt. He holds it right to refuse some, because of their obduracy, what God grants others from a compassion not due to them. He has God give signs visible to those who seek him (Brunschvicg 430).[^pascal-1670]
- [[Paul K. Moser]] holds that one can be responsible, through one's adopted stance, for lacking evidence that is available (p. 50).[^moser-2013] Paul's appearance came to a persecutor, which this has to fit.
- [[Michael J. Murray]]'s freedom reply meets the version about evidence given to everyone at once. He writes that he is not claiming freely willed acts are metaphysically impossible under a significant threat. He claims only that such a threat interferes with a person exercising morally significant freedom in a fully robust way. What carries his argument is evidence together with the threat of temporal and eternal punishment, and hiddenness works by keeping that threat epistemically ambiguous. He assumes, after raising it as an objection to himself, that God makes each person as aware of him as freedom allows. Those who still fall short do so, on his account, through their own blindness or through God's hardening as punishment for sin. He does not discuss the cases of Thomas, Paul or Elijah.[^murray-1993] [[Richard Swinburne|Swinburne]]'s version runs on desire rather than threat (pp. 268–270).[^swinburne-2004] For [[Tyler Paytas]], knowing God would stop obedience feeling like a sacrifice of self interest.[^paytas-2017]
- In a dialogue in [[Peter van Inwagen]]'s chapter, the theist answers that even stars rearranged to spell "I am who am" could be a lesser being's work (pp. 27–29). He leaves open whether any sign could make belief in God reasonable (p. 29).[^vaninwagen-2002] [[Travis Dumsday|Dumsday]] adds this at p. 37 n. 8. Someone struck by the problem of evil could read the stars rearranged into a message as evidence against that being's goodness. Whatever could do it could have stopped the genocides.[^dumsday-2015]
- Two findings from other fields bear on the premise that knowing God would swamp the will. Certainty of being caught deters.[^nagin-2017] In two small studies believers and nonbelievers cheated equally, though those who saw God as punishing cheated less.[^shariff-2011]
- [Matthew 28:17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2028:17&version=NIV) puts worship and doubt in one verse. [[Keith H. Reeves|Reeves]] argues the eleven are the subject throughout. He notes the verb for doubt occurs only twice in the New Testament, here and at [Matthew 14:31](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2014:31&version=NIV), where Jesus glosses it as little faith.[^reeves-1998] The subject is contested; on either reading doubt and sight sit in one sentence.
- Scripture has its own policy on demanded proof. [Mark 8:11–12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208:11-12&version=NIV) refuses a sign to Pharisees asking "to test him", and [Matthew 12:39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2012:39&version=NIV) excepts the sign of Jonah. At [Luke 16:31](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2016:31&version=NIV) the character Abraham, inside a parable, says people who will not hear Moses and the prophets will not be persuaded if someone rises from the dead.
- God chose the resurrection's witnesses and commanded them to preach ([Acts 10:41–42](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%2010:41-42&version=NIV)), and Paul's revelation came so that he might preach among the Gentiles ([Galatians 1:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%201:16&version=NIV)). What they were shown may be an exception by design.
- The replies to the demographic version, from testimony and from divine [[Middle knowledge|middle knowledge]], are set out in [[Unbelief Clusters by Country, Not by Motive]].
- [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]] makes the sequence deliberate and [[Religious Experience Is Evidence for God]] puts the same reply as method. [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] denies the premise. [[Hell Is Self-Chosen Separation]] answers the penalty half rather than the evidence half.
- [[Faith Can Be Rational Before the Evidence Is In]] answers the reading that belief must follow evidence.

# Limits
- The argument turns on one premise: that God withholds the inward gift needed to believe, or the grace needed to seek him, from some who never refused it. Aquinas and *Lumen Gentium* 16 deny it, holding that God does not fail anyone who does what he can. Matthew 11:21, read as Augustine reads it, says the miracles went past people who would have repented. [[William Lane Craig]] calls the passage "probably religious hyperbole".[^craig-1995] It would be settled by a case of someone who sought God and was never given what belief needs. The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* doubts anyone can discern such a case.[^sep-hiddenness]
- Pius IX restated in 1854 and 1863 that no one is saved outside the Catholic Church.[^bullivant-2011][^quanto-1863] [[Stephen Bullivant|Bullivant]] dates to 1854 the entry into magisterial teaching on salvation of the older principle that [[Invincible ignorance|invincible ignorance]] of the true religion carries no fault.[^bullivant-2011]
- This is not [[J. L. Schellenberg|Schellenberg]]'s current argument, which replaced culpability with resistance and argues from a perfectly loving God's openness to relationship.[^sep-hiddenness] It sits nearer the argument from non-belief and the problem Craig sets out, that some persons do not receive Christ and are damned.[^drange-1993][^craig-1989]
- The First Vatican Council's *Dei Filius* (1870) denies the first clause on its outward reading: miracles make revelation credible, though argument does not necessarily produce assent.[^dei-filius] Signs in scripture's narratives do not always produce lasting belief. After Carmel, Ahaziah serves Baal ([1 Kings 22:53](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Kings%2022:53&version=NIV)), and Jehu later fills a temple of Baal with its worshippers before killing them ([2 Kings 10:18–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%2010:18-28&version=NIV)). The calf follows Sinai at [Exodus 32:1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2032:1-4&version=NIV).
- Reported direct revelation from God is not rare. In Pew's survey across 19 sub-Saharan African countries, 30% or more said they had received a direct revelation from God in nine of them.[^pew-2010-africa] The survey does not separate that from the public, checkable event the biblical cases describe. So the claim that Carmel-type evidence is not on offer today has to be argued rather than cited.
- Read as history rather than as scripture's own account, it leans on the Gospels and Acts, which is awkward for someone who doubts the evidence for God generally. Paul's hostility rests on his own letters, and Maitzen's version carries no such load.
- The reply is contested, not abandoned. Dumsday writes at pp. 423–424 that the moral freedom response is a prominent line and that Schellenberg has pursued several routes of reply, and declines to adjudicate.[^dumsday-2010] The encyclopedia doubts that any single explanation of non-resistant non-belief is total, though several partial ones might make one.[^sep-hiddenness]
