# Description
God holds people responsible for believing something he gives them no clear evidence for, and by scripture's own account the evidence is his to give. Thomas asks to see and touch the wounds and Jesus offers exactly that ([John 20:27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2020:27&version=NIV)). Paul gets an appearance while he is, in Luke's phrase, still breathing out murderous threats against the church ([Acts 9:1–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%209:1-6&version=NIV)). Elijah gets fire in front of a crowd ([1 Kings 18:38–39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Kings%2018:38-39&version=NIV)). At [Numbers 14:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers%2014:11&version=NIV) God asks how long the people will refuse to believe in him despite all the signs he has performed among them, which takes for granted that signs were given so that they would.

[[Michael J. Murray]] writes in the 1993 paper that he is not claiming freely willed acts are metaphysically impossible under a significant threat, only that such a threat interferes with a person exercising morally significant freedom in a fully robust way.[^murray-1993] What carries his argument is evidence together with the standing threat of eternal punishment, and hiddenness works by keeping that threat epistemically ambiguous. His 2002 restatement widens threats into incentives generally and grounds the hiddenness in what would preclude soul making.[^murray-2002] [[Richard Swinburne]]'s version, as [[Travis Dumsday|Dumsday]] states it at p. 427, is that a strong and constant awareness of God would so hamper the desire to do evil that real moral choice is severely impaired.[^dumsday-2010] [[John Hick]]'s epistemic distance describes humanity's general situation rather than what one person may be shown, and [[J. L. Schellenberg]] reports at pp. 97–99 that Hick never states or defends the claim that people can choose what to believe, though he often writes as though he does.[^schellenberg-1993]

What Thomas and Paul reach is the narrower point that assurance about God does not swamp contrary desire. Schellenberg makes it against Swinburne at pp. 129–130 without leaving the text: Paul in Romans 7 was as convinced on experiential grounds of God's existence and goodness as anyone, and by his own admission went on doing what he did not want to do.[^schellenberg-1993]

The harder version of the complaint drops the biblical cases. [[Stephen Maitzen]] argues that theistic belief is spread unevenly enough across the world that no theistic account of individual unbelief absorbs the pattern, because the motivations those accounts invoke do not cluster by country.[^maitzen-2006] The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* puts his point against the freedom reply directly: it is not as though the Thai are twenty times likelier than the Saudis, on coming to believe God exists, to be coerced into love, trust and obedience.[^maitzen-2006][^sep-hiddenness] Pew's 2020 estimates put 95% of the world's Hindus in India and 67% of the religiously unaffiliated in China.[^pew-2025-landscape]

The complaint's second half, that the penalty falls on everyone regardless, is put too widely. [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] holds that anyone following natural reason towards good would be given what salvation needs, by inward inspiration or by a preacher sent, and *Lumen Gentium* 16 says someone ignorant of the gospel through no fault of his own can be saved.[^aquinas-de-veritate][^lumen-gentium]

# Based On
- The premise it works from: God wants a response that is freely given, which is the standing reason for staying hidden. See [[Coerced Love Isn't Love]].
- [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]] carries the existence claim, that there are people who do not resist God and still do not believe. [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] closes off the answer that they were resisting anyway.
- [Numbers 14:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers%2014:11&version=NIV) signs performed, belief in God not produced, and God states it as a complaint rather than as the design. This is the verse that says what Exodus 19:9 is usually quoted for, where the object of the trusting is Moses.
- [1 Kings 18:38–39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Kings%2018:38-39&version=NIV) fire falls in front of a crowd, and the people respond.
- [John 20:27–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2020:27-28&version=NIV) Thomas is offered what he asked for, and no touching is narrated. [[Raymond E. Brown|Brown]] writes at p. 1046 that Thomas comes to belief without probing the wounds, and traces the touching to later writers.[^brown-1970]
- [Acts 9:1–2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%209:1-2&version=NIV) has Paul breathing out murderous threats, so he is hostile when the evidence arrives. Acts 9:3–6 narrates a light and a voice; the seeing language is Luke's own at 9:17 and 26:16 and Paul's at [1 Corinthians 15:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2015:8&version=NIV), where the verb is the one used of the other resurrection appearances.
- [Matthew 28:17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2028:17&version=NIV) worship and doubt in one verse. [[Keith H. Reeves|Reeves]] argues the eleven are the subject throughout, and notes the verb for doubt occurs only twice in the New Testament, here and at Matthew 14:31 where Jesus glosses it as little faith.[^reeves-1998] The subject is contested; on either reading doubt and sight sit in one sentence.
- [[Belief Isn't Directly Chosen]] carries the premise the argument needs, and the limit on it: a defender of the freedom reply can grant that belief is not chosen and still hold that people control which evidence they seek out.
- Source checks still open on this node are tracked in the repo under `Verification/`.

# Countered By
- The strong counter is Murray's own answer to the biblical cases, which the counterexamples do not get past. He puts individual revelation on the table and caps it by the same constraint: God may make each person as aware of him as is possible for that person without the revelation removing the possibility of morally significant free choice.[^murray-1993] On that account a private appearance is limited by what it would do to the recipient, so Thomas and Paul are cases the argument covers rather than cases it missed.
- [[Peter van Inwagen]] denies that a public sign could do the work at all. In his dialogue the theist says no sign would have to be the work of a necessary, omnipresent, omnipotent being, since even the stars rearranged could be a lesser being's doing; the atheist replies that theists have then imagined a being no one could rationally believe in.[^vaninwagen-2002] [[Travis Dumsday|Dumsday]] sharpens the first half: someone struck by the problem of evil could read the stars rearranged into a message as evidence against that being's goodness, since whatever could do it could have stopped the genocides.[^dumsday-2015]
- [[Tyler Paytas]] grants the premise and moves the reply off it. He concedes a person could still transgress while believing the consequences will be dire, then relocates the loss: under knowledge of God, obedience stops being experienced as a sacrifice of self interest, so virtue cannot be formed.[^paytas-2017] He raises a narrower version of the distribution question himself, and offers then sets aside the answer that revelation given to some would become common knowledge and cost the rest that experience.[^paytas-2017]
- Scripture has its own policy on demanded proof. [Mark 8:11–12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208:11-12&version=NIV) refuses a sign from heaven flat. At [Luke 16:31](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2016:31&version=NIV) the character Abraham, inside a parable, says people who will not hear Moses and the prophets will not be persuaded if someone rises from the dead.
- [John 20:29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2020:29&version=NIV) is weaker than its usual use suggests. Brown reads it at p. 1049 as a contrast between two kinds of blessedness rather than a demotion of Thomas, though he grants those who did not see are in a certain way nobler.[^brown-1970] [[Craig S. Keener|Keener]] takes it further at 2:1215, that faith without sight is the greater and that Thomas's faith was insufficient.[^keener-2003]
- [James 2:19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=James%202:19&version=NIV) has the demons believe and shudder, so what God is after is allegiance rather than agreement and more proof would not deliver it. The verse is being extended: James is contrasting faith with works, not discussing evidence.
- On distribution, [[Max Baker-Hytch]] argues that people rely on testimony for much of what they know, so belief clusters geographically either way.[^baker-hytch-2016] [[Andrew Blanton]] answers him in print.[^blanton-2025] [[Jason Marsh]] replies to Maitzen from divine middle knowledge, holding that those deprived of the chance to believe here would have freely rejected God whatever God had created, and that the geography is where God places them to secure a later conversion.[^marsh-2008]
- The biblical cases all have a part in the story rather than being ordinary believers, so they may be exceptions by design.
- [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]] makes the sequence deliberate and [[Personal Relationship with God]] puts the same reply as method. [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] denies the premise. [[Hell as Self-Chosen Separation]] answers the penalty half rather than the evidence half.

# Limits
- The one time a whole nation got the public demonstration it did not go the way this needs. At [Exodus 20:18–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2020:18-21&version=NIV) the people who saw the mountain ask Moses to speak instead so they will not have to hear God, and stay at a distance. The calf follows in the same book at [Exodus 32:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2032:1&version=NIV).
- Elijah's fire did not end Baal worship. [2 Kings 3:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%203:2&version=NIV) has Joram removing a sacred stone of Baal his father put up, and [2 Kings 10:18–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%2010:18-28&version=NIV) has Jehu filling a temple of Baal with its worshippers before killing them.
- [John 12:37](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2012:37&version=NIV) cuts both ways. After so many signs they still would not believe, escalating at 12:39 to "they could not believe". Brown reads it at p. 485 against [Deuteronomy 29:2–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2029:2-4&version=NIV), where the Lord is said not to have given them a mind that understands.[^brown-1966] Signs failing to produce belief cuts against the argument; the capacity being withheld is the argument's own complaint in a stronger form.
- It leans on Acts and John as records of what happened, which is awkward for someone who doubts the evidence for God generally. Maitzen's version carries no such load.
- Reported private revelation is not rare. In Pew's survey across 19 sub-Saharan African countries, 30% or more said they had received a direct revelation from God in nine of them.[^pew-2010-africa] No survey separates that from the public, checkable event the biblical cases describe, so the claim that Carmel-type evidence is not on offer today has to be argued rather than cited.
- The demographic figures record where religious groups sit, not how individuals came by their religion.
- The reply is contested, not abandoned. Dumsday writes at pp. 423–424 that the moral freedom response is a prominent line and that Schellenberg has pursued several routes of reply, and declines to adjudicate.[^dumsday-2010] The encyclopedia holds it doubtful that any single theistically friendly explanation of non-resistant non-belief is a total one.[^sep-hiddenness]
