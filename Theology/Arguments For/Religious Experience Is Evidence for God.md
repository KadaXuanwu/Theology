# Description
Reports of experiencing God raise the probability that God exists, because how things seem is good grounds for how they are unless there is a special reason for doubt.[^swinburne-2004][^webb-2022] [[Richard Swinburne]] calls this the [[Principle of credulity|principle of credulity]]: if it seems to someone that something is present, then, absent special considerations, it probably is.[^swinburne-2004][^swinburne-2018][^webb-2022] For those who lack the experience, his [[Principle of testimony|principle of testimony]] applies: absent special considerations, what others report experiencing is probably as they report it.[^swinburne-2004][^gasser-2022] A substantial minority in Britain report an awareness of the presence of God, and reports of religious experience there are not confined to churchgoers.[^orb-2000][^hay-socha-2005] Muslims in Turkey report God's presence too.[^yaran-2015]

Swinburne goes through the considerations that defeat ordinary perceptual claims and argues that they do not hold, or not always, for experiences of God.[^webb-2022] On his account such reports are to be trusted as long as other grounds do not make God's existence highly improbable.[^gasser-2022] Unless the other evidence makes theism very improbable, Swinburne holds, the experiences make it probable overall.[^swinburne-2004][^gasser-2022] In 2018 he held that the principle applies to belief in God, "although perhaps to a reduced degree".[^swinburne-2018] The experience is "a different kind of apparent perception", he wrote, but not "a very different kind".[^swinburne-2018]

[[William P. Alston|William Alston]] holds that experience of God can make a person prima facie justified in believing that God exists.[^alston-1994-response] He denies that this is an argument from the experiences to God's existence (1994, p. 175).[^alston-1994-response] No basic way of forming beliefs, sense perception included, can be shown reliable without relying on its own outputs, he argues.[^alston-1986-perceiving][^alston-1986-circularity] So to demand an outside check of experience of God alone is "a double standard".[^alston-1986-perceiving][^alston-1994-precis] A belief formed from experience of God is justified unless there are sufficient reasons against it, "precisely the status of individual perceptual beliefs" (1986, p. 657).[^alston-1986-perceiving] By his own account an argument of this circular kind "cannot be used to rationally produce conviction" in someone who does not already accept its conclusion.[^alston-1986-circularity]

# Based On
- Swinburne's main statement is chapter 13 of *The Existence of God*.[^swinburne-2004] In 2018 he argued that, with no defeater, an experience of God of a given strength makes "there is a God" at least that probable.[^swinburne-2018] His reasons are that the claim is "a very simple claim", and that there is "a significant probability" that God would make his presence known to some people.[^swinburne-2018]
- In *Perceiving God* (1991) Alston argued it is rational to engage in any socially established practice of forming beliefs not shown unreliable, and that the Christian one qualifies.[^alston-1991][^alston-1994-precis] In 1995 he was "disposed to ditch" that appeal to practical rationality (pp. 71–72).[^alston-1995] In its place he put the simpler claim that there is no appeal beyond the practices we find ourselves firmly committed to.[^alston-1995] He also wrote that the justification experience of God gives is "less than that conferred on sense perceptual beliefs by sense experience" (p. 79).[^alston-1995]
- [Psalm 34:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2034:8&version=NIV) says "Taste and see that the Lord is good". The International Critical Commentary glosses this "make a trial, test by experience".[^briggs-1906] It reads the psalm as temple thanksgiving, in which afflicted worshippers "are reminded of their own experience" of rescue in verses 4–5.[^briggs-1906] [Jeremiah 29:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah%2029:13&version=NIV) promises the exiles in Babylon that they will find God when they seek him with all their heart, a promise tied to their return.[^driver-1906] [John 7:17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%207:17&version=NIV) promises that whoever chooses to do God's will "will find out whether my teaching comes from God". One commentary calls this an "inward criterion" and the principle "universal in its application".[^westcott-1908] None of the three asks whether God exists: each speaks to people who already believe in him.[^briggs-1906][^driver-1906][^westcott-1908] Alston counts as the Christian practice's "self-support" that the spiritual development it promises "can be seen (from within) to be fulfilled significantly often" (1988, p. 438).[^alston-1988]

# Countered By
- **Religious diversity is the strong counter**, held as a claim in [[Experiential Confirmation Doesn't Distinguish Religions]]. Some mystics' claims across traditions contradict each other on "the basic nature of what is allegedly experienced", a personal God or a nonpersonal [[Brahman]].[^jones-gellman-2026] [Moroni 10:4–5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/moro/10?lang=eng) promises an answer "by the power of the Holy Ghost" to those who ask God whether the Book of Mormon is true. They must ask "with a sincere heart, with real intent, having faith in Christ". [[Evan Fales]] finds it "extraordinarily hard to explain" why God would appear "through the figure of Jesus to a Christian, as Allah to a Muslim" (p. 103).[^fales-2020] [[J. L. Schellenberg]] argues that a Christian's belief entails that her rivals' beliefs are false, and that without independent justification for that, her own justification is lost.[^schellenberg-1994][^alston-1994-response] Alston puts the objection this way: without "some external reason" to think the Christian practice more reliable than the others, picking it looks "arbitrary" (1988, p. 442).[^alston-1988] On his account diversity diminishes the practice's justification, though not to the point of irrationality (p. 446).[^alston-1988] In 1994 he called diversity the difficulty "with my answer to which I am least satisfied" (p. 178).[^alston-1994-response]
- [[William L. Rowe]] argued that the principle of credulity holds only where we know how to find positive reasons for thinking an experience delusive (p. 91).[^rowe-1982] That fails for experience of God, he argued, because God "may choose to reveal himself to A but not reveal himself to B under similar conditions" (p. 90).[^rowe-1982] Fales argues that perceptual claims earn their weight through cross-checking.[^fales-2020] The checks Christian mystics typically use are, he argues, "either epistemically irrelevant or question-begging, absent quite strong auxiliary assumptions" (p. 104).[^fales-2020]
- Across five countries, [[Absorption|absorption]] and a "porous" model of mind each predicted who reported "vivid sensory experiences of what they took to be gods and spirits".[^luhrmann-2021] A porous model treats the boundary between mind and world as permeable.[^luhrmann-2021] In 28 congregants at one evangelical church, [[Tanya Luhrmann]] and two colleagues found that absorption predicted unusual sensory experience of God.[^luhrmann-2010] With absorption held constant, time in prayer tracked how vividly God was felt as a person.[^luhrmann-2010] In a randomised trial, a month of imagination-based prayer was followed by more reports of intense spiritual experience than a month of lectures on the Gospels.[^luhrmann-morgain-2012] [[Neil Van Leeuwen]] and [[Michiel van Elk]] propose that general belief in supernatural agents comes first, mostly from culture, and leads people to seek situations that trigger such experiences.[^vanleeuwen-vanelk-2019] For the [[Agency detection|agency detection]] account, held as a claim in [[Humans Are Primed to See Agents and Purpose]], [[Lari Launonen]] finds the evidence "scarce".[^launonen-2021] In the study he cites, people shown ambiguous cues of human agency leaned toward assuming none, and their supernatural beliefs did not affect how readily they detected agents.[^launonen-2021] Launonen holds that "explaining is not debunking", and also argues that cognitive science undercuts most experiences of hearing God speak.[^launonen-2021][^launonen-2024]
- [[Demanding Belief Without the Proof]] argues that the evidence scripture reports some people receiving is given unevenly, while all are held responsible for belief.

# Limits
- The argument stands or falls on whether the conflict between traditions over what is met, a personal God or a nonpersonal Brahman, defeats reports of God.[^jones-gellman-2026] The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on mysticism holds that this divergence "still damages" the case for accepting mystical claims under Swinburne's principle of credulity.[^jones-gellman-2026] Swinburne holds that people describe their experiences in their own religion's terms, and that conflict between descriptions challenges only a "particular detailed claim".[^swinburne-2004][^yaran-2007] Babylonian and Greek astronomers described the heavens in conflicting ways, he notes, which did not show there was nothing there.[^swinburne-2004][^yaran-2007] [[Caroline Franks Davis]] argued that the record supports a "broad theism", the ultimate met as a presence "with whom individuals can have a personal relationship" (p. 191).[^franks-davis-1989][^jones-gellman-2026] The same encyclopedia entry holds that she "has to twist the Advaitins' and Buddhists' accounts" to get there.[^jones-gellman-2026] If a shared experience of a personal God survives the conflict, the reports are modest evidence that God exists. If only a sense of a presence or power survives, they point beyond the self but not clearly to God. A study across traditions of what people report, controlling for upbringing, would settle which is right.
- Evidence for God is not yet evidence for Christianity, and the reports do not single out the Christian God. The poll item naming God drew 38% in Britain in 2000, while over 76% said yes to at least one item about a presence or power.[^orb-2000][^hay-socha-2005] Inside the Christian practice, scripture checks the experience: in Alston's account the practice's checks "will be heavily based on the canonical scriptures of a tradition" (1988, p. 438).[^alston-1988] Classical mystics in general did not take their experiences "as verifying the doctrines of their basic scriptures but vice versa".[^jones-gellman-2026] Alston writes that his argument "provides no grounds for signing on with that practice rather than with some alternative form" not disqualified by his criteria (1994, p. 177).[^alston-1994-response]
- The Christian tradition states tests an experience can fail, such as bad fruits or conflict with scripture, but they work from inside it.[^jones-gellman-2026][^losin-1987] Psalm 34:8, Jeremiah 29:13 and John 7:17 state no such test. A 1987 defence of the principle holds that such tests assume orthodox theology is true and are "of much less use on an intertraditional basis" (p. 67).[^losin-1987] Alston holds that a single report "cannot be decisively disconfirmed by the experience of others" (1986, p. 661).[^alston-1986-perceiving] His failure conditions for a whole practice are "a massive and persistent inconsistency in its output" and conflict with more firmly established practices (1988, p. 437).[^alston-1988] He draws no precise line for how much is too much.[^alston-1994-precis] [[No Outcome Counts Against It]] and [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]] take up a step that cannot come back negative.
