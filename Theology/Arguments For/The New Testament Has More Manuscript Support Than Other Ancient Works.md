# Description
The New Testament survives in more copies, and in copies closer to the date of writing, than any other text from Greek or Roman antiquity. Historians reconstruct the reign of Tiberius or the Gallic campaigns from a handful of manuscripts written a thousand years after the events, and do so without much complaint. On the ordinary standards of ancient history the New Testament is the best attested document of its world.

# Based On

- [[Papyrus P52]] for the earliest surviving fragment.
- [[Dead Sea Scrolls]] and [[Great Isaiah Scroll]] for the Old Testament half of the same point.
- [[Tacitus]] and [[Josephus]], whose own works survive in far fewer copies and who are nevertheless used as evidence in [[Jesus Existed]].

# Countered By
Needs nodes. The counter is that the number of copies bears on what the text said, not on whether it is true, and that the comparison is picked to flatter: Roman history rests on inscriptions, coins and public documents as well as manuscripts, and the events in the Gospels have no such record. The complaint about double standards also has an answer: the New Testament is questioned harder because it asks for more than Caesar does.

# Limits
- The crux: manuscript attestation establishes the wording, and the argument is often used as though it established the content. What would settle the larger point is evidence outside the text, which this is not.
- Most of the copies are medieval, produced by an institution that had every reason to keep copying. Counting them measures the survival of the church as much as the survival of the text.
- More copies also means more variant readings. The same corpus supports both the apologetic figure and Bart Ehrman's several hundred thousand variants.
- It says nothing about the gap between the events and the first writing, which is where most of the historical argument actually sits.
