# Description
Belief in God can be rational without an argument behind it, the same way belief in other minds or in the reality of the past is rational without one. [[John Calvin|Calvin]] held that everyone carries an implanted sense of divinity, the sensus divinitatis, that produces belief in God directly, the way perception produces belief in the external world.[^calvin-institutes] [[Alvin Plantinga]] rebuilds this as a claim about warrant: a belief is warranted when it comes from cognitive faculties that are working properly, aimed at truth, in the setting they were made for.[^plantinga-wcb] If the sensus divinitatis is such a faculty, the belief it produces is warranted the same way ordinary perceptual belief is, and needs no argument behind it. The account of warrant doing the work here is general. It is Plantinga's analysis of knowledge applied to one more case, not a premise that assumes Christianity is true, and he made the same case earlier, in "Reason and Belief in God".[^plantinga-1983]

This is a narrower question than [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] asks. That claim reads Romans 1 and 2 on what creation shows and what it leaves people answerable for. This argument asks only whether a belief produced without an argument behind it can still be rational, which is a question about warrant, not a reading of Paul.

# Based On
- [[John Calvin|Calvin]]'s claim of an implanted sense of divinity in everyone.[^calvin-institutes]
- [[Alvin Plantinga]] rebuilds this as an account of warrant in *Warranted Christian Belief*, restating an argument he had already made in "Reason and Belief in God".[^plantinga-wcb][^plantinga-1983]
- [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]], the claim this argument was split out of.
- [[Humans Are Primed to See Agents and Purpose]] and [[Kelemen 2004]], the empirical side. Both hold open whether the instinct they describe is the sensus divinitatis at work or a natural process that explains belief in God without needing one to be real.
- The philosopher Kelly James Clark and [[Justin Barrett]] argue that findings in the cognitive science of religion fit a working belief forming faculty at least as well as they fit an account meant to explain the belief away.[^clark-barrett-2010]

# Countered By
- [[Evan Fales]] argues directly against Plantinga's account: a properly functioning faculty is identified only by its output, belief in God, so nothing independent of the belief itself shows the faculty is there, and unbelief gets explained away as a damaged version of the same faculty.[^fales-2003] Of the counters here, this is the strongest, since it is aimed at the warrant account itself rather than at a reading of Romans 1. [[Creation Alone Doesn't Point to God]] carries the same objection where it meets the reading of Romans 1 directly.
- [[Peoples, Duda & Marlowe 2016]]: if the sensus divinitatis is a real faculty, its output across 33 studied forager societies is animism in all of them and a single creator in a minority. What the faculty is supposed to produce and what actually shows up are not obviously the same thing.
- [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]]: a faculty everyone has, aimed at producing belief in God, should not leave so many people who looked honestly and came away unconvinced. Plantinga's own answer, in *Warranted Christian Belief*, is that the faculty is muffled by sin and can be suppressed without being destroyed.[^plantinga-wcb] [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] examines how far that answer can be pushed before it stops ruling anything out.
- The Great Pumpkin objection: if belief in God can be rational without an argument behind it, so can belief in anything, including the Great Pumpkin rising from the pumpkin patch every Halloween. Plantinga raises this against his own position and answers that which beliefs count as properly basic cannot be fixed by a general rule stated in advance. It has to be argued case by case, from examples of beliefs that are obviously reasonable to hold outright against examples that obviously are not.[^plantinga-1983] Needs sources: the exact page range and wording of that reply in "Reason and Belief in God" (1983). It is well attested in secondary discussion of the essay but was not opened directly for this draft.
- [[Demanding Belief Without the Proof]] presses the broader hiddenness complaint against any account, including this one, that relies on a belief producing mechanism rather than public evidence.

# Limits
- Conditional on theism. The argument shows belief in God is warranted if the sensus divinitatis is a properly functioning faculty, and whether it is depends on whether God exists. It supplies no independent test for whether the faculty is really there, which is exactly what would settle it, and exactly what Fales's objection above presses on.
- Plantinga's own reply to the Great Pumpkin objection gives no rule that fixes in advance which beliefs are properly basic. It argues from examples, which is enough to treat belief in God and belief in the Great Pumpkin differently, but it leaves the boundary a matter of argument rather than a test applied mechanically.
- The cognitive science material cited above is read two ways, and the split itself is a limit: since [[Humans Are Primed to See Agents and Purpose]] and the researchers behind it hold that a belief being natural does not make it true, the same findings cannot be used to independently secure the premise that a real, truth aimed faculty is at work.
