# Description
Belief in God can be warranted without resting on any argument, provided God exists and gave people a working faculty that forms it. [[Alvin Plantinga]] calls [[Warrant|warrant]] what turns true belief into knowledge (p. 153). A belief has it only if faculties working properly produce it, in a suitable environment, under a design plan successfully aimed at truth (p. 156). Nothing in this requires inference, and perception and memory give warranted belief without argument (pp. 175–176, 178).[^plantinga-wcb]

Plantinga models a [[Sensus divinitatis|sensus divinitatis]] on [[John Calvin|Calvin]], "a kind of faculty or a cognitive mechanism" that "in a wide variety of circumstances produces in us beliefs about God" (p. 172). The night sky, guilt and danger can trigger it (p. 174), and its beliefs "are not conclusions from" those circumstances (p. 175). Belief formed this way and warranted is [[Properly basic belief|properly basic]] (p. 178).[^plantinga-wcb]

If God exists and wants to be known, "the natural thing to think" is that he made people able to know him, so the belief is probably warranted (pp. 188–190). If God does not exist, probably no faculty successfully aimed at truth produces it, so it probably has no warrant (pp. 186–188). Whether it is warranted is therefore "not, after all, independent of the question whether theistic belief is true" (p. 191). A successful objection "will have to be to the truth of theism, not to its rationality" (p. 191). He adds, as the more modest claim, that no sensible objection of the other kind has "been so far proposed" (p. 191).[^plantinga-wcb] He claims only that the model is possible, open to no objection that does not assume Christian belief false, and near the truth if that belief is true (p. 351).[^plantinga-wcb]

# Based On
- *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000) is the source, with the account of warrant from *Warrant and Proper Function* (1993), to which p. 153 points. The argument's target is [[Evidentialism|evidentialism]] about God, "the claim that religious belief is rationally acceptable only if there are good arguments for it" (p. 82).[^plantinga-wcb]
- [[John Calvin|Calvin]] holds that the human mind has "by natural instinct, some sense of Deity" (*Institutes* I.3.1).[^calvin-institutes] Plantinga keeps "the basic idea" and says, "Whatever Calvin thinks, however, it's our model" (pp. 172–173).[^plantinga-wcb] [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] carries Calvin's claim.
- The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* calls the result "a stand-off between theists and de-bunkers of religion". On its account the belief comes from faculties working properly if God exists, and not if naturalism is true. It reports a 2016 paper's conclusion that "the de-bunker has failed to undercut religious belief".[^forrest-2025] [[Michael Bergmann]], who defends it, calls [[Reformed epistemology]] "a minority position in philosophy of religion" (p. 1). He also calls it "one of the more prominent positions" on the rationality of religious belief (p. 1).[^bergmann-2023]
- [[Kelly James Clark]] and [[Justin Barrett]] read the cognitive science of religion as this faculty at work. The same encyclopedia entry reports their suggestion that [[Agency detection|hypersensitive agency detection]] "could itself be part of the divine plan".[^forrest-2025] As [[Halvor Kvandal]] quotes them, their god-faculty is not "a dedicated functional system" but a general one (p. 189).[^kvandal-2019] Plantinga's book does not discuss this science.[^plantinga-wcb] [[Humans Are Primed to See Agents and Purpose]] carries the findings.

# Countered By
- The strong counter is the [[Defeater|defeater]] objection. It asks whether belief in God, properly basic when nothing tells against it, stays so for a reflective believer. [[Philip L. Quinn]] held that "many, perhaps most, intellectually sophisticated adult theists in our culture" are seldom if ever in conditions for beliefs about God to be properly basic for them. They have "very substantial reasons" for thinking God does not exist (p. 481). These range "from various problems of evil to naturalistic theories according to which theistic belief is illusory or merely projective" (p. 481).[^quinn-1985] Plantinga holds that the classic projection theories presuppose atheism (p. 368). Against Quinn's own version he argues that belief in God "is not ordinarily accepted as an explanation" (pp. 369–371). On evil: "If Christian theism is true", what we see "does not, in the typical case, constitute a defeater for belief in God" (p. 492).[^plantinga-wcb] In 1986 he held that the belief's own warrant may outweigh an objection, or that learning "from reliable sources" of a refutation may serve. Neither is an argument for God.[^plantinga-1986] By Plantinga's own summary, the defeaters he takes up leave intact the warrant Christian belief "can enjoy if the model, and indeed Christian belief, is, in fact, true" (p. 499).[^plantinga-wcb] [[Joshua C. Thurow|Joshua Thurow]] argues that the process cognitive science describes yields belief in "whatever kind of invisible agent concept happens to be floating around one's community" (p. 299). Given "some independent reason to doubt that God exists", such as evil, belief that issues only from it is "debunked" (p. 300). Belief resting partly on argument is not, and he counts reasoning from a phenomenon "taken to be a sign of God's existence" as argument (p. 300).[^thurow-2018]
- Kvandal argues that the cognitive science gives no support to a faculty specialised for God, since religious beliefs arise "as by-products of numerous other systems" (pp. 180–181). He argues that Clark and Barrett's general faculty departs from the claim "that we can know God without inference or argument" (p. 193). If the field's findings are approximately true, and justified belief must come from an epistemic virtue, he concludes that "the theistic beliefs subjects are prone to form are therefore unjustified".[^kvandal-2019] [[Lari Launonen]] argues that the strong [[Debunking argument|debunking arguments]] "gain their strength primarily from more familiar claims about evolutionary epistemology and religious diversity" (p. 1).[^launonen-2021] A 2019 paper finds "no good evidence that agency detection is meaningfully related to whether or not one has supernatural beliefs" (p. 225).[^vanleeuwen-2019] Barrett and a co-author say the field "cannot determine if those supernatural beings exist".[^greenway-2022]
- The Great Pumpkin objection asks: "if belief in God is properly basic, why can't just any belief be properly basic?" (1981, p. 48). Plantinga answered that criteria for proper basicality "must be reached from below rather than above", from examples (p. 50). "The Christian community is responsible to its set of examples, not to theirs" (p. 50). He added a difference that assumes God exists: there is "no Great Pumpkin and no natural tendency to accept beliefs about the Great Pumpkin" (p. 51).[^plantinga-1981] He took it up again in "Reason and Belief in God" (1983, pp. 74ff.), as his 2000 book notes (p. 344).[^plantinga-1983][^plantinga-wcb] In 2000 he calls the plain objection "plainly false", since granting that some beliefs are properly basic commits no one to all (p. 344). A sharper version, which he calls Son of Great Pumpkin, says voodooists could claim the same. Plantinga holds they could be "within their intellectual rights", but their belief may lack the warrant belief in God has (p. 346). That would be so "if the A/C model is true, but voodoo belief originates in some kind of cognitive error" (p. 346).[^plantinga-wcb]
- The encyclopedia entry names as "the most pressing criticism" of Plantinga's later position that it "largely ignores the question of justification, or reasonableness".[^forrest-2025] [[Richard Swinburne]] asks whether belief is "probably true, given our evidence" (pp. 207–208). Plantinga's reply is that probability on public evidence "is neither necessary nor sufficient for warranted Christian belief" (p. 221).[^plantinga-2001] A 2026 paper argues that the conditional "does not rule out theistic worlds where theistic belief is rationally impermissible" (p. 351).[^chan-2026]
- Other objections ask whether the faculty exists, which Plantinga holds is probable if God exists (pp. 188–190).[^plantinga-wcb] [[Evan Fales]] supposes theism true for the argument and asks for "any independent evidence, presumably of an empirical sort, for the existence of such faculties" (p. 360).[^fales-2003] The evidence on unbelief sits in [[Peoples, Duda & Marlowe 2016]], [[Creation Alone Doesn't Point to God]] and [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]]. [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] weighs the reply that the faculty can be damaged, and [[Belief in God Follows Birthplace]] the spread of belief.

# Limits
- The argument stands or falls on what happens when a reflective believer meets an objection she cannot answer that does not assume God is absent. Evil and Thurow's argument are the standing cases. On Plantinga's account a belief is defeated when a mind with the believer's beliefs and experience would give it up while working properly. The part of its design plan that sustains the belief must also be aimed only at truth (pp. 362–363). For one to whom God's existence "seems very clear", evil then gives no defeater from where she stands. Whether it gives any turns on the design plan, so on whether Christian belief is true (pp. 491–492).[^plantinga-wcb] Quinn asks, against each reason to doubt, for "an even better reason for thinking the potential defeater in question is false" (p. 483).[^quinn-1985] Plantinga's 1986 reply lets the belief's own warrant outweigh the objection, with no argument needed.[^plantinga-1986] It turns on which account of defeat is right, one fixed by proper function or one fixed by the reasons a believer has in view. That is a question for epistemology, and more sources will not settle it.
- Plantinga does not claim, as part of his philosophical position, that belief in God has warrant (p. 347). He adds, "I am not arguing that these beliefs are in fact true" (p. 347). Whether it is true lies, he says, "beyond the competence of philosophy" (p. 499). So "rational" singles out belief in God only in the sense of warrant, and probably only if God exists (pp. 346–347).[^plantinga-wcb]
- The account of warrant assumes no Christianity, nor that a design plan "implies having been created by God" (p. 154). Yet a note on that page says Plantinga's earlier book argues "there is no viable naturalistic account of proper function".[^plantinga-wcb] Others who hold a proper function account of warrant give naturalistic ones.[^boyce-iep] Plantinga adds that the account "needs a certain kind of supplementation and fine tuning" (p. 156).[^plantinga-wcb]
- Calvin gives the sense the purpose of leaving people without excuse, "to prevent any man from pretending ignorance" (I.3.1).[^calvin-institutes] A defender of Plantinga's reading calls it "a legitimate development and explanation of Calvin's implicit epistemology" (p. 100). The same paper holds Calvin committed to "a rationality of proper function", whether or not he was concerned with rationality as "intellectual rights" (p. 100).[^sudduth-1998] Plantinga notes that the [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] passage he draws on suggests the knowledge of God it describes comes "by way of inference" (p. 176). So, "strictly speaking", it "would not be basic" (p. 176). The inference, he adds, is so quick that it may be hard to tell from believing in the basic way (p. 176).[^plantinga-wcb]
- Plantinga holds that the same standing probably extends to "Judaism, Islam" and some other religions, but not to voodooism, flat earth belief or naturalism (pp. 349–350).[^plantinga-wcb] On this account, a parallel built to order, with a faculty of its own, would differ from belief in God only in which of the two is true. Extending the standing to other theistic religions costs bare belief in God nothing, and [[Experiential Confirmation Doesn't Distinguish Religions]] takes up the Christian case.
