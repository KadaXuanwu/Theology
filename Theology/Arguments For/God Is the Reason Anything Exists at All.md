# Description
God is offered as the reason there is a universe at all, not as one cause among the causes inside it.[^wildman-2006] The objection that God gets slotted in wherever an explanation is missing, so that every advance in science takes ground back, is aimed at a different claim. A finished physics would say how things behave once there are things to behave.[^ellis-2007][^carroll-2021] The argument counts the laws themselves among what still needs accounting for, whether they might have been otherwise or rest on the natures of things that need not exist.[^pruss-2009][^carroll-2021]

[[Thomas Aquinas]] separates creating from keeping in existence and treats the second as the first still going on, calling preservation "a continuation of that action whereby He gives existence".[^aquinas-st-1041] He cites [[Augustine]] for it: were God's ruling power withdrawn, "all nature would collapse".[^aquinas-st-1041] He then denies that this makes God the only thing keeping each creature in being, since some creatures depend on other creatures for their preservation as well as on God as their principal cause.[^aquinas-st-1042] The dependence being claimed runs at every moment rather than at a first one.[^aquinas-st-1041] Aquinas argued that even a world without beginning would be caused by God in everything in it.[^marenbon-2024] [[Sean Carroll]] finds no requirement in physics for something outside the universe to sustain it, and argues the universe can simply exist.[^carroll-2021]

What the argument reaches for is a [[Necessary being|necessary being]], with everything else owing its existing to it.[^reichenbach-2026] On that account God is not one more item in the world's inventory.[^reichenbach-2026] [[Brian Davies]] describes [[Classical theism|classical theism]] this way: "God is what accounts for there being any world at all".[^page-2019] [[Philip L. Quinn]], who calls God a person, defends creation and conservation too.[^page-2019]

# Based On
- The philosophical form is the contingency argument in [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]]'s version. In 1697 he argued that even a world without beginning needs a reason outside the series of its states.[^leibniz-1697] [[Alexander R. Pruss|Pruss]] gives it five steps. Every contingent fact has an explanation; one contingent fact includes all the others; so it has an explanation; that explanation must involve a necessary being; and the being is God.[^pruss-2009] Its defenders give reasons for the first step. Pruss writes that people believe the principle mainly because they take it to be "self-evident, obvious, intuitively clear, in no need of argumentative support".[^pruss-2006] He grants that "if we want to convince others, we need arguments".[^pruss-2009] He holds that calling something a brute fact "should be a last resort", since doing so where a not implausible explanation can be formulated "would undercut the practice of science".[^pruss-2006] The argument is still worked on in analytic philosophy of religion, for example by Pruss and [[Joshua L. Rasmussen|Rasmussen]].[^pruss-2018][^oppy-2019] Their 2018 version restricts the first step to the fact that contingent causal things exist.[^oppy-2019]
- The Stanford Encyclopedia reports that the cosmological argument was "largely abandoned outside of Thomistic circles" in the first part of the 20th century. In the late 20th and early 21st centuries theists and nontheists have generally shown "a healthy skepticism" about it, though "dissenting voices can be heard".[^reichenbach-2026] In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, 35.9% of philosophers who answered on the [[Principle of sufficient reason|principle of sufficient reason]] accepted or leaned toward "true" and 45.7% toward "false". The question names no form of the principle.[^bourget-chalmers-2023]
- [Colossians 1:17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians%201:17&version=NIV) "in him all things hold together". συνέστηκεν is a perfect of συνίστημι, glossed in Thayer as to cohere or hold together.[^thayer-1889] The clause says things hold together in him; its one verb means to cohere or hold together, not to sustain.[^thayer-1889] In the perfect the verb is intransitive, with the things as its subject, and such a perfect marks "the enduring result rather than the completed act".[^thayer-1889][^smyth-1920] A classic commentary glosses the verb "hold together, cohere", calls Christ "the principle of cohesion in the universe", and sets [Hebrews 1:3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%201:3&version=NIV) beside it, where Christ is "sustaining the Universe".[^lightfoot-1879] A study of the term reads the verse as about cohesion more than bare existence.[^collins-2014]
- [Acts 17:28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%2017:28&version=NIV) "in him we live and move and have our being". The last phrase renders ἐσμέν, plain "we are", so the English is heavier than the Greek.[^holmes-2010] [[Mark Allan Powell]] notes that the line survives in no ancient text, and says Clement of Alexandria and Chrysostom credited it to Epimenides.[^powell-2018] Both are in fact silent on who wrote this line.[^rothschild-2014] Augustine read the first clause as God working without pause in what he made, and [[John Calvin|Calvin]] cited the verse for universal providence.[^augustine-gen-ad-lit][^calvin-institutes]

# Countered By
- [[God of the Gaps]] is the argument this one answers.
- **Oppy's parity is the strong counter.** A naturalist, [[Graham Oppy]] argues, can stop where the theist stops. The stopping point can be the bare fact that some things exist (p. 769), or a first natural state that every possible world shares (pp. 770–771). Those undecided on whether a necessary being exists, he holds, "will quite properly be unmoved by any of these arguments" (p. 770). He expects that what philosophers say about them "is determined by their background views" (p. 771).[^oppy-2019] In *Arguing about Gods* he argues that no argument for or against God is successful, in the sense of one that ought to persuade every reasonable person.[^oppy-2006][^pruss-2007] [[Kenneth L. Pearce|Kenneth Pearce]], who defends the argument, accepts the parity for causal explanations and claims an advantage only for God as the ground of the whole, not a cause within it. That, he writes, "will be decisive only if a sufficiently strong Principle of Sufficient Reason is endorsed".[^pearce-2017]
- [[Peter van Inwagen]] argues that if all contingent truths together had an explanation, nothing could have been otherwise.[^vaninwagen-1983][^pruss-2009] Pruss calls this "the main objection" and answers that a free choice can explain without entailing what it explains.[^pruss-2009]
- [[Bertrand Russell]] held that the universe is "just there, and that's all".[^russell-copleston-1948] Carroll argues that every answer ends in "a feature of reality that has no further explanation", and that we cannot demand a reason we would find satisfying. He leaves open that a deeper principle might explain why this universe exists, with that principle itself then left unexplained.[^carroll-2021]
- The conserving half rests on a further premise, that each thing needs God at every moment to go on existing. A 2023 book defends the rival view, that at least some things in time go on existing with nothing sustaining them unless something destroys them. It argues that a proof from contingent beings to a necessary being fails.[^schmid-linford-2023]
- [[Herman Philipse]] argues that theism, even if it could be stated meaningfully, has no significant predictive power.[^philipse-2012][^pinsent-2013] [[Andrew Pinsent]]'s review notes that at certain points it is ambiguous whether Philipse is attacking proofs of a first cause or necessary being, or an expanded understanding of God that includes personal attributes. Most of the book, Pinsent writes, targets the God of [[Richard Swinburne]]'s theism as a "theory".[^pinsent-2013]
- [[Gregory W. Dawes|Gregory Dawes]] grants that divine action could in principle explain something, then argues it scores badly on ordinary explanatory virtues.[^dawes-2009][^monton-2009] He concludes that his arguments "might give us reason to prefer natural explanations when these are available". A theistic explanation should then be "at best, an explanation of last resort".[^dawes-2009]
- Even if it works it delivers a necessary being, not the God of Christianity. Pruss writes that "rare is the Christian cosmological arguer who claims to be able to show that the First Cause is a Trinity".[^pruss-2009] Aquinas holds that natural reason cannot reach the Trinity.[^aquinas-st-321] One reply to Oppy argues that there can be only one first cause. A critic of that reply argues that the same reasoning would allow only one divine person.[^schmid-2023] [[Immanuel Kant]] argued that the step from a necessary being to God relies on the ontological argument.[^kant-1787] The Stanford Encyclopedia rejects this, partly because the argument needs necessity only in fact, which Swinburne holds of God though God could have failed to exist.[^reichenbach-2026] So the argument can escape the gap objection and still fall short of the creed.

# Limits
- The argument stands or falls on one premise: that there being anything at all that might not have existed, laws and starting state included, has an explanation. Many critics, the Stanford Encyclopedia reports, put the key in this premise and in the one that contingent things alone cannot supply that explanation.[^reichenbach-2026] One cosmologist argues that physics cannot reach the question.[^ellis-2007] A poll of philosophers does not settle it, and escaping the gap objection is no evidence for it. What would move it is an argument that the principle holds for the whole world without making everything necessary. That is where Pruss and van Inwagen part.[^pruss-2009] So might a case that anyone who seeks explanations is committed to the principle. One has been made on practical grounds, binding the inquirer until a fact turns out to be brute.[^melamed-lin-2023]
- The claim is wider than the record supports. The same theologians defend miracles and providence, and the creeds are claims about events. Aquinas also holds that God can act "by producing the effects of secondary causes without them".[^aquinas-st-1056] Calvin would not grant universal providence without God's "special charge of every one of his works".[^calvin-institutes] The Fourth Lateran Council, an ecumenical council held in 1215, teaches that God created "at the beginning of time".[^lateran-iv] What holds is narrower: the contingency argument depends on no gap in natural explanation, so closing such gaps does not touch it. That covers one argument, not theism as practised.
- [[Nicholas Saunders]] separates general divine action, creating and keeping things in being, from special divine action, God bringing about physical effects that would not have happened had he not acted.[^tracy-2003] In his Preface (p. xvii) he concludes that neither the quantum nor the chaos approach to special divine action "survives detailed scientific and theological scrutiny", which leaves "very little detailed contemporary support" for it.[^saunders-2002] Two reviewers report that he still rates [[Arthur Peacocke]]'s whole and part model the best account on offer (p. 213), a model his Preface calls a bold metaphysical assertion.[^tracy-2003][^yong-2008] The immunity claimed here covers only the first kind. The line itself is not clear-cut, and the Stanford Encyclopedia judges that the Thomist account, on which God supports and grounds every created cause, blurs it.[^de-cruz-2022]
- [[Justin Barrett]] and [[Frank Keil]] found university and college students using an anthropomorphic God concept inconsistent with their own stated theology. The three studies had 52, 81 and 13 subjects.[^barrett-keil-1996] The stories probed whether God hears, attends and acts in one place at a time.[^heiphetz-2016][^hodge-2018] None of that bears on whether he is necessary. The reading of the task is disputed.[^hodge-2018][^heiphetz-2016] A 2025 study found US adults more likely than chance to allow a universe without God. The authors say this does not refute the argument.[^nichols-steinberg-2025] Whatever this argument defends, it is not obviously the concept people run on, which [[Humans Are Primed to See Agents and Purpose]] takes up.
- It sits badly beside other nodes. [[Religious Experience Is Evidence for God]] and [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]] put the evidence in lived experience. [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] rests on [Romans 1:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:20&version=NIV) and God's nature being seen from what has been made. Both kinds are observational claims, though [[Joseph Fitzmyer|Fitzmyer]] renders Romans 1:20 as perceived "by reflection on what he has made".[^fitzmyer-1993] [[Creation Alone Doesn't Point to God]] treats unfalsifiability as a cost, so it cannot be a virtue here. One theologian holds that a design argument that succeeded would count against theologies of God as the ground of being.[^wildman-2006] [[No Outcome Counts Against It]] takes up what that costs.
