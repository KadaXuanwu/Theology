# Description
God is offered as the reason there is a universe at all, not as the cause of some event inside it. The objection that God gets slotted in wherever an explanation is missing, so that every advance in science takes ground back, is aimed at a different claim. A finished physics would say how things behave once there are things to behave. A law of gravity is already something on this account, and the something is what still needs accounting for.

The distinction is not a modern retreat. [[Thomas Aquinas]] separates creating from keeping in existence and treats the second as the first still going on, calling preservation "a continuation of that action whereby He gives existence".[^aquinas-st-1041] He then denies that this makes God the only thing keeping each creature in being, since some creatures depend on other creatures for their preservation as well as on God as their principal cause.[^aquinas-st-1042] The dependence being claimed runs at every moment rather than at a first one, and it is not the kind physics competes for.

What the argument reaches for is a being that could not have failed to exist, with everything else owing its existing to it. On that account God is not one more item in the world's inventory, so there is no place in physics for the claim to be crowded out of. This is what classical theism names. The Stanford Encyclopedia dates the split inside perfect being theology, between classical theists and theistic personalists, perhaps as early as 1644.

# Based On
- The philosophical form is the contingency argument: things that might not have existed do exist, so the explanation is something that could not have failed to. Still worked on in analytic philosophy of religion, for example by [[Alexander R. Pruss|Pruss]] and [[Joshua L. Rasmussen|Rasmussen]].[^pruss-2018]
- [Colossians 1:17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians%201:17&version=NIV) "in him all things hold together". συνέστηκεν is a perfect of συνίστημι, glossed in Thayer as to cohere or hold together. The clause says things hold together in him, not that he holds them together, and no word in it means sustain. Whether the perfect carries an ongoing present sense is the load bearing point for the sustaining reading, and it is not sourced here.
- [Acts 17:28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%2017:28&version=NIV) "in him we live and move and have our being". The last phrase renders ἐσμέν, plain "we are", so the English is heavier than the Greek. [[Mark Allan Powell]] notes that the line survives in no ancient text and was credited to Epimenides by Clement of Alexandria and Chrysostom, and that the second quotation in the verse has similar lines in two Stoic poets, Aratus and Cleanthes.

# Countered By
- [[God of the Gaps]] is the argument this one answers.
- The strong counter is [[Herman Philipse]], who argues that theism has no significant predictive power.[^philipse-2012] [[Andrew Pinsent]]'s review notes it is ambiguous whether Philipse is attacking proofs of a first cause or an expanded understanding of God that includes personal attributes, which is the distinction this node runs on.
- [[Gregory W. Dawes|Gregory Dawes]] grants that divine action could in principle explain something, then argues it scores badly on ordinary explanatory virtues.[^dawes-2009]
- [[Graham Oppy]] argues theism moves the unexplained fact rather than removing it, since God's choice to create this universe rather than another is itself contingent.[^oppy-2006] [[Edward Feser]] replied.[^feser-2021]
- Even if it works it delivers a necessary being, not the God of Christianity. Kant argued that the step from a necessary being to God relies on the ontological argument, and the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that a recent version of the argument reaches "a very powerful and intelligent designer-creator, not the all-perfect God of [[Anselm]]". This concedes the point about gaps and still leaves the argument short of the creed.
- Of these, only Pinsent's review and the Stanford Encyclopedia entry were opened. Philipse, Dawes and Oppy were reached at abstract or not at all, so this section is cited rather than checked.

# Limits
- The claim is wider than the record supports, and this line is reasoning rather than a sourced finding. The same theologians defend miracles and providence, and the creeds are claims about events. What holds is narrower: the contingency argument depends on no gap, so closing gaps does not touch it. That covers one argument, not theism as practised.
- [[Nicholas Saunders]] separates general divine action, creating and keeping things in being, from special divine action, miracles and answered prayer. Reviewers report he finds the attempts to square the second with quantum theory and chaos theory still far from a satisfactory account.[^saunders-2002] The immunity claimed here covers only the first kind.
- [[Justin Barrett]] and [[Frank Keil]] found people using an anthropomorphic God concept inconsistent with their own stated theology, across three studies with 52, 81 and 13 subjects.[^barrett-keil-1996] Whatever this argument defends, it is not obviously the concept people run on.
- It sits badly beside other nodes, again reasoning rather than a source. [[Personal Relationship with God]] and [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]] put the evidence in lived experience, and [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] rests on [Romans 1:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201:20&version=NIV) and God's nature being seen from what has been made. Both are observational claims. [[Creation Alone Doesn't Point to God]] treats unfalsifiability as fatal, so it cannot be a virtue here.
