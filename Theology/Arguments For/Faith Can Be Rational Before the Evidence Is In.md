# Description
Choosing to believe ahead of the evidence can be rational when the evidence cannot settle the question, the choice cannot be put off, and declining to decide is itself one of the two live options rather than a neutral third one.

[[William James]] built this case in "The Will to Believe" against the rule of the mathematician and philosopher W. K. Clifford that believing anything on insufficient evidence is always wrong.[^james-1896][^clifford-1877] James limits the license to what he calls a genuine option: one that is living, meaning it already has some pull on the person facing it, forced, meaning there is no way to opt out that does not amount to choosing one side, and momentous, meaning the stakes matter and the choice cannot simply be revisited later.[^james-1896] Where all three hold and the intellect alone cannot decide the question, treating the choice to withhold belief as safe or neutral misdescribes it. It is a choice too, with a risk of its own.

James took his supporting cases from outside religion first. Whether someone likes you often depends, he writes, on whether you meet them half way and assume that they do. A train of passengers, each individually brave, will be robbed by a few armed men simply because none of them knows the others will back him up if he resists.[^james-1896] In both cases the outcome depends on believing it before the proof is in, which is what keeps the same move, applied to religious belief, from being special pleading invented for it. Blaise Pascal, the 17th century French mathematician and philosopher, had already made a pragmatic case for belief along similar lines, weighing an infinite possible gain against a finite stake. James distances his own argument from Pascal's: without some existing pull toward the specific wager on offer, he writes, "the option offered to the will by Pascal is not a living option" at all.[^james-1896]

The scriptural pattern of obeying before confirmation is a separate case, carried by the claim [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]]. This argument is the philosophical one, that the leap can be rational on premises that do not themselves assume Christianity is true.

# Based On
- [[William James]], "The Will to Believe" (1896), replying to W. K. Clifford, whose "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) argued the opposite rule.[^james-1896][^clifford-1877]
- [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]], the scriptural pattern of obedience before confirmation. This argument runs beside that claim rather than restating it.
- [Psalm 34:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2034:8&version=NIV) and [Jeremiah 29:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah%2029:13&version=NIV) put the same structure as an invitation: try it, then see.
- Blaise Pascal's wager is the older pragmatic case for belief, built on similar reasoning. The Description above gives James's own reason for treating it as a separate case.

# Countered By
- W. K. Clifford's own rule is the strongest counter, and the one this argument exists to answer: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence."[^clifford-1877] Clifford does not rest this on outcomes. In the section he titles "The Duty of Inquiry" he argues that a shipowner who sincerely believed his aging ship seaworthy, on evidence he had stifled his own doubts to keep, would still be guilty of the deaths that followed even if the ship completed the crossing, because for Clifford the right or wrong of a belief turns on how it was reached, not on whether it happened to be true.[^clifford-1877] On that rule a leap that turns out well is not thereby justified, so James has to answer Clifford's argument for the rule and not only its conclusion.
- [[Belief Isn't Directly Chosen]]: if belief cannot be picked the way an action can, the choice between believing and not believing that James describes is not open to begin with, only the indirect routes that claim covers.
- [[Experiential Confirmation Doesn't Distinguish Religions]]: the same kind of commitment that James says can help bring about its own confirmation also confirms Islam, Mormon belief, and other traditions that contradict each other, so the method cannot be what recommends Christianity over them.
- [[The Threat of Hell Compels the People Who Believe It]]: a step taken under an eternal penalty is not the free choice between two live options that James describes, since fear of the penalty is doing some of the choosing.
- [[Demanding Belief Without the Proof]]: if the evidential gap is one God could close and does not, the option James asks people to take is not the ordinary kind of forced choice his other examples describe, where nobody is withholding the missing proof.

# Limits
- The whole argument depends on the option really being forced, that declining to believe functions as a live choice with a risk of its own rather than a neutral default that costs nothing. James's own cases show this can be true for some facts, where believing something helps bring it about. Whether it is true of belief in God specifically, that withholding belief forfeits some chance that would otherwise arrive, is what the argument needs and does not itself show. Settling it needs an account of what withholding belief is supposed to forfeit, beyond the stakes being described as high.
- The option must also be living in James's own sense, meaning it already has some pull on the person choosing. He denies this is true of Pascal's wager for someone with no prior inclination toward it. The same test limits how far this argument reaches: for someone with no antecedent draw toward Christianity at all, the option may not be live for them either, and the permission James grants may not reach that person.
- It licenses belief. It does not make it true. What it reaches is permission, not evidence.
