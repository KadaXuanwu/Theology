# Description
Faith that runs ahead of the evidence can be rational when the question is [[Living option|living]], [[Forced option|forced]] and [[Momentous option|momentous]] and the evidence cannot settle it. Waiting is then a choice with its own risk. In "The Will to Believe", [[William James]] made this case against [[W. K. Clifford]]'s [[Evidentialism|evidentialist]] rule that belief on insufficient evidence is always wrong.[^james-1897][^clifford-1877]

Waiting means acting as if the answer were no. If the answer is yes, James writes, the one who waits loses the good "just as certainly as if we positively chose to disbelieve". Putting the avoidance of error ahead of the gaining of truth is itself a preference the evidence does not supply.[^james-1897]

For some facts, faith helps create the fact, as when trusting that another person likes you is what makes the liking come. A rule forbidding faith until the evidence is in would keep such facts from coming at all. For religion, James calls irrational any rule "which would absolutely prevent me from acknowledging certain kinds of truth if those kinds of truth were really there".[^james-1897]

James sets out the rule for moral questions and social life before he turns to religion, so it is not a rule written for religion alone. He calls it a right to believe "any hypothesis that is live enough to tempt our will", and grants that "we may wait if we will".[^james-1897] Stated as a choice to commit rather than to believe, the argument does not need belief to be chosen at will, which [[Belief Isn't Directly Chosen]] denies.

# Based On
- James calls an option genuine when it is "of the forced, living, and momentous kind". Where such an option "cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds", our passional nature "not only lawfully may, but must, decide" it.[^james-1897] That is the 1897 book text; the 1896 magazine printing reads "must, and lawfully may".[^james-1896]
- James grants that we are "absolutely impotent" to believe a plain falsehood just by willing it, such as that Lincoln is a myth. What he defends is the part our "willing nature" plays, meaning "fear and hope, prejudice and passion".[^james-1897] In a 1904 letter he wrote that the essay's title "should have been the right to believe".[^koopman-2017]
- His cases of faith that brings its fact about include another person's liking, promotions, and passengers who could resist train robbers only if each trusted the others. He concludes that "in truths dependent on our personal action" faith based on desire is "certainly a lawful and possibly an indispensable thing".[^james-1897] The 1896 printing has no train robbery and reads "where faith in a fact, based on need of the fact, can create the fact".[^james-1896]
- Moral questions, James adds, "cannot wait for sensible proof".[^james-1897]
- In a trust experiment, people often fulfilled trust because they believed the other player trusted them, which the authors call "trust responsiveness".[^bacharach-2007] A review of 35 years of classroom research finds that teacher expectations do fulfil themselves, but the effects are "typically small". It adds that expectations may predict outcomes more because they are accurate than because they fulfil themselves.[^jussim-harber-2005]
- [[Lara Buchak]] argues that faith in p "requires terminating one's search for further evidence and acting on the supposition that p". It needs no degree of belief beyond what the evidence supports. On [[Expected value|expected utility]] theory it can be rational "if the costs associated with gathering further evidence or postponing the decision are high", in religious and ordinary life alike.[^buchak-2012][^bishop-mckaughan-2023]
- [[Elizabeth Jackson]] argues that belief, faith and hope can each make it rational to act as if p, and keep a long commitment rational "in the face of counterevidence".[^jackson-2021]
- The scriptural pattern of obeying first and seeing after is the claim [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]], which cites [Psalm 34:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2034:8&version=NIV) and [Jeremiah 29:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah%2029:13&version=NIV). In context, Psalm 34:8 follows the speaker's report of answered prayer in [Psalm 34:4–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2034:4-6&version=NIV). Jeremiah 29:13 sits inside a promise to the exiles in Babylon of return after seventy years ([Jeremiah 29:10–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah%2029:10-14&version=NIV)), echoing [Deuteronomy 4:29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%204:29&version=NIV).

# Countered By
- **Clifford's case is the strong counter:** it judges how a belief was reached, and its duty of investigating reaches action as well as belief. His rule, at p. 295, is "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for any one, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence". James misquotes it as "for every one".[^clifford-1877][^james-1897] Clifford's shipowner silences his doubts about an old ship, acquires "a sincere and comfortable conviction" that she is safe, and she sinks with her passengers. He is "verily guilty", because the question "has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it". Had she arrived safely, he "would only have been not found out".[^clifford-1877]
- Inquiry, for Clifford, "is not to be made once for all", and "It is never lawful to stifle a doubt". Even someone who cannot think otherwise "still has a choice in regard to the action suggested by it, and so cannot escape the duty of investigating".[^clifford-1877] The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* reads this as a duty across time to "go out and gather evidence, remain open to new evidence".[^chignell-2018]
- His ground is social: "no one man's belief is in any case a private matter which concerns himself alone". The danger is that society "should become credulous, and lose the habit of testing things and inquiring into them".[^clifford-1877]
- Clifford allows three narrow exceptions. Testimony counts when there is reason to trust the informant. There is a duty "to act upon probabilities" before belief is justified, because such action is how "evidence is got". Beyond the reach of verification he allows hypotheses, not beliefs.[^clifford-1877]
- Evidentialism of some sort is "far and away the dominant ethic of belief" among early modern and contemporary philosophers.[^chignell-2018] On a dominant version of it, the one epistemic duty is to hold the attitude present evidence supports, and whether to gather more is left to morality and prudence. A 2023 paper in *Philosophical Studies* rejects that view and argues that "there are epistemic norms on evidence-gathering".[^flores-woodard-2023]
- [[Bertrand Russell]] argued in 1909 that the option is not forced. At an unmarked fork one must take a road, "But I do not believe either hypothesis". For religion one may hold the belief "more or less probable" with "a greater or less degree of hope that it may be true", and keep part of the comfort.[^russell-1909] Complete belief in a momentous question, he adds, "will justify persecution", which partial belief does not.[^russell-1909]
- The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on faith notes a reply from probability theory. Some degree of belief always fits the evidence, leaving "no possible circumstances where 'the evidence does not decide'".[^bishop-mckaughan-2023]

# Limits
- The argument stands or falls on whether waiting really is a choice with its own cost. If acting on the hypothesis with partial belief and hope keeps whatever good commitment brings, the option is not forced and the permission never applies.[^russell-1909] What that good is depends on the hypothesis in question, so it cannot be settled from outside. [[John Bishop]] argues that belief may be ventured only where what matters is not acting on p "with some more or less specific degree of confidence" but believing it. He holds that theism's framework principles may be such an option, "with suspension of judgment practically equivalent to rejection". That needs them to be beyond assessment by evidence, which he says "would, of course, require extended argument".[^bishop-2002]
- The commitment form asks a person to stop looking, which Buchak permits when waiting is costly and Clifford's "never lawful to stifle a doubt" forbids.[^buchak-2012][^bishop-mckaughan-2023][^clifford-1877] Which rule of inquiry is right is a question about norms that more evidence will not settle.
- For religion James claims only that it feels "as if evidence might be forever withheld from us unless we met the hypothesis half-way". He makes even this conditional on the hypothesis being true.[^james-1897] That conditional gives no reason to expect the evidence to come. For facts that do depend on the believer, the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* notes the reply that knowing faith helps create the fact is itself evidence for it.[^chignell-2018]
- Some of what follows religious commitment is experience the practice itself shapes. Christians randomly assigned a month of imaginative prayer reported more unusual religious experience than a Bible study group, with substantial individual differences.[^luhrmann-2013] [[Tanya Luhrmann]], the study's first author, says she lacks "the authority to pronounce on whether God is real or whether God is not real".[^npr-2012] If evidence of God waits on commitment, that is the withholding [[Demanding Belief Without the Proof]] counts against God.
- The permission reaches only someone for whom the option is live. James used this test against [[Blaise Pascal]]'s wager, which "is not a living option" without "some pre-existing tendency to believe in masses and holy water".[^james-1897] Russell notes that on James's principles the beliefs "different men ought to adopt, are different".[^russell-1909] The argument therefore favours no faith over another, and [[Experiential Confirmation Doesn't Distinguish Religions]] carries the same cost for experience.
- Nothing in it makes Christianity more likely to be true.
