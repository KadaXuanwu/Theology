  - Pascal's wager. Reason cannot settle whether God exists, so treat the choice as a bet. If God exists the believer gains everything and if not the believer loses little, so wager on God. Pensées, the wager fragment, Lafuma 418, Brunschvicg 233.
  - The argument is a decision under uncertainty, not evidence. An infinite gain outweighs any finite cost at any probability above zero, so it needs no view on how likely God is, only that he is not impossible.
  - Pascal answers the reply that belief cannot be chosen by prescribing practice first. Act as a believer acts and belief follows. That answer is part of the argument and needs stating.
  - The standard objections need stating at full strength. The many gods objection, that the wager does not say which God to bet on. The objection that a God who rewards belief taken up for gain is not the Christian God. The mixed strategies objection from Hájek, that once any strategy with a chance of ending in belief has infinite expected value, the wager no longer picks out belief.
  - William James distanced his own argument from the wager, saying an option with no existing pull on a person is not a living option, so the two pragmatic arguments are kept apart.

# Description
Wagering on God is the better bet for anyone who gives him some chance of existing, because the bet can win something infinite and stakes only something finite. [[Blaise Pascal]] sets it out in the *Pensées* fragment that opens "Infini rien" (Lafuma 418, Brunschvicg 233, Sellier 680).[^pascal-proust]

Reason, Pascal says, "can decide nothing here", and no one can stay out: "you must wager. It is not optional. You are embarked."[^pascal-1670] If God exists, the one who wagers for him gains "an infinity of an infinitely happy life", and "what you stake is finite".[^pascal-1670] If the bet is lost, Pascal writes, "vous ne perdez rien", though the believer goes without "plaisirs empestés", glory and "délices".[^pascal-proust]

Pascal reasons next from equal chances, then lets the odds drop out: there is "a chance of gain against a finite number of chances of loss".[^pascal-1670] This third argument is the one the literature calls the wager. At any positive, finite chance an infinite gain outweighs any finite stake, so wagering for God has the higher [[Expected value|expected value]].[^hajek-2022]

Pascal's objector replies, "I am forced to wager, and am not free. I am not released, and am so made that I cannot believe."[^pascal-1670] [[Belief Isn't Directly Chosen]] sets out the same point. Pascal's answer is to begin with practice, following those who began "by acting as if they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said".[^pascal-1670] This lessens the passions, and Pascal adds: "Naturellement même cela vous fera croire et vous abêtira."[^pascal-proust] One English translation renders the last words "make you believe, and deaden your acuteness". [[William James]], translating freely, gives "stupefy your scruples".[^pascal-1670][^james-1896]

The practice pays in this life too: "You will be faithful, honest, humble, grateful, generous, a sincere friend, truthful".[^pascal-1670] The argument gives a reason to act, not evidence that God exists.[^jordan-2022]

# Based On
- [[Alan Hájek]]'s encyclopedia entry finds four arguments in the fragment, three of them main ones. It treats the third, from infinite expected value at any positive, finite chance, as the wager proper.[^hajek-2022]
- [[Stephen T. Davis]] reads the practice route as indirect belief formation: acting as believers act brings belief, perhaps after months or years. He assumes rather than shows that it works.[^davis-1991] [[Jeff Jordan]] holds that acting on the wager needs only indirect influence over belief, not the power to believe at will.[^jordan-2022]
- James's own case, set out in [[Faith Can Be Rational Before the Evidence Is In]], applies only to a [[Living option|living option]].[^james-1896] He calls Pascal's option "not a living option" without "some pre-existing tendency to believe in masses and holy water".[^james-1896] Where that tendency exists, Pascal's argument "seems a regular clincher".[^james-1896] Jordan writes that James "probably saw his argument as having a similar status as Pascal's Wager", since James praises the wager for those already inclined.[^jordan-2022] He notes that apologists can use Pascal's third version as "a worst-case device", even against a strong case for atheism. James's argument licenses belief only in an option that is "intellectually open".[^jordan-2022]

# Countered By
- The strong counter is the many gods objection. Any faith that promises an infinite reward for its own practice can run the same argument.[^hajek-2022] [[Denis Diderot]] put it in one line: "Un iman en peut dire tout autant que Pascal". It stands in his *Addition aux Pensées philosophiques*, published in 1770 according to his editors, not in the 1746 *Pensées philosophiques*.[^diderot-1770] Of Pascal's option James writes that "no Turk ever took to masses and holy water on its account". His Mahdi offers "your infinite gain if I am genuine against your finite sacrifice if I am not".[^james-1896] [[J. L. Mackie]] names "the Anabaptists or the Mormons or the Muslim Sunnis" among the churches where salvation might lie instead of Rome.[^mackie-1982][^hajek-2022] Islam makes that promise: the Qur'an offers those who believe and do good "everlasting Gardens graced with flowing streams, where they will stay forever" (Q 98:7–8).[^abdel-haleem-2004] Such a rival gives both wagers an infinite expected value, so they tie.[^hajek-2022] A principle that breaks the tie by probability is, in Hájek's words, "not found in the Wager itself, although it might be regarded as a friendly addition".[^hajek-2022] So the wager can turn an existing preference for one faith into a reason to act, but cannot supply the preference.[^hajek-2022][^james-1896]
- Jordan replies that a person need weigh only the hypotheses live for them in James's sense.[^jordan-2018] Earlier he held that gods with no tradition behind them can be set aside, and others appeal to the simplest conception of God.[^hajek-2022] A 2001 paper argues that both restrictions, to traditional and to simplest hypotheses, fail on epistemic and anthropological grounds.[^saka-2001] A 2019 paper proposes a way of comparing infinite rewards that it says also answers the many gods objection.[^hajek-2022][^jackson-rogers-2019] [[Graham Oppy]]'s 2026 reply argues that it fails there.[^oppy-2026] Oppy finds a 2007 treatment of some cases with finitely many gods "quite compelling".[^oppy-2018][^bartha-2007] He holds that uncountably many possible gods, each perhaps with only an [[Infinitesimal|infinitesimal]] chance, remain "a serious, independent objection".[^oppy-2018]
- The [[Mixed strategy|mixed strategies]] objection attacks the arithmetic. [[Antony Duff]] put it in 1986 and Hájek developed it in 2003.[^hajek-2003] Since no course of action makes it certain that one will never come to believe in God, Duff wrote, "every course of action has an infinite expected value".[^duff-1986] Tossing a coin and wagering only on heads then scores as high as wagering outright.[^hajek-2022] Hájek counts anything an agent does as "really a mixed strategy" between wagering for and against.[^hajek-2003] So the wager as stated picks out no act.[^hajek-2003] Several critics, Mackie among them, hold the argument valid while rejecting it on other grounds.[^hajek-2022] Hájek himself offers valid reformulations of the wager, then argues that each falls to a dilemma.[^hajek-2003] A repair, he argues, needs salvation's value to stay the same when any finite amount is added, so that nothing beats it. It must also shrink when multiplied by a chance below one. He calls this, at p. 47, "the seemingly impossible".[^hajek-2003]
- A 2011 defence answers that whoever loses the toss can toss again, so repeated mixing ends in wagering for God. A 2012 reply and counter-reply dispute whether it must.[^hajek-2022] One 2011 model has a wagerer prefer wagering outright to any mixed strategy, if the chance of God is not infinitesimal.[^herzberg-2011] A 2020 model built on another number system gives the same ranking, though its authors say they are less interested in fidelity to Pascal's argument.[^chen-rubio-2020] Oppy writes that the 2007 recasting "immediately disposes of the standard 'mixed strategies' objection", which survives if the chance of God may be infinitesimal.[^oppy-2018] The 2019 paper calls both objections "formal, but not substantive" problems for the wager.[^jackson-rogers-2019]
- James objected that faith "adopted wilfully after such a mechanical calculation would lack the inner soul of faith's reality". In the Deity's place, he wrote, "we should probably take particular pleasure in cutting off believers of this pattern from their infinite reward".[^james-1896] Hájek puts it more weakly: God might not reward belief taken up for such reasons.[^hajek-2003] One reply holds that the pleasure of salvation is "of the most exalted kind". If seeking it counts as greed at all, it is "a noble greed that is to be acclaimed".[^hajek-2022]

# Limits
- The argument stands or falls on two premises the wager fragment does not state, and which of them decides it is open. The first is that among courses of action that each carry some chance of the infinite gain, doing nothing included, the one that makes it likelier is preferred. Without it the mixed strategies objection holds.[^hajek-2003] On Oppy's reading and in the 2011 model, the preference holds for anyone whose chance of God is not infinitesimal.[^oppy-2018][^herzberg-2011] Granting such a repair, this premise turns on whether a rational degree of belief in God may be infinitesimal. Whether a repaired value still makes salvation the best possible good is Hájek's open dilemma.[^hajek-2003] The second premise is that the person gives no rival with the same promise, such as Islam, as high a chance as the Christian God. The wager cannot supply that input.[^hajek-2022] It turns first on whether a decision may leave out a rival the person gives some chance but feels no pull toward, as Jordan's restriction to live hypotheses requires.[^jordan-2018][^jordan-2022] Failing that, it turns on the evidence for each revelation.
- The modern reading needs a positive, finite degree of belief in God, not merely that God is possible. Someone who gives God zero, or no degree of belief at all, stands outside the argument.[^hajek-2022]
- No study was found that has non-believers take up a tradition's practices and then measures belief in God. Davis assumes the route works.[^davis-1991] If striving fails to produce belief, a follower of Pascal "might reply" that genuine striving "already displays a pureness of heart that God would fully reward".[^hajek-2022] A 2024 replication of the classic dissonance experiment across 39 laboratories found no effect of choice on attitude. Writing an essay against one's own view still moved attitudes more than writing a neutral essay. The study measured attitudes, not religious belief.[^vaidis-2024]
- On the [[Jansenism|Jansenist]] theology Pascal held, no human effort could contribute to salvation "even as a partial cause". That view "seemed to make human effort redundant", so the payoff may not depend on the wager at all.[^clarke-wood-2015]
