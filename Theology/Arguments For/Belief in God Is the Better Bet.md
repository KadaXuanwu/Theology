# Description
Belief in God is the better bet even when reason cannot settle whether he exists, because on the wager Blaise Pascal set out, the possible gain is infinite and the possible loss is small.

Blaise Pascal, the 17th century French mathematician and philosopher, lays out the wager in the fragment of the *Pensées* headed "Infini rien", known by its place in the standard editions as Lafuma 418 and Brunschvicg 233.[^pascal-1670] Reason alone, he argues, cannot decide whether God exists, so the choice has to be made the way a bet is made rather than the way a proof is settled. Wager that God exists, and he does: the gain is an eternity of happiness. Wager that he exists, and he does not: the loss is whatever the wagering life cost, which is little next to what was at stake. Wager against him, and he exists: the same infinite good is forfeited. Wager against him, and he does not: the gain is only what an irreligious life afforded anyway.[^hajek-2022] Because any probability above zero, multiplied by an infinite payoff, still comes to infinity, and no finite payoff can outweigh it, wagering for God carries the higher expected value at every probability above zero. The argument therefore needs no estimate of how likely God's existence is, only that it is not impossible.[^hajek-2022] Alan Hájek's Stanford Encyclopedia entry reconstructs the passage into several distinct versions of this kind; the one that needs only a positive probability, not a specific estimate, is the version usually meant by "the" wager.[^hajek-2022]

Pascal anticipates the objection that a person cannot simply decide to believe. His answer is to prescribe practice ahead of belief: act as a believer acts, take part in the same rites, taking holy water and having Masses said, and let the habit work on the passions that argument alone could not move. He expects belief to follow the practice rather than the practice to follow belief, and he concedes plainly that the process will dull the sharpness of mind that was resisting belief in the first place.[^hajek-2022]

# Countered By
- The many gods objection. Nothing in the decision itself picks out the Christian God over any other possible one: the same reasoning could recommend a different religion just as well to someone raised in it, and the same construction can generate as many rival gods, each promising an infinite payoff for a different behaviour, as anyone cares to invent, which by itself stalls the calculation.[^hajek-2022] [[Experiential Confirmation Doesn't Distinguish Religions]] makes the same structural point about a different method: a method that confirms every religion its own believers try it on cannot be what recommends one over the rest.
- [[Belief Isn't Directly Chosen]]: nobody can simply decide to believe. Pascal's own answer already grants this and works within it, prescribing practice rather than direct choice. What that leaves open is whether the practice reliably produces the belief Pascal promises, or only its outward behaviour, which his fragment does not itself settle.
- [[The Threat of Hell Compels the People Who Believe It]]: wagering for God to avoid the wager's own infinite loss is a choice made to avoid a threatened penalty, which that node argues is coerced rather than free.
- The moral objection. The wager's framing has been called out of keeping with so grave a subject since the eighteenth century, and the objection has since sharpened into the charge that a God who hands out infinite reward for self interested belief is rewarding the wrong thing, and belief taken up for the payoff is not the faith Christianity itself asks for.[^hajek-2022]
- Hájek's own mixed strategies objection is the strongest of these, because it attacks the argument's validity rather than one of its premises. Any strategy with a positive chance of ending in belief, not only wagering for God outright but also, for instance, tossing a coin and wagering for God on heads, carries the same infinite expected value as wagering for God straight out. Once that is granted, expected value no longer recommends Christian belief over any of the indefinitely many mixed strategies that share the same positive chance of arriving there. Hájek has proposed refinements built on utility ratios and relative expectations to try to restore a preference for wagering outright, but whether they succeed is not settled.[^hajek-2003][^hajek-2022]

# Limits
- The decision has to isolate exactly one wager, on the Christian God, with a unique infinite payoff, against every rival, for the arithmetic to recommend anything in particular. The many gods objection and the mixed strategies objection both deny that the options divide that cleanly, and Pascal's fragment supplies no principled way to narrow the field back down to the Christian wager alone. Settling this needs a way to run the calculation over a single, well defined partition of options, which is what both objections say is missing.
- It reaches prudence, not truth. Even where the calculation goes through, it gives a reason to want God to exist, not a reason to think he does.
