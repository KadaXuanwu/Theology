# Description
The Gospel writers knew Israel's scriptures and told events of Jesus's life in their words. A match between a Gospel scene and a scripture arose in one of four ways, often mixed in one scene. A detail was drawn from the text, or an event was told because the text had it. A verse was found and fitted to a story already told, or the act itself was arranged after the text. In each the match was made by someone who already held the text, so it is not independent evidence that the scripture was fulfilled. That limits the match as evidence and says nothing about whether the event happened.

That the [[Passion narrative|passion story]] is told in scriptural language is common ground in the studies read here.[^goodacre-2006][^vorster-1993] The step from "made by someone who held the text" to "not independent evidence" is this node's own reasoning.

# Origins
- [[David Friedrich Strauss]] made a scriptural pattern a mark of myth in *The Life of Jesus Critically Examined*, first published in German in 1835.[^strauss-1892][^vette-2020] He added that each such test alone "will rarely prove more than the possible or probable unhistorical character of the record" (p. 89).[^strauss-1892]
- [[Martin Dibelius]] held in *Die Formgeschichte des Evangeliums* (1919) that where eyewitness memory failed, the Old Testament had to help out as the oldest passion gospel.[^dibelius-1919] The 1933 edition says that with Judas's thirty silver pieces the Bible passages produced history. It adds that it would be wrong to say so of every event (p. 188).[^dibelius-1933]
- [[John Dominic Crossan]]'s name for the strong form is "prophecy historicized". In a lecture given in 1993 and printed in 1994 he said the detailed passion accounts "are not history memorized but prophecy historicized".[^crossan-1994] [[Mark Goodacre]] answered in 2006 that the choice is "simply too stark". He proposed "scripturalization, the retelling of traditional materials in the light of the Scriptures".[^goodacre-2006]

# Based On
- **The passion is told in the psalms' words.** A 1993 study finds Psalms 22, 38 and 69 so "knitted into" Mark's passion story that it "is narrated in the language of the Old Testament".[^vorster-1993] A 2026 German study describes the passion narratives as woven through with psalm quotations.[^dahlke-2026] [[Raymond E. Brown|Raymond Brown]] wrote that of the details the first followers knew, "what is preserved in the narrative is mostly what echoes Scripture".[^brown-1994]
- **The Gospels place the insight after the events.** [John 12:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2012:16&version=NIV) and [Luke 24:44–47](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2024:44-47&version=NIV) put the disciples' understanding of scripture after the events. That is the writers' own account of the order. It shows a fit seen late, not which came first.
- **A detail drawn from the text.** [Mark 11:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2011:1-7&version=NIV) has one animal at the entry and [Matthew 21:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2021:1-7&version=NIV) has two. [[Maarten Menken]] puts the she-donkey down to Matthew's editing, "on account of the quotation from Zech 9,9" (p. 310).[^menken-2002] He finds that Matthew also turned the money promised in [Mark 14:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2014:11&version=NIV) into thirty silver pieces ([Matthew 26:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026:15&version=NIV)) under the influence of [Zechariah 11:12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah%2011:12&version=NIV).[^menken-2002] A 2020 thesis on Mark holds that the sour wine of [Mark 15:36](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2015:36&version=NIV) "appears to have been created solely on the basis of" the [[Septuagint|Greek]] of [Psalm 69:21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2069:21&version=NIV). It derives the darkness at noon from [Amos 8:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Amos%208:9&version=NIV).[^vette-2020] A 2016 book by [[Robert J. Miller]] calls three details of John's execution scene "almost certainly narrative fictions, created by John out of the very scriptures they fulfill" (p. 195). They are the lots for the clothing, the unbroken legs and the spear thrust.[^miller-2016]
- **An event told because the text had it.** Dibelius judged it likely that the guards divided Jesus's clothes, since that was their recognised right. He held that it was told because Psalm 22 had it (p. 33).[^dibelius-lecture-1933] No historian of Roman practice was read on that right.
- **A verse found for a story already told.** Menken finds that Matthew's [[Fulfilment quotation|fulfilment quotations]], the one at [Matthew 27:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2027:9-10&version=NIV) apart, "have obviously been appended to existing narratives" (p. 309).[^menken-2002] The citation at [Matthew 2:23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%202:23&version=NIV) "is not to be found anywhere" in the Old Testament, a 2018 study says.[^august-2018]
- **An act arranged after the text.** In Mark 11:1–7 Jesus sends for the animal himself. A 1993 book on Jesus finds it possible "either that the prophecy created the event or that the prophecy created the story and that the event never occurred". Its author writes: "I incline to the view that it was Jesus himself who read the prophecy and decided to fulfil it".[^sanders-1993]

# Carries
- Where a detail was drawn from the text, the Gospel took material from the scripture. [[Later Biblical Writers Drew on Earlier Ones]] holds that the two are then not independent witnesses for that material.
- That rule does not by itself cover a verse fitted to a story, an event picked for telling or an act Jesus arranged. For those this node reasons one step further: whoever chose, edited or arranged the match already held the text, so the match cannot test it independently.
- The claim covers a match made in a Gospel's own text, by quotation or by wording, and it bears on fulfilment in the sense of a forecast come true. A 2008 study finds that Matthew's fulfilment quotations "are not bound to prophetic predictions".[^kirk-2008] Miller's book weighs the ten passages John quotes as fulfilled. It counts [Zechariah 9:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah%209:9&version=NIV) the only one that "can safely be considered a messianic prophecy". Of the ride it says "there is nothing particularly messianic about riding a donkey" (pp. 198–199).[^miller-2016]

# Disputed By
- **The verse was fitted to the story.** This is the strong objection. It holds that where the direction can be tested, the text was bent to the story. At [Matthew 27:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2027:9-10&version=NIV) Menken finds "a strong influence of the narrative on the quotation, and a superficial influence of the quotation on the narrative" (p. 326). In the Judas story older than Matthew "no influence of Zech 11,12-13 can be detected" (p. 323).[^menken-2002] Brown says four of the five such quotations in Matthew's infancy story "have a definite air of being appended".[^brown-1993] A 1983 study of the passion concludes that "in general, the influence has proceeded from the history to the text rather than vice versa".[^moo-1983] Its author held the "fabrication of incidents on the basis of Scriptures" unlikely, and the 2020 thesis reports him so (p. 11).[^moo-1983][^vette-2020] He reports finding no case where creation from the text was the most probable explanation.[^moo-1983] On these findings a story shaped from its quoted verse is the exception. Menken's and Brown's findings reach quoted verses only. Under the same stories they find scriptural models that Matthew does not quote. Behind Judas stands Ahithophel's betrayal of David, and behind the infancy story the patriarch Joseph and the birth legends of Moses.[^menken-2002][^brown-1993]
- **Scriptural wording does not show where a detail came from.** A 2010 study agrees with Goodacre that a story's debt to scripture "does not, in and of itself, tell us whether it has an anchor in history".[^allison-2010] The 2020 thesis says that showing a detail owes to scripture "is not the same as showing it does not owe to historical memory". It adds, "though in some cases it may make that conclusion inevitable" (p. 219).[^vette-2020] The same thesis holds that scholars can judge when Mark depends on the scriptures for its content, "but not when they depend on historical memory" (p. 176).[^vette-2020]
- **Jesus read the scriptures of himself.** A 1998 essay holds that "Zechariah is clearly of great significance" for Jesus's self-understanding, and offers that account "as a historical hypothesis".[^wright-1998] This moves the shaping from the writers to Jesus.

# Limits
- The claim rests on a step no source read makes for the Gospels: a match made by someone holding the scripture is not independent evidence of its fulfilment. "Not independent" does not mean "of no weight", and no source read measures what weight is left. The nearest reasoning found is a reference entry on prediction and accommodation in science. It reports the worry that a theorist who "built her theory to accommodate data" has "overfit the data".[^barnes-2022] There a theory is fitted to data, and here the report was written by people who held the text. No application of that work to prophecy was found. One in print would settle whether the step can be cited.
- The claim is no finding about any event. Crossan calls the crucifixion "as historically certain as anything in the world can ever be".[^crossan-1994] The 2020 thesis holds that with the scriptural material taken out of Mark 14–15 "a coherent narrative still exists" (p. 176). Of the crucifixion scene it says "the bulk of the narrative remains", and its list includes Simon of Cyrene, Golgotha and the inscription (p. 222).[^vette-2020]
- Which way a match arose is settled scene by scene, and verdicts differ. On the sour wine Brown judged that the difficulty of relating Mark and Luke to the psalm "suggests that the scene was not invented through reflection on that psalm".[^brown-1994] He adds that the offering was kept or brought into the early tradition because it showed Jesus mocked as the psalm's sufferer was.[^brown-1994] A 2012 study that derives the second animal from Zechariah reports the contrary view that two animals stood in the tradition.[^coppins-2012]
- The second animal and the thirty pieces are found by setting Matthew beside Mark. They hold on Matthew's use of Mark, the majority solution to the [[Synoptic Problem]], which [[Later Biblical Writers Drew on Earlier Ones]] sets out.
- For an allusion a Gospel does not mark, the claim holds only if the allusion is real, and resemblance alone does not show use.[^sandmel-1962]
- The studies read cover the passion, the entry, the Judas story and Matthew's infancy chapters. Of the view that virtually all Gospel narrative was made from earlier scripture, the 2020 thesis says "Few scholars would go this far".[^vette-2020]
- The academic case that the story came before the verse rests here in part on fragments of three books. They cover the passion in general, the sour wine and the infancy stories.[^moo-1983][^brown-1994][^brown-1993] For the infancy stories the case that the events are historical was read in full only in a commentary for InterVarsity Press, a confessional house. There [[Craig S. Keener]] argues that Matthew "chose the Scripture texts to fit the stories".[^keener-1997]
- The claim concerns evidence, not meaning. [[Richard B. Hays|Richard Hays]] judged that Luke gave the dying Jesus "words from a different psalm" in [Luke 23:46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2023:46&version=NIV), retelling the story "as an act of theological interpretation".[^hays-2019]
