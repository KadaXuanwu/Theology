# Description
The books at the centre of the Bible were in wide use as scripture by the end of the second century.[^irenaeus][^cosgrove-2000][^wimmer-2012][^kruger-2012] The books at its edges stayed in dispute for centuries and still differ between churches.[^kruger-2012][^lim-2022] Use as scripture means being cited as authoritative; the New Testament's centre is the four Gospels and Paul's main letters, cited most often for their length.[^kruger-2012][^cosgrove-2000]

# Origins
[[John Barton]]'s *Holy Writings, Sacred Text* (Westminster John Knox, 1998) tells the [[Canon|canon]] as two independent stories.[^cosgrove-2000] One is "the growth and acceptance of various Christian writings as possessing authority", the other "Christian attempts to list and define which books should count as Scripture".[^barton-1998] For the first he draws on a 1988 statistical study of Bible citations in Christian writers up to [[Eusebius]].[^cosgrove-2000][^stuhlhofer-1988] [[Michael J. Kruger]] wrote in 2012 of "widespread agreement about the core canonical books, but some disagreement over the peripheral books was inevitable".[^kruger-2012] A 2010 Oxford monograph argues that the four Gospels were widely used and authoritative by the end of the first century or the start of the second.[^wimmer-2012] A 2012 review says it "rather convincingly shows" this, though the book's second half "might seem more speculative".[^wimmer-2012]

# Based On
- **The Old Testament in Christian use.** The Old Testament belongs to the centre on other ground: it was already scripture, and second-century Christian writers read it as speaking of Christ.[^eusebius-he4][^irenaeus] By the count of the United Bible Societies' Greek New Testament, the New Testament quotes the prophetic books 84 times.[^moyise-2022] A bishop of Sardis compiled "extracts made from the Law and the Prophets concerning the Saviour".[^eusebius-he4] His Old Testament list, from about 190, lacks Esther.[^eusebius-he4][^lim-2022] [[Irenaeus]] of Lyon, writing about 180 to 185, read Christ as "the treasure hid in the Scriptures".[^van-oort-2013][^irenaeus]
- **The New Testament core.** Irenaeus defended four Gospels as the only ones, neither more nor fewer.[^irenaeus] Kruger reports the 1988 counts: the core New Testament books "were used substantially more often (in proportion to their size) than non-canonical books (and even the Old Testament)".[^kruger-2012] Barton's core, on the same counts, is Matthew, Mark, Luke, John and Paul's main letters, cited far more often than any other writings.[^cosgrove-2000]
- **The edges moved both ways.** Kruger writes that some books, the Shepherd of Hermas and the Apocalypse of Peter among his examples, were "'canonical' in the eyes of certain communities". They never joined "the church's permanent collection".[^kruger-2012] Eusebius's *Church History*, as finished about 325, sorts the writings into accepted, disputed and rejected.[^barnes-1980][^eusebius-he3] James, Jude, 2 Peter and 2 and 3 John are disputed, though recognised by many. The Shepherd and the Apocalypse of Peter are rejected.[^eusebius-he3]
- **The fourth-century lists.** An Easter letter of the bishop of Alexandria for 367 is the earliest surviving Christian list of exactly the 27 books.[^brakke-1994] Its Old Testament omits Esther and counts Baruch with Jeremiah. Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit and the Shepherd stay outside its canon, to be read by new converts.[^athanasius-fl39] A list agreed at the Council of Hippo in 393 and read at the Council of Carthage in 397 includes Tobit, Judith and two books of Maccabees.[^carthage-397] So does [[Augustine]].[^augustine-doctrina]
- **Still different.** The Council of Trent in 1546 counted Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch and two books of Maccabees as Scripture.[^trent-1546] It pronounced anathema on anyone who does not receive the books on its list "entire with all their parts".[^trent-1546] The Westminster Confession, which the Orthodox Presbyterian Church keeps among its standards, says the [[Apocrypha]] "are no part of the canon of the Scripture".[^westminster-confession] The Protestant Old Testament has the same books as the Jewish Bible. Catholics and Orthodox "have their own arrangements that include other books in their canons".[^lim-2022] The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church "is satisfied with the tradition of eighty-one canonical books", a number "neither unambiguous nor definitive".[^asale-2016]

# Carries
- It licenses resting a case from the New Testament's authority on the early use of the four Gospels and Paul's main letters.[^van-oort-2013][^irenaeus][^cosgrove-2000] It gives no such footing to the books still disputed about 325.[^eusebius-he3]
- Any claim about "the Bible" as a whole has to say which church's list it means.[^lim-2022]

# Disputed By
- **The closed list definition is the strong counter.** The date given for a canon turns on the definition: the fourth century for a closed list, about 200 for writings regarded as scripture, about 100 for authoritative ones.[^wimmer-2012] [[Albert C. Sundberg]] proposed in 1968 that a canon is a fixed, final, closed list.[^kruger-2012] On that definition, calling anything in the second or third century a canon is anachronistic.[^kruger-2012] [[Eugene Ulrich]] defines canon as "the definitive list of inspired, authoritative books", the result of "inclusive and exclusive decisions after serious deliberation".[^ulrich-2002] On that definition, he writes, "talk of an open canon is confusing and counterproductive"; he would speak instead of "a growing collection of books considered as sacred scripture".[^ulrich-2002] Kruger reports that it "has begun to emerge as the more dominant one", "at least in some circles", with [[James Barr]] among its early supporters.[^kruger-2012] Barton, Kruger writes, "still seems to prefer" it.[^kruger-2012] On this view, in a phrase Kruger quotes from one holder, Christians before the fourth century had only a "boundless, living mass of heterogenous" texts.[^kruger-2012] Kruger writes that the definition captures the canon's "fluid" edges before the fourth century. He is "hard pressed to find" a formal act closing the canon before the Council of Trent.[^kruger-2012] The claim takes the definition's wording and speaks of use. Whether Paul's letters stood with the Gospels as one recognised set before the fourth-century lists stays open.
- **The fourfold Gospel as chosen.** [[Francis Watson]] writes in *The Cambridge Companion to the Gospels* (2006) that "the fourfoldness of the church's canonical gospel is not a natural phenomenon".[^watson-2006] It "was established by the collective decision of the most influential Christian communities of both east and west in the latter half of the second century".[^watson-2006] Others hold that the "Great Church" imposed a fourfold Gospel and that Irenaeus created the New Testament canon.[^wimmer-2012] The 2010 monograph argues that Irenaeus had predecessors and that the four were authoritative Scripture across the Empire by the end of the second century.[^wimmer-2012] The reading disputes who made the four authoritative, and whether before Irenaeus. It leaves their use by his time standing.

# Limits
- The claim rests on one finding. From the earliest writers after the New Testament on, Christians cited the Gospels and Paul's main letters far more often, for their length, than other writings. For the Gospels from Irenaeus on, and for all the writings about 325, the record shows the split directly.[^wimmer-2012][^eusebius-he3] Before Irenaeus, and for Paul's letters, it rests on the 1988 counts as Kruger and Barton report them.[^kruger-2012][^cosgrove-2000][^stuhlhofer-1988] Neither report says which of Paul's letters the counts cover. The study's tables or Barton's pages would settle it. If they showed Hermas or Barnabas cited in the second century nearly as often for their length, "core" would be a label taken from the later lists.
- It does not say whether the churches recognised these books or chose them. Kruger holds that the books "do not become canonical simply by the actions of the church".[^kruger-2012] The chosen reading holds the opposite for the Gospels.[^watson-2006] Early use and late lists fit both readings.
- It says nothing about whether the process was guided, which church's list is right, or when the Jewish canon closed.
- It concerns which books counted, not the wording of each. [[Jews Used Several Forms of the Hebrew Bible Text Before One Remained]] takes up the wording of the Hebrew books.
- Kruger places a "general consensus on the boundaries of the canon" in the fourth century, with "pockets of the church that still had differing views".[^kruger-2012] The Catholic list was bound under anathema only in 1546.[^trent-1546][^kruger-2012] For the Ethiopian church a canon "is not a list of books with nothing to be added or removed".[^asale-2016]
- In the first four centuries the Syriac churches mostly read the Gospels as one harmony of the four.[^petersen-2011]
- The closed list view is known here through Kruger, who argues against using it alone, and through Ulrich's quoted definition. Before Irenaeus, the evidence against it rests on reports of the 1988 counts and on reviews. The chosen reading is stated here as quoted in a review of a book that argues against it.
