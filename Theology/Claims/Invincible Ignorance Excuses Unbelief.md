# Description
Ignorance of the gospel that a person could not have overcome excuses their unbelief, so they are not damned for it. Catholic teaching has held this in its magisterium since 1854 and the Second Vatican Council extended it to anyone who seeks God sincerely and follows their conscience. The category reaches further than people who never heard: on the tradition's own reasoning [[Invincible ignorance|invincible ignorance]] can survive a hearing, where the hearing was inadequate.

# Origins
[[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] supplies the definition. Ignorance is invincible "because it obviously cannot be overcome even by effort", and "invincible ignorance is never a sin".[^aquinas-st-i-ii-76] He treats the unbelief of those who have heard nothing as a punishment rather than a sin, while holding that such people are damned for their other sins.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-10] On the never heard and salvation he writes of someone "brought up in the woods or among brute animals" that God "would either reveal to him by an internal inspiration the things which are necessarily to be believed, or would direct some preacher of the faith to him".[^aquinas-de-veritate]

[[Stephen Bullivant]] dates the entry into magisterial teaching. Pius IX's allocution *Singulari quadam* of 1854 "introduced the Thomistic principle of invincible ignorance into the Church's magisterial teaching on salvation", holding "it is to be held for certain that they who labor in ignorance of the true religion, if this ignorance is invincible, are not bound by any fault in this matter in the eyes of the Lord".[^bullivant-2011] The Holy Office repeated it in *Suprema haec sacra*, its 1949 letter to Archbishop Cushing of Boston, which is the text footnoted in *Lumen Gentium* 16.[^bullivant-2011]

# Based On
- *Lumen Gentium* 16 (1964) and the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* at §847 and §1260, which say someone who does not know the gospel through no fault of their own can be saved.[^lumen-gentium][^catechism]
- [[Francisco de Vitoria]], *De Indis* (1539), on hearers who were told and not persuaded: they "are not bound to believe from the first announcement of the Christian faith, in the sense of sinning mortally by not believing due to this alone: because it is merely announced and proposed to them ... without miracles or any other proofs or arguments".[^bullivant-2011]
- Las Casas extends it to conduct. A great many unbelievers are excused "so long as they see the extremely corrupt and detestable conduct of the Christians".[^bullivant-2011]
- [[Karl Rahner|Rahner]]'s criterion, which Bullivant adopts: what matters is not having heard, nor having heard a plausible presentation, but having been "existentially confronted" with the gospel.[^bullivant-2011]
- *Gaudium et spes* 19 (1965) states the split from the other side. Those who wilfully drive God from their heart "are not devoid of fault", and believers themselves "often bear a certain responsibility for this".[^bullivant-2011]

# Carries
- The unevangelised are not damned for unbelief. Aquinas already says any damnation is for other sins.[^aquinas-st-ii-ii-10]
- Bullivant reads three grounds out of Vitoria and Las Casas on which invincible ignorance can persist after someone has been evangelised: an intrinsically inadequate proclamation, social factors undermining its authority, and the misconduct of Christians, which "may so defame Christianity as to prolong invincible ignorance over a long period of time, and perhaps indefinitely".[^bullivant-2011]
- The structure the tradition actually has is two boxes and not three. An adequate presentation obliges assent under pain of mortal sin; an inadequate one leaves the ignorance standing.[^bullivant-2011] There is no box for a hearer who got an adequate presentation and was honestly not convinced. The tradition widens ignorance rather than granting a category of blameless examined unbelief, which is where this meets [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]].

# Disputed By
- It is Catholic, not Christian. Westminster Confession 10.4 denies salvation to the non-elect who are called by the ministry of the Word, and then to non-Christians "be they never so diligent to frame their lives according to the light of nature", calling the contrary opinion "very pernicious, and to be detested".[^schaff-creeds] The Reformed tradition has no category answering to invincible ignorance. What it has is 10.3, which saves the elect "who are incapable of being outwardly called by the ministry of the Word", a fact about God's decree rather than about the person's knowledge or diligence.[^schaff-creeds]
- Pius IX is on both sides within a decade. His *Syllabus errorum* of 1864 condemns the opinion that "Good hope at least is to be considered regarding the eternal salvation of all those who are not in the true Church of Christ".[^bullivant-2011]
- *Lumen Gentium* 16 does not end where its optimistic reading ends. Its closing sentences say that men "have become vain in their reasonings", and that some, "living and dying in this world without God, are exposed to final despair". [[Nicholas J. Healy Jr.|Healy]] notes these have not had the attention they deserve in most commentaries on the council.[^healy-2015]
- [[Ralph Martin]] argues the conditions *Lumen Gentium* 16 sets are strict and very often unmet, so the optimistic reading over-reads the text. Healy answers him from de Lubac and Ratzinger.[^healy-2015]
- Bullivant's own constructive conclusion, that in a plural society perhaps an overwhelming majority are never existentially confronted, runs on [[Peter Berger|Berger]]'s plausibility structures at a deterministic strength Berger gave up in 1999. The secularisation thesis it needs has been the central dispute in the sociology of religion for fifty years, and Bullivant engages none of that critique.[^pollack-2014]

# Limits
- It says who is excused, not that condemning the unexcused would be just. Nothing in the tradition traced here defends the justice of the penalty; it narrows who falls under it.
- Vitoria's exception turns on the quality of the presentation, not on the honesty of the hearer.[^bullivant-2011] A hearer who got a good presentation and remained unconvinced is not covered by it.
- How many people fall inside the categories is not something theologians can judge. Pius IX asks "who would arrogate so much to himself, as to be able to designate the limits of this kind of ignorance", and Bullivant treats that as a standing caution.[^bullivant-2011]
- The categories are not empirically checkable. Adequacy of proclamation, existential confrontation and secret election are none of them things a survey reaches.
- The sourcing runs through one historian. Bullivant 2011 carries the Vitoria, Las Casas, Pius IX, Rahner and Ratzinger material in translation, and the primary texts behind him were not opened separately. [[Francis A. Sullivan]]'s *Salvation Outside the Church?* (Paulist, 1992) is the other standard history and was reached only through Bullivant's citations of it.
