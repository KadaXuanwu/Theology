# Description
The servant of [Isaiah 52:13–53:12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2052:13-53:12&version=NIV) is the people Israel, spoken of as one man. Isaiah 40–55 calls Israel "my servant" by name, and the poem says "my servant" and names no one, so the reader carries the name over.[^driver-neubauer-1877][^budde-1899] The reading reaches the whole people only if the nations and kings of [52:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2052:15&version=NIV) speak the confession of [53:1–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2053:1-6&version=NIV). Readings now in print divide among the whole people, a faithful part of Israel and one man who bears Israel's name.[^collins-2014][^berges-2012-song][^shepherd-2012] A 2020 dissertation on the [[Servant songs|servant poems]] finds that the question has stayed without an answer able to command consensus (pp. 7–8).[^zeilmann-2020]

# Origins
- The earliest report found is a Christian's. [[Origen]], probably in the late 240s, writes that a Jewish opponent applied the poem to "the whole people, regarded as one individual".[^origen-celsus][^paget-2017]
- [[Rashi]], who died in 1105, reads the poem so in his own commentary: the prophet "mentions all Israel as one man". His gloss on 52:13 is narrower, "My servant, Jacob, [i.e.,] the righteous among him". At 53:1 he writes, "So will the nations say to one another".[^rashi][^jacobs-1905]
- [[David Kimhi]] writes at 52:13 that "my servant" is Israel, as at 41:8. He gives the confession to the nations.[^kimhi]
- [[Ulrich Berges]] writes that in Jewish tradition the servant texts "referred, for the most part, collectively to Israel" (p. 488).[^berges-2012-song] A messianic reading runs beside that one. The [[Targum]] renders 52:13 "my servant the messiah", and the [[Talmud]] applies 53:4 to the [[Messiah|messiah]].[^targum-jonathan][^sanhedrin-98]
- [[Samuel David Luzzatto]] states the step from the name. The prophet says "Israel my servant" elsewhere, "so here it is plain that he is referring likewise to Israel" (p. 412).[^driver-neubauer-1877]
- [[Karl Budde]] argued it in *The American Journal of Theology* in 1899. "In reality the speakers, the 'we' of vss. 1 ff., are the heathen nations; the servant of Yahweh of whom they speak is the people of Israel" (p. 505).[^budde-1899] A 1905 reference article reports that other scholars agreed with him.[^hirsch-1905]
- [[John J. Collins]] holds it in a 2014 textbook: "The explanation that requires least hypothetical speculation is that the servant is Israel, described metaphorically as an individual". On that reading, he writes, "the speakers are the kings".[^collins-2014]

# Based On
- **The name.** A reference article counts the word "servant" 20 times in Isaiah 40–55 and finds that "In 13 of these instances the servant is Israel the people".[^ej-isaiah] In the Jewish Publication Society's translation, quoted throughout, [41:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2041:8&version=NIV) reads "you, Israel, My servant". [44:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2044:1&version=NIV), [44:21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2044:21&version=NIV) and [45:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2045:4&version=NIV) name Jacob or Israel in the same way.[^jps-1985]
- **Isaiah 49:3.** Inside one of the four servant poems the speaker is told, "You are My servant, Israel in whom I glory".[^jps-1985] The word "Israel" stands there in the [[Great Isaiah Scroll]] and the [[Septuagint]].[^flint-ulrich][^nets-2007] Budde knew a single manuscript without it and held that the verse "would settle the case" (p. 518).[^budde-1899]
- **The poem.** It calls its figure "My servant" at 52:13 and "My righteous servant" at 53:11 and gives no name.[^jps-1985] The Hebrew noun messiah is not in it. A 1906 lexicon lists one use of the noun in Isaiah, of Cyrus at [45:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2045:1&version=NIV).[^bdb-1906]
- **The speakers.** At 52:15 kings "shall see what has not been told them". Isaiah 53:1 goes on with "Who can believe what we have heard?" and names no speaker.[^jps-1985] On the claim the kings and nations speak on, and "my people" at 53:8 is each nation's own. In Rashi's comment the nations say that "because of the transgression of my people, this plague came to the righteous among them".[^rashi]
- **The servant's innocence.** Budde takes it as the nations' view. In their conscience "the only worshiper of the true God appears as the only innocent one" (p. 509).[^budde-1899]

# Carries
- On the claim the poem is about a people in exile, and its servant is no single figure to come. Collins writes that in the exile Israel "might even be said to have died", and he compares Ezekiel's valley of dry bones.[^collins-2014]
- On that explanation, Collins writes, the prophet explains the exile as "vicarious punishment for the sins of other peoples".[^collins-2014] Kimhi denies that Israel bore the nations' guilt: the confession is what the nations will think.[^kimhi] On Kimhi's form no guilt passes from the nations to Israel. The transfer in 53:5–6, the language [[The Penalty Falls on a Substitute]] draws on, is only what the nations suppose.

# Disputed By
- **The poem sets its servant over against a people.** This is the strong objection. The servant is cut off for the sin of "my people" (53:8). He "had done no injustice" ([53:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2053:9&version=NIV)), where [42:19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2042:19&version=NIV) asks "Who is so blind as My servant" and [43:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2043:24&version=NIV) tells Jacob "you have burdened Me with your sins".[^jps-1985] In the poem that names him Israel he has a task toward Jacob ([49:5–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2049:5-6&version=NIV)). Origen answered his opponent from 53:8.[^origen-celsus] A 2024 study reports "his people" at 53:8 in the Great Isaiah Scroll, in letters probably added later.[^buttner-2024] [[Bernhard Duhm]], in a commentary first published in 1892, separated four poems (42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12) from the book, on this ground among others. Elsewhere, he wrote, the servant is Israel as it is, blind, deaf and full of sins. The hero of the poems stands over against the people and is innocent. He struck "Israel" from 49:3 as an insertion and read one man, a teacher of the Torah.[^duhm-1902] Berges holds that the kings cannot be the speakers. They "have shut their mouths in amazement (52.15)", and they "cannot say that they have suffered misfortune 'because of the sin of my people' (53.8)" (pp. 67–68).[^berges-2012-book]
- **Answers given.** Budde writes that "my people" "admits of no explanation whatever" and proposes to read "for our transgressions" (pp. 510–511). At 49:5–6 he makes God the one who brings Jacob back and raises up the tribes (pp. 517–522), as the Jewish Publication Society's translation does.[^budde-1899][^jps-1985] A reference article reports the reply to Duhm that "any distinction between these units and their contexts is somewhat arbitrary".[^ej-isaiah]
- **A faithful part of Israel.** Berges reads neither the nation nor one man. The poem "actually refers to the group of returnees or rather a part thereof", who suffer "on behalf of all the people" (pp. 488–495).[^berges-2012-song] In Rashi the nations speak and the righteous of Israel suffer. In Berges Israelites speak and a part of Israel suffers for the rest.
- **One man who bears the name.** A twelfth century commentary explains the chapter "on the supposition that it refers to Israel" and then gives its author's own view, that the servant is the prophet.[^ibn-ezra-1873][^gottheil-1904] A 2001 commentary holds that "the confessing 'we' of the Old Testament is always Israel and not the nations". A 2012 study sums up its view of the name: the commission "is transferred to an individual in 49.3".[^shepherd-2012] The 2020 dissertation reads an individual prophet and quotes a German commentator's formula: the servant is Israel, but Israel is not the servant (p. 310).[^zeilmann-2020]
- **The comparison with 41:8.** A commentator who reads the Messiah calls the comparison rash: at 41:8 the prophet names Israel, and here he "says simply my servant". He writes that "the commentators agree in explaining" the chapter "of the Captivity of Israel" (pp. 99–100).[^driver-neubauer-1877]

# Limits
- The claim turns on who speaks 53:1–6. If the nations and kings of 52:15 speak, "my people" at 53:8 can be each speaker's own people and the servant can be Israel as a whole. If Israelites speak, the servant stands apart from the people and can only be a faithful part of it or one man. The poem names neither its servant nor its speakers. Two commentaries on these chapters, of 2012 and 2015, were not read for this node.
- "Israel" covers three things here: the nation as it is, the righteous within it and a title one man bears. The naming verses outside the four poems give only the first.
- Who has read the poem so does not show whom its poet meant. A reference article dates the prophet of these chapters to about 540 BC, some sixteen centuries before Rashi.[^ej-isaiah][^jacobs-1905] Origen's report is a Christian's account of a Jewish opponent, not a Jewish text. A tenth century commentator lists how the commentators differed: the prophets or Jeremiah, "the pious of their own sect", and "David and the Messiah". His own reading is the Messiah, and the nation is not on his list (pp. 19–20).[^driver-neubauer-1877][^kohler-1904]
- Rashi and Kimhi do not carry the name through all four poems. Rashi takes the task of 49:6 as the prophet's own, and Kimhi reads 42:1 of the King Messiah.[^rashi][^kimhi]
- The servant of the poet and the servant of the finished book may differ. A 1994 study holds that the fourth poem came from one prophet's disciples and was later reread of the people (p. 139).[^ruppert-1994] The 2020 dissertation concludes that the question of who the servant is has no single answer that always holds (p. 314).[^zeilmann-2020]
- The fullest statement of the whole people reading read in full for this node is Budde's of 1899, and Collins's textbook was read only in part. A reference article reports a "preponderance of scholarly opinion" for the people of Israel, "perhaps" with an individual beside it, and quotes a book of the 1950s for the individual.[^ej-isaiah]
- Whether the poem is fulfilled in a later figure is a question the claim does not reach.
