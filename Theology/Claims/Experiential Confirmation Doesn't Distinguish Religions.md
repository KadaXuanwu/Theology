# Description
"Commit first, and you will know it is true" is a method other religions run in the same form, with the same reported result. If the same procedure produces confident confirmation of incompatible claims, it can produce conviction but cannot sort true from false. Whatever the experience is worth to the person having it, it does not work as an argument to someone outside, because the identical argument is available to the person across the street.

# Origins
Not a religious claim but an epistemological objection to one. The general form goes back to Hume on competing miracle reports; the modern version is usually put as the problem of religious diversity, and John Hick made it central.

# Evidence
- [Moroni 10:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/moro/10?lang=eng) is nearly word for word the same promise for the Book of Mormon: ask with a sincere heart, with real intent, having faith in Christ, and God "will manifest the truth of it unto you". The confirmation is promised on the same conditions and reported as delivered by millions of people whose conclusion contradicts the Christian one.
- The structure repeats outside the Abrahamic religions. Practice-first, verification-after is the standard shape in Buddhist meditative traditions too, with practitioners reporting confirmation of a framework that has no creator in it.
- Failure is explained the same way in every case: insufficient sincerity, insufficient faith, insufficient practice. No outcome is allowed to count as a negative result.

# Supports
- Anything resting on personal experience as its main support needs an answer here first. Applies to [[Personal Relationship with God]] and [[Obedience Comes Before Evidence]].
- It sharpens [[Demanding Belief Without the Proof]]. If the only evidence available is the kind that comes after commitment, and that kind cannot distinguish between religions, then the person deciding which commitment to make has nothing to go on.

# Disputed By
- Needs writing. The obvious reply is that a method being shared does not make it worthless, only insufficient on its own, and that the Christian version claims a relationship with a person rather than a feeling about a proposition. Worth building as a proper node rather than leaving here.

# Limits
- It shows the method cannot arbitrate between religions. It does not show any particular experience is false. Overreaching here would be the mirror of the mistake it is pointing out.
- Someone can accept the whole argument and still hold that their own experience is veridical while others are mistaken. That is not incoherent, it just means the experience is no longer doing the argumentative work.
- The parallel is strongest where the promise is explicit, as in Moroni 10:4. Traditions that make no such promise are not touched by it.
