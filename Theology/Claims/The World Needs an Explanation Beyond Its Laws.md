# Description
That there is anything at all that might not have existed, laws and starting state included, has an explanation. [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]]'s [[Principle of sufficient reason|principle of sufficient reason]] asks of every true fact "a sufficient reason why it is thus and not otherwise".[^leibniz-1714] This claim asks it only of what might have been otherwise. A finished physics would say how things behave once there are things to behave.[^ellis-2007][^carroll-2021] The claim counts the laws themselves among what still needs accounting for, whether they might have been otherwise or rest on the natures of things that need not exist.[^pruss-2009][^carroll-2021]

# Origins
- The Stanford Encyclopedia calls the principle "nearly as old as philosophy itself". It credits Anaximander, the pre-Socratic philosopher, with its first use, on the basis of [[Aristotle]]'s *De caelo*.[^melamed-lin-2023]
- Leibniz was the first to call it by name and to treat it as a major principle of philosophy.[^melamed-lin-2023] In 1697 he applied it to the world: even a world without beginning needs a reason outside the series of its states.[^leibniz-1697]
- Needs sources: what [[Samuel Clarke]] made of the principle in his correspondence with Leibniz. Candidate: H. G. Alexander (ed.), *The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence* (Manchester University Press, 1956), not opened.
- [[Alexander R. Pruss]] defends the principle in *The Principle of Sufficient Reason: A Reassessment* (2006) and "The Leibnizian Cosmological Argument" (2009).[^pruss-2006][^pruss-2009] Pruss and [[Joshua L. Rasmussen]] restrict the first step of their 2018 version to the fact that contingent causal things exist.[^pruss-2018][^oppy-2019]

# Based On
- Pruss writes that people believe the principle mainly because they take it to be "self-evident, obvious, intuitively clear, in no need of argumentative support".[^pruss-2006] He grants that "if we want to convince others, we need arguments".[^pruss-2009]
- He holds that calling something a brute fact "should be a last resort", since doing so where a not implausible explanation can be formulated "would undercut the practice of science".[^pruss-2006]
- In his fifth paper to Clarke, Leibniz argues for the principle inductively. He says there are many cases where a fact has a sufficient reason and none where a fact is known to lack one, so it is reasonable to assume the principle where the reason is not known. He calls this "the method of experimental philosophy, which proceeds a posteriori".[^melamed-lin-2023]
- Fatema Amijee, a philosopher, has argued that anyone trying to explain why a given fact obtains must assume the principle as long as no fact is found to be brute. Since that inquiry is intrinsically valuable, she argues, it is "rationally non-optional", so the commitment is too.[^melamed-lin-2023]
- Needs sources: Amijee's own paper (the Stanford Encyclopedia cites "Amijee 2022"), Pruss's arguments for the principle in the 2006 book by chapter, and a philosopher of science who argues that explanatory practice presupposes the principle.

# Carries
- Pruss's version of the contingency argument opens with the principle in general form: every contingent fact has an explanation. One contingent fact includes all the others, so it has an explanation, and that explanation must involve a [[Necessary being|necessary being]].[^pruss-2009]
- It carries the reply to gap objections. [[Erkki V. R. Kojonen|Kojonen]] reports that reply: on the classical view God is the reason there is anything at all, not the cause of particular unexplained events. That version does not shrink as science advances because it was never occupying a gap (pp. 299–300).[^kojonen-2016]
- [[Michał Heller]]'s view, as one Polish survey reports it, is that every gap in science is temporary except three. The first is why there is anything at all.[^oleksowicz-2014]

# Disputed By
- [[Peter van Inwagen]] argues that if all contingent truths together had an explanation, nothing could have been otherwise.[^vaninwagen-1983][^pruss-2009] Pruss calls this "the main objection", which makes it the strong counter to the claim itself. He answers that a free choice can explain without entailing what it explains.[^pruss-2009]
- A naturalist, [[Graham Oppy]] argues, can stop where the theist stops. The stopping point can be the bare fact that some things exist (p. 769), or a first natural state that every possible world shares (pp. 770–771).[^oppy-2019]
- [[Bertrand Russell]] held that the universe is "just there, and that's all".[^russell-copleston-1948] [[Sean Carroll]] argues that every answer ends in "a feature of reality that has no further explanation", and that we cannot demand a reason we would find satisfying. He leaves open that a deeper principle might explain why this universe exists, with that principle itself then left unexplained.[^carroll-2021]

# Limits
- The claim reaches the whole world, laws and starting state included, only if the principle can hold there without making everything necessary. That is where Pruss and van Inwagen part.[^pruss-2009] Many critics, the Stanford Encyclopedia reports, put the key to the cosmological argument in this premise and in the one that contingent things alone cannot supply the explanation.[^reichenbach-2026]
- The principle is "a family of principles". Its forms differ in what needs a reason and in modal strength: a strong form holds in all possible worlds, a weak one is only contingently true. It can also be held as a fact about the world or only as a regulative principle, a condition for intelligibility that guides the study of nature.[^melamed-lin-2023] This claim says what needs a reason but not how strong the principle is.
- [[Kenneth L. Pearce]], who defends the argument, accepts Oppy's parity for causal explanations and claims an advantage only for God as the ground of the whole, not a cause within it. That, he writes, "will be decisive only if a sufficiently strong Principle of Sufficient Reason is endorsed".[^pearce-2017]
- In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, 35.9% of philosophers who answered on the principle accepted or leaned toward "true" and 45.7% toward "false". The question names no form of the principle.[^bourget-chalmers-2023]
- George Ellis, a cosmologist, argues that physics cannot reach the question.[^ellis-2007]
- The claim says nothing about what supplies the explanation. The argument built on it reaches for a necessary being, with everything else owing its existing to it.[^reichenbach-2026] Pruss writes that "rare is the Christian cosmological arguer who claims to be able to show that the First Cause is a Trinity".[^pruss-2009]
