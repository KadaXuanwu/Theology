# Description
Scripture can be God's word, inspired and authoritative, while a command it reports in God's name is the human writer's portrayal of God's will and not God's own speech. So "the text says God commanded it" does not by itself show that God commanded it. A text in which God orders a killing then counts against that text's reliability on that point, not against God.

# Origins
Origen read the wars of Joshua as a figure of the soul's war on sin. Taken literally, he held, they would have Christians "shed blood incessantly", where Christ teaches peace from the same reading.[^magree-2023] He also cited [Ezekiel 20:25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel%2020:25&version=NIV), where God says he gave Israel statutes that were not good.[^magree-2023]

[[C. S. Lewis]] wrote that the Old Testament is not God's word in the sense that every passage gives impeccable science or history. The Word is found in it, he wrote, through "discriminations made by our conscience and our critical faculties".[^lewis-1958]

Nicholas Wolterstorff set out a philosophical account of how God speaks through scripture in *Divine Discourse* (1995). On his account God speaks chiefly by appropriating scripture's human discourse, the way one person can adopt another's words as their own.[^wolterstorff-1995][^himma-2000] On [[Kenneth Einar Himma|Himma]]'s reading of the book, appropriation needs agreement only on a text's main point, and the human author's message may differ from the one God appropriates.[^himma-2000] Wolterstorff's own case is [Psalm 137:8–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%20137:8-9&version=NIV). He takes the psalmist to bless whoever smashes Babylonian infants, and notes that the church has rarely if ever held that "God was speaking that blessing".[^barker-2009]

[[Wesley Morriston|Morriston]] turned the claim on the conquest commands in 2009. Short of better reasons for inerrancy, he argues, a Christian should conclude that God did not command genocide.[^morriston-2009] The worst passages then reflect the moral development of their human authors, not the acts of a perfectly good God.[^morriston-2009] In the same issue of *Philosophia Christi*, [[Randal Rauser]] argues that God did not command the Canaanite genocide.[^rauser-2009] One of his four arguments is an undercutting defeater for the claim that Yahweh gave the command.[^rauser-2009]

[[Eric A. Seibert|Eric Seibert]] separates the "textual God", the portrait a passage draws, from the "actual God", and tests each portrait against the God Jesus reveals.[^seibert-2009] He reads [1 Samuel 15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015&version=NIV) as political propaganda for David against Saul, and concludes that "God never issued this genocidal decree".[^seibert-2009] Scripture's authority, on his account, rests on its content and the church's recognition, and "not everything found there is authoritative".[^seibert-2009]

In 2014 the Pontifical Biblical Commission, a Catholic body of biblical scholars, wrote that not every biblical passage holds the full revelation of God. Nor, it wrote, does every passage hold a perfect revelation of morality.[^pbc-2014] Each passage is to be weighed against the fullness of revelation in Jesus, within a canonical reading.[^pbc-2014]

Needs sources. Gregory A. Boyd's *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God* (Fortress Press, 2017), in two volumes, reads the violent portraits of God as accommodation, to be read through the cross. It is known here only through reviews and was not opened.

# Based On
- *Dei Verbum*, the Second Vatican Council's constitution on revelation (1965), asks the interpreter what the sacred writers intended and "what God wanted to manifest by means of their words".[^dei-verbum] Truth is expressed differently according to literary form, and each text is read within the unity of the whole of scripture.[^dei-verbum] Article 13 calls God's words in human language a "condescension" of eternal wisdom. Article 15 says the Old Testament contains "some things which are incomplete and temporary".[^dei-verbum]
- Wolterstorff applies the gap between writer and God to a prophetic oracle. In [Hosea 9:11–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hosea%209:11-17&version=NIV) God says, among other things, "I will no longer love them". Himma reports that on Wolterstorff's reading God appropriates the essence of that passage and not its letter.[^himma-2000] The summary of his chapter on biblical narrative asks how human discourse can mediate God's when it propounds error or expresses morally offensive attitudes.[^wolterstorff-1995]
- A reported command can be tested by its content. [[Richard Swinburne|Swinburne]] wrote that "the prophet who commends cheating and child torture can be dismissed straight away".[^swinburne-1992] Morriston applies the same test to the canon. When a book says God commanded what would otherwise be a heinous crime, he holds, the content of the command defeats the claim that God authored it.[^morriston-2012] [[C. Stephen Evans]] argues that a command known to aim at what is bad would not be authentic.[^evans-2022] His abstract raises it over Abraham and Isaac and does not mention the conquest.[^evans-2022]
- Later biblical writers revise earlier ones. [Ezekiel 18:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel%2018:20&version=NIV) says a child will not share a parent's guilt, against the punishment across generations in [Exodus 20:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2020:5&version=NIV).[^morriston-2012] Morriston counts this among many such challenges inside the Bible. Ethical criticism of the genocide texts, he concludes, continues a process the biblical authors began.[^morriston-2012]
- [Hosea 1:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hosea%201:4&version=NIV) has God punish the house of Jehu "for the massacre at Jezreel", which [2 Kings 10:30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%2010:30&version=NIV) has God commend. The Anchor Bible commentary reports that many scholars read Hosea as reversing that verdict, and itself denies that Hosea condemns the killing.[^andersen-freedman-1980] Needs sources: the International Critical Commentary on Hosea (1997), 17–18, seen at snippet only, reads a condemnation and calls the two texts a contradiction.
- Needs sources. How the messenger formula works inside a narrative, as in "This is what the LORD Almighty says" at [1 Samuel 15:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:2&version=NIV). A form-critical study would say whether the formula carries the narrator's own claim or reports a prophet's. Claus Westermann's *Basic Forms of Prophetic Speech* (English translation 1967) is the standard study of the formula and was found but not opened.

# Carries
- If it holds, a text in which God orders a killing counts against [[The Bible Only Speaks Truth]] on that point, not against God's goodness. Morriston presents the conquest commands as exactly that challenge to the biblical inerrantist.[^morriston-2009]
- It reaches what [[Conquest Texts Use Ancient War Hyperbole]] cannot. That claim's Limits put the commands of [Deuteronomy 20:16–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2020:16-18&version=NIV) and [1 Samuel 15:2–3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:2-3&version=NIV) beyond it, since an overstated report does not soften an order. This claim questions the order itself.
- It stands against the claims that keep the commands as God's. [[The Covenant Changed with Christ]] answers only whether they still bind. [[God Delays Judgment to Allow Repentance]] answers only why judgment came when it did.
- It licenses a negative step only: the report alone does not establish the command. It does not say which reported commands are God's own.

# Disputed By
- The evangelical Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978) denies that the writers' "finitude or fallenness" introduced distortion into God's word.[^icbi-1978] Its Exposition says "what Scripture says, God says".[^icbi-1978] It allows the reporting of falsehoods, with Satan's lies as the example, and names no narrated command.[^icbi-1978] The Chicago Statement on Biblical Hermeneutics (1982) denies that any "discourse or saying reported in Scripture" was invented.[^icbi-1982] It also denies that one passage corrects another, and that a narrative presenting itself as factual may be given a genre that negates its history.[^icbi-1982] These statements state [[The Bible Only Speaks Truth]] in its strict form. The 1978 Statement disclaims creedal weight in its own Preface.[^icbi-1978]
- *Dei Verbum* 11 holds that "everything asserted by the inspired authors" is asserted by the Holy Spirit.[^dei-verbum] Whether a narrated command is something the writer asserts is the point it leaves open.[^dei-verbum] The Commission reads article 11 as teaching the truth that bears on salvation in every part of scripture, not only in matters of faith and morals.[^pbc-2014]
- The Westminster Confession rests scripture's authority wholly on God as its author. A passage whose sense is in question, it says, has one sense, to be found from places that speak more clearly.[^westminster] The Thirty-Nine Articles hold that the Old Testament "is not contrary to the New", and forbid expounding one place so that it is "repugnant to another".[^thirty-nine-articles] Neither names a passage.
- [[Eleonore Stump]] argues that rejecting such a text on moral grounds means "the rejection of the entire idea of divine revelation in these texts".[^stump-2011][^morriston-2012] Once moral intuition judges the texts, she holds, they can no longer correct human standards.[^stump-2011][^morriston-2012] Morriston answers that the correcting can run in both directions. He grants her that every disquieting text will then need the same handling.[^morriston-2012]
- The test reaches the New Testament. Ken Esau, reviewing Seibert, points to Herod struck down in [Acts 12:21–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%2012:21-23&version=NIV) and to the violence of Revelation.[^esau-2011] He notes that Seibert will not apply to Jesus the method he uses on the Old Testament God.[^esau-2011] Seibert's rejoinder grants that some of Jesus' sayings on judgment "may be secondary".[^seibert-2011] J. Daryl Charles, reviewing Boyd, calls the method "functional Marcionism".[^charles-2019] He asks why New Testament writers, in a world as "fallen and culturally conditioned", should be trusted more.[^charles-2019] This is the strong objection, the concrete form of Stump's. It needs no confessional premise, and Seibert's own rejoinder concedes part of it.
- Kit Barker argues that Wolterstorff's reading of Psalm 137 leaves "something that resembles a creed, and a very short one".[^barker-2009] He reads God as sanctioning psalms like this one as "appropriate responses to extreme violence", and finds the New Testament consistent with it.[^barker-2009] On his reading the violent text becomes more God's own, not less.
- In [1 Samuel 15:10–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:10-11&version=NIV) the narrator, not only Samuel, reports the word of the LORD, and the LORD calls the order "my instructions". Saul loses the kingship for rejecting it ([1 Samuel 15:23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%2015:23&version=NIV)). Needs sources: a commentator who argues from this frame that the chapter itself vouches for the command as God's.

# Limits
- The claim reaches a given command only with a criterion that says which reported commands are God's own. Seibert's is the God Jesus reveals.[^seibert-2009] Wolterstorff reads the canon as a whole, on the assumption that God speaks only what is true and conducive to love.[^wolterstorff-1995][^barker-2009] The Commission weighs each passage against the fullness of revelation in Jesus.[^pbc-2014] Swinburne, Morriston and Evans test the content of the command.[^swinburne-1992][^morriston-2012][^evans-2022] Wolterstorff grants that reading for God's discourse may lead us to "conclude that God said what God did not say".[^barker-2009] What would settle it is a criterion fixed before the verdict. It would sort the commands without presupposing which go, and say how far into the New Testament it reaches. [[No Outcome Counts Against It]] makes the same demand in general form.
- It is a claim about what can be, not about what was. It shows that the step from "the text says" to "God said" needs support. It does not show that any given command was not God's.
- Origen is a precursor for the reading, not for the claim. The verse he cites, Ezekiel 20:25, has God say he gave the statutes that were not good, which keeps them as God's.[^magree-2023]
- The revision inside the Bible is weaker than it looks. Morriston grants that Ezekiel announces a new order without explicitly disputing the old one.[^morriston-2012] Whether Hosea condemns Jehu's killing is disputed among commentators.[^andersen-freedman-1980]
- The examples of Wolterstorff's reached here are psalms, Paul and a prophetic oracle, not a narrated command. His essay on the conquest, "Reading Joshua", argues from hyperbole in the victory reports, as far as its abstract shows.[^wolterstorff-2011] No source reached for this draft applies his model to Deuteronomy 20 or 1 Samuel 15.
- Catholic teaching does not settle it. Neither *Dei Verbum* nor the Commission says whether a narrated command is something the writer asserts. The Commission places the law of extermination within a history of revealed morality and does not say the command misreports God.[^pbc-2014] Its study is, by its own preface, not an official statement of Church teaching.[^pbc-2014]
- Accommodation alone does not yield the claim. [[Stephen N. Williams|Stephen Williams]] allows the slaughter as a possible divine command only if "sorrowfully commanded", accommodated to human violence.[^williams-2012] That keeps the command as God's.
- Needs sources. Swinburne's reply to Morriston in *Divine Evil?* (2011), seen as an extract only, denies that God inspired the first author of [Psalm 137:9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%20137:9&version=NIV). He still holds that, read within the Christian Bible, the passage speaks for God. That is a position between this claim and its denial.
