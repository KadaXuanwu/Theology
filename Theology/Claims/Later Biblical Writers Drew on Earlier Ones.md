# Description
Many biblical writers used earlier texts that are now in the Bible as sources. Where one book took material from another, the two are not independent witnesses for that material, though each stays a separate witness for the rest. The step from use to non-independence applies a rule of historical method stated in handbooks of 1898 and 1920.[^langlois-1898][^marshall-1920]

# Origins
- For the Hebrew Bible the practice is called [[Inner-biblical exegesis|inner-biblical exegesis]].[^sarna-1963] [[Michael Fishbane]] writes in *Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel* (1985) that he learned of the practice and the term from his teacher.[^fishbane-1985] That teacher had used the term in a 1963 study of Psalm 89.[^sarna-1963]
- [[Bernard M. Levinson]] has argued since his 1997 book that Deuteronomy innovated on earlier law.[^levinson-1997][^levinson-2007] Biblical authors, he says, developed a "rhetoric of concealment" that "served to camouflage the actual literary history of the laws".[^levinson-2007]
- A handbook of 1898 holds that "An agreement is only conclusive when the agreeing statements represent observations which are independent of each other" (p. 200).[^langlois-1898] The 1920 handbook, which cites it, holds that two or more credible eyewitnesses as a rule establish a fact, but "the witnesses must be independent of each other". Three reports of 14 July 1789 are "only one" witness, because two copied the third (p. 35).[^marshall-1920] A 1991 study of the historical Jesus applies the rule through the criterion of [[Multiple attestation|multiple attestation]]. Its example is Peter's confession. Its record in Mark, Matthew and Luke "does not satisfy the criterion of multiple attestation, since both Matthew and Luke are dependent on Mark for the basic narrative". It adds that Matthew may draw on a separate tradition for part of the scene.[^meier-1991]

# Based On
- **Daniel and Jeremiah.** [Daniel 9:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Daniel%209:2&version=NIV) names Jeremiah's seventy years ([Jeremiah 29:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah%2029:10&version=NIV)), which the chapter recasts as seventy "sevens" ([Daniel 9:24–27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Daniel%209:24-27&version=NIV)). A 2009 study calls the result "a radical reinterpretation of the notion of exile".[^athas-2009]
- **Chronicles.** [[Ehud Ben Zvi]] writes that Chronicles "uses sources that eventually became biblical texts (notably Samuel and Kings, but also Genesis, Psalms, and prophetic books)". Samuel and Kings are "the sources for the lion's share of the book".[^ben-zvi-2005]
- **The first three Gospels.** They agree in narrative as well as sayings, so "it will not do to argue on this basis that the Gospels are linked only orally". Every main solution to the [[Synoptic Problem]] links them in writing. In 2001 the consensus had "pronounced strongly in favour of the Priority of Mark", which Matthew and Luke then used.[^goodacre-2001]
- **2 Peter and Jude.** A 2004 study finds it "now widely accepted that 2 Peter is dependent on Jude". It reads [2 Peter 2:1–3:3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Peter%202:1-3:3&version=NIV) as "a free paraphrase" of [Jude 4–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jude%204-18&version=NIV).[^callan-2004]
- **The prophets.** By the count of the fifth edition of the United Bible Societies' Greek New Testament, the New Testament quotes the prophetic books 84 times.[^moyise-2022] Some are applied in a sense they lacked in their setting: [Hosea 11:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hosea%2011:1&version=NIV), applied in [Matthew 2:15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%202:15&version=NIV), looks back to the exodus.[^moyise-2022]
- **Law.** [Exodus 20:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2020:24&version=NIV) allows sacrifice at local altars. Levinson argues that Deuteronomy 12, restricting sacrifice to one place, abrogates Exodus's "affirmation of multiple altar sites". It does so by "presenting cultic centralization as a rejection of prohibited cultic practices".[^levinson-2026] Deuteronomy sets its rule against adding or taking away ([Deuteronomy 12:32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2012:32&version=NIV)) straight after that change.[^levinson-2007] [[Scripture Can Be God's Word Without Reporting God's Speech]] sets out a further case, [Ezekiel 18:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel%2018:20&version=NIV) against [Exodus 20:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2020:5&version=NIV).

# Carries
- On the 1920 handbook's rule, agreement between two books on material one took from the other is not independent confirmation of it.[^marshall-1920] Counting such books as separate authors who agree overcounts by the borrowed share.
- Which Gospel material counts as multiply attested then depends on the solution to the Synoptic Problem one adopts.

# Disputed By
- **The dependence is partial.** This is the strong objection, and it bounds the claim rather than denying it. Ben Zvi finds Chronicles "conceptually so different from each of the sources" that none can be called its point of departure. With Samuel and Kings it "has a quite free hand, limited only by core facts agreed upon in its community".[^ben-zvi-2005] A 2004 commentary on 1 Chronicles, a review reports, argues that the book is not a commentary on Samuel and Kings. The review adds that the commentator "seems to see it as an alternative to the earlier work".[^mitchell-2005]
- **A common source for Chronicles.** [[A. Graeme Auld]], in *Kings Without Privilege* (1994), holds that Samuel and Kings and Chronicles each drew on a common earlier text, "The Book of Two Houses". On his view Chronicles follows Samuel and Kings in content without using them. The view has found some support, but most Chronicles scholars hold that the Chronicler used Samuel and Kings directly.[^jonker-2014]
- **No literary link among the first three Gospels.** [[Eta Linnemann]] argues in *Is There a Synoptic Problem?* (1992) that the three arose without literary dependence on one another. She holds that "every divergence of Matthew and Luke from Mark speaks against literary dependence".[^linnemann-1992] A 2002 volume of three views on the Gospels' origins gives the independence view a chapter of its own.[^thomas-2002] A 2001 survey calls a literary relationship among the three "a firm consensus in scholarship", from which "occasionally a dissenting voice will sound".[^goodacre-2001]
- **John may stand apart.** [[C. H. Dodd]] held that John rests on an old tradition independent of the other Gospels. A 1981 survey reported that five major commentaries, [[Raymond E. Brown|Raymond Brown]]'s among them, hold to common tradition "but no literary dependence". It also lists scholars who found that John used or knew Luke or Mark, and concludes that "perhaps" there is "no longer any substantive consensus".[^carson-1981]
- **Direction.** [[Michael J. Kruger]], who defends Peter's authorship of 2 Peter, holds that "it is not at all obvious that 2 Peter is dependent upon Jude". He also writes that "Most scholars see a clear literary relationship" between the two.[^kruger-1999] The view that puts Matthew first and Mark last is held by "a weighty and vocal minority".[^goodacre-2001]
- **The date of Exodus's older law.** [[John Van Seters]], in *A Law Book for the Diaspora*, dates the law collection of [Exodus 20:22–23:19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2020:22-23:19&version=NIV) after Deuteronomy. He holds that it was written in the Babylonian exile and depends on Deuteronomy's laws.[^van-seters-2003][^wright-2004] On that dating the altar law of Exodus 20:24 would be the later text. A 2004 review notes that "most scholars think" the collection is earlier than Deuteronomy.[^wright-2004]

# Limits
- The claim holds passage by passage, not for whole books. Its weight turns on how much of the agreement between two books lies in borrowed material. For the Gospels the weight turns on the solution to the Synoptic Problem and on whether John used the other three. The survey of the John question cited here is from 1981; a newer one appeared in 1992 and was revised in 2001.[^carson-1981][^smith-1992]
- Knowing a person is not drawing on a text, and [Galatians 1:18–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%201:18-19&version=NIV) records a visit. [Luke 1:1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%201:1-4&version=NIV) names none of its "many" predecessors, so Mark as one of them is an inference.
- Not every difference is a change. The Qumran evidence suggests that Samuel circulated in several versions, only one of which the Chronicler used.[^mitchell-2005] The Qumran scroll 4QSam-a often agrees with 1 Chronicles against the [[Masoretic Text|Masoretic]] Samuel.[^jonker-2014] [[Jews Used Several Forms of the Hebrew Bible Text Before One Remained]] covers that plurality.
- Resemblance alone does not show use. A 1961 presidential address to the Society of Biblical Literature warned against the "extravagance" that "first overdoes the supposed similarity in passages". Its author still held "Mark was a source utilized by both Matthew and Luke".[^sandmel-1962] The cases here rest on a named source or on shared wording and order.
- The witness rule rests here on handbooks of 1898 and 1920 and one example in the 1991 study. No peer reviewed or academic press statement of it was read in full for this node. The 1920 handbook's case is copying, so whether Daniel 9 also depends on Jeremiah for what it composed in answer to it is a further step.[^marshall-1920]
- Shared editors would be a separate route, which the claim does not argue. [[Karel van der Toorn]] holds that "The scribes who manufactured the Bible were professional writers affiliated to the temple of Jerusalem", as a 2010 review reports. The review calls the basis for that temple workshop "tenuous".[^schniedewind-2010]
- Dependence is not error. The 1981 survey faults the step from the independence of John's tradition to its trustworthiness.[^carson-1981]
- The claim shows how agreement arose, not whether the process was guided. [[Richard B. Hays]] holds that the emergence of the biblical writings "is itself part of God's mysterious 'authorial' action".[^hays-2016]
