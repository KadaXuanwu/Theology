# Description
Nobody picks what to believe the way they pick what to do. Someone shown an argument either finds it convincing or does not, and wanting the other result does not produce it. What a person does control is indirect: what they read, who they listen to, which questions they keep open, and where they let their attention rest.

# Origins
The mainstream position in current epistemology is that direct doxastic control does not exist. The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on doxastic voluntarism puts it that direct control is "widely rejected by contemporary authors", while indirect doxastic control is "uncontroversial".[^sep-doxastic-voluntarism]

Two arguments carry most of the weight. The psychological one is William Alston's, in a 1988 paper on the deontological conception of epistemic justification: ask whether you could believe that the United States is still a British colony for a reward of $500,000,000, and the answer settles it.[^alston-1988] The conceptual one is Bernard Williams's, in a 1973 paper collected in *Problems of the Self*: if belief could be produced at will, a person could judge a proposition true regardless of thinking it true, and would know they had that power.[^williams-1973] Jonathan Bennett's "Why Is Belief Involuntary?" is the third standard reference.[^bennett-1990]

Both are reported here from the encyclopedia's account of them rather than from the papers, which were not opened. Nobody has checked the Williams pagination, and the piece is chapter 9 of *Problems of the Self*, not of *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*, which is a common slip.

# Based On
- Needs sources. The encyclopedia entry is the only thing here that was read at full access.
- The encyclopedia records Alston's argument as endorsed by [[Alvin Plantinga]] and defended since by others, which is the evidence that it is the field's position and not one author's.[^sep-doxastic-voluntarism]

# Carries
- It is what makes an unconvinced person's position not a refusal, which is the ground [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]] stands on.
- It constrains what suppression can mean. If belief is not directly chosen, a suppression account has to run through attention and inquiry rather than through a decision, which is the shape [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] finds it already retreating into.
- It reaches only direct control. That is the whole of what it licenses.

# Disputed By
- A parity based voluntarism is a live minority. The encyclopedia names Matthias Steup as its principal defender, with Blake Roeber, Brian Weatherson and Danny Frederick alongside him.[^sep-doxastic-voluntarism] The move is not to claim direct control but to argue that belief and voluntary action are on a par, so whatever makes action voluntary makes belief voluntary too.
- Needs the primary literature. None of the voluntarist papers was opened.

# Limits
- Indirect influence is uncontroversial and does a lot of work, so this claim reaches less far than it first appears. Someone can grant that belief is not directly chosen and still hold a person responsible for which evidence they sought out, whom they asked, and how long they looked.
- It says nothing about whether a given person's unbelief was arrived at honestly. That is a separate question and it is measured, badly, at [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]].
- The claim is about belief, not about assent to a practice, a commitment or a way of life. An argument that treats faith as trust rather than as a verdict on a proposition is not touched by it.
- Whether direct control is impossible or merely rare is not settled here, and the encyclopedia's "widely rejected" is a report about a field rather than a demonstration.
