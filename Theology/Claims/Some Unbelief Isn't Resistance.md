# Description
At least one person, at some time, has lacked belief that God exists without that unbelief being caused by their own resistance to God. This is the non-resistance half of the existence premise in [[J. L. Schellenberg]]'s hiddenness argument, the half the term [[Non-resistant non-belief|non-resistant non-belief]] names. On his account resistance is a self-deceived closing of the door on God. It is made of a wish, such as to go one's own way, and acts or omissions such as careless investigation.[^sep-hiddenness]

# Origins
Schellenberg first framed the claim in terms of fault. His 1993 book counts nonbelief "reasonable" only if it is "not the result of culpable actions or omissions on the part of the subject" (p. 3 n. 2).[^schellenberg-1993] In *The Hiddenness Argument* of 2015 he calls that focus on culpability "a mistake".[^sep-hiddenness]

The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry by [[Daniel Howard-Snyder]] and [[Adam Green]] lists four kinds of case. They are honest seekers, cultures that "lack the idea of a personal God altogether", prehistoric hunter-gatherers, and former believers "who would like nothing more than to regain it".[^sep-hiddenness]

# Based On
- The encyclopedia states the premise over one person and one time, and [[Andrew Blanton]] notes the argument "requires only that a single non-resistant non-believer exist" (p. 548).[^sep-hiddenness][^blanton-2026] Denying the claim takes the universal thesis that all unbelief, at every time, came from the person's own resistance.
- [[Max Baker-Hytch]] reports that most replies offer reasons God might permit such unbelief, while "a substantial minority" appeal to human sinfulness to call it into question. An attack on the existence premise, he writes, "looks like an uphill battle, to put it mildly" (p. 651).[^baker-hytch-2025] [[Stephen Napier]], who argues against the premise, calls its acceptance an "orthodoxy" (p. 572).[^napier-2023] Blanton writes that "many critics" doubt it, citing eight works (p. 547).[^blanton-2026]
- Blanton reads Schellenberg as requiring "a direct causal connection between the relevant form of resistance to God and non-belief" (p. 549). On that reading a resister "must, by definition, begin in a state of belief in God and only subsequently lapse into non-belief" (p. 550). Unbelief that is only kept in place can be blameworthy without being resistance (pp. 551–552). A person whose unbelief formed before they could be held responsible for it therefore counts as non-resistant (p. 561).[^blanton-2026]
- In the Understanding Unbelief survey of 2019, 82 per cent of Chinese and 70 per cent of Japanese unbelievers had a nonreligious upbringing.[^bullivant-2019] In the US, 9 per cent of adults were raised with no religion and still have none.[^pew-2025] Both surveys ask about religion in upbringing, not belief in God.[^bullivant-2019][^pew-2025] Schellenberg names people who "have never so much as had the theistic idea in question here squarely before their minds" (p. 331).[^schellenberg-2005] [[Belief in God Follows Birthplace]] carries Blanton's case of Vietnam.
- Schellenberg's 2017 survey counts the unbelief, "over aeons of evolution", of those "never even able to resist personal relationship with God" as a case.[^schellenberg-2017-part1] As the encyclopedia reports it, the chance that all the "hundreds of millions of nonbelievers in the secular West" were resistant "at the dawn of their capacity" is "Vanishingly small".[^sep-hiddenness]
- Baker-Hytch examines accounts built on a description of fallen human nature, Plantinga's among them. They do not "obviously offer anything that could be regarded as positive evidence" that humans are that way, he finds (p. 652).[^baker-hytch-2025]
- [[Lari Launonen]], who defends a partial suppression thesis, calls real cases such as former believers and lifelong seekers "the most serious problem for the universal suppression thesis" (p. 274). Their unbelief "is very hard to explain away as the result of self-deceptive suppression", he writes (p. 276).[^launonen-2025]
- Among 520 paid online respondents screened to exclude believers, the mean wish for a god to exist was 34.48 on a scale of 0 to 100 (SD 32.70).[^bradley-2014] Emotional reasons for unbelief did not go with wanting God less.[^bradley-2014][^bradley-2018] [[David F. Bradley]]'s thesis had predicted that they would.[^bradley-2014]

# Carries
- It fills the non-resistance half of the existence premise and does not give Schellenberg's conclusion. Howard-Snyder's reply, as his editors sum it up, is that "a lack of openness can actually promote a better long-term relationship in certain circumstances" (p. 7).[^green-stump-2016]
- It does not make anyone blameless. Blanton warns against conflating "non-resistant non-belief with blameless non-belief" (p. 552). A non-resistant person can still be at fault for keeping the unbelief, he argues.[^blanton-2026]
- *Lumen Gentium* 16, from the Second Vatican Council in 1964, speaks of those who "without blame on their part, have not yet arrived at an explicit knowledge of God".[^lumen-gentium] [[Invincible Ignorance Excuses Unbelief]] sets out that teaching.
- It is the case [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] has to cover where it reads [[John Calvin]]'s implanted sense of God as present in everyone.

# Disputed By
- The strong objection is [[Paul A. Macdonald Jr.]]'s. He denies that Schellenberg "is entitled to make the claim that it is more reasonable to affirm than deny the existence of at least one genuine nonresistant nonbeliever" (p. 148). The premise, on his reconstruction, works "only if more likely than not we'd detect these nonbelievers' resistance toward God if there were any" (p. 143). Desires can be divided and self-deceived, so a non-believer can act on a desire for God "while still retaining an undetectable resistance toward God" (pp. 145–146). He applies this to prehistoric non-theists: "we cannot assume that these nontheists never had God sufficiently in mind in order to resist him" (p. 146). He applies the same doubt to those in secular culture "who seem never to have thought about God". For all we know, he writes, their unbelief "has been generated by resistance toward God, and continues to be sustained by it" (p. 146). Of former believers, "For all we know, in each case a deeper, undetectable resistance toward the divine helped create doubt" (pp. 146–147). The number of apparent cases, he argues, "does not by itself justify our claiming" that even one is more likely than not genuine (p. 149). He closes that step: "for all we know, the phenomenon of nonresistant nonbelief simply never has existed nor does it exist now" (p. 149).[^macdonald-2021]
- On Macdonald's account a non-believer's seeming desire for a relationship with God gives "prima facie" justification that she has it (p. 151). His stated aim "has not been to show that there is real reason to doubt that nonresistant nonbelief in God actually exists" (p. 155).[^macdonald-2021]
- A second rival holds that "most individuals begin life with belief in God, or at least something like God". They acquire unbelief later, on this view, when they can be held responsible for it (p. 560). Blanton reports [[Matthew Braddock]]'s argument from the cognitive science of religion. Humans, on that argument, are disposed to believe in agents with "creator or designer status", a cluster Braddock calls "recognizably theistic-like" (p. 560).[^blanton-2026] [[Deborah Kelemen]] defines "intuitive theists" as children "disposed to view natural phenomena as resulting from nonhuman design". By about age five, she concludes, the description "may have explanatory value" (p. 295). She reports Evans's finding that 8 to 10 year olds "from both fundamentalist and nonfundamentalist American homes favored 'creationist' accounts" of how species began (p. 296).[^kelemen-2004] Non-religious adults in a speeded task "displayed a default bias to increasingly judge pictures of natural phenomena as 'purposefully made by some being'". The judgments persisted when belief in nature itself as an agent was controlled (p. 72).[^jarnefelt-2015]
- Napier argues that the apparent cases are either resistance or the kind of non-resistant unbelief "not incongruent with what a good and loving God would allow" (p. 572). His conclusion "tolerates the existence of non-resistant non-belief" (p. 573). His test is openness to belief, which he takes from Schellenberg (p. 575). A person whose ideological commitments preclude even considering theism fails it (p. 577), and he does not ask whether upbringing put them there.[^napier-2023]
- The universal suppression thesis holds that all unbelief comes from self-deceived suppression of a knowledge of God, or evidence for God, that everyone has or had (pp. 263–264, 274).[^launonen-2025] Calvin held that God "has endued all men with some idea of his Godhead", "to prevent any man from pretending ignorance" (*Institutes* I.3.1).[^calvin-institutes] [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] sets the thesis out, with why Baker-Hytch and Launonen reject its universal form. [[Alvin Plantinga]]'s version of Calvin's model allows unbelief that no act of the person caused. Damage to the sense of God is "not necessarily sin on the part of the person with the disease" (p. 214 n. 22). Perhaps in some people at some times, he writes, it "doesn't work at all" (p. 215). His person "brought up to think there is no such person as God" still ignores "the promptings of the sense of divinity" (pp. 215–216).[^plantinga-wcb]
- [[Douglas V. Henry]] argued in 2001 that "meaningful investigation is the exception rather than the rule" (p. 80).[^henry-2001] His target is "reasonable nonbelief", which he reads as inculpable nonbelief. By 2008 he held that "some form of reasonable nonbelief is probably instantiated" (pp. 280–281). Over every apparent case, he wrote, "hovers the shadow of self-deception" (p. 286).[^henry-2008] Blanton answers Henry's 2008 argument that unreflective nonbelief is culpable. It concerns keeping unbelief, not acquiring it, Blanton holds, and so leaves non-resistance standing (p. 558).[^blanton-2026]

# Limits
- The claim supplies the non-resistance half, not the whole premise. The premise also needs the same person to be capable of a relationship with God at that time, a condition Schellenberg leaves "assumed but left tacit".[^schellenberg-2017-part1] On his criterion as Blanton reports it, the capable can form beliefs about God and "give and receive love in its ordinary forms" (p. 549).[^blanton-2026] [[James Dominic Rooney]] holds that people born after an original human resistance "have not necessarily performed any act which would constitute a personal act of resistance" (p. 644). They lack, he argues, the "proximate" capacity to relate to God (p. 644). He offers this first as a "defense" true "for all we know", then argues from the experience of saints that "something like original sin has occurred" (p. 642).[^rooney-2024] [[Charity Anderson]]'s review reports that [[Michael Rea]] argues almost anyone, even someone who lacks the concept of God, can relate to God "just by trying".[^anderson-2019]
- On Blanton's account the case of people raised without belief turns on whether their unbelief formed before they could be held responsible for it (pp. 560–561).[^blanton-2026] Macdonald's objection is that we cannot assume they never had "God sufficiently in mind in order to resist him" (p. 146).[^macdonald-2021] Blanton replies to the theistic bent argument that such a bias "is likely to be inhibited" in non-theistic settings (p. 561). He cites Braddock's own statement that "countervailing influences such as cultural factors" can lead people to worldviews "that do not affirm a high god" (p. 561).[^blanton-2026] Launonen reports "some evidence of implicit theism among atheists". He adds that "Many studies have also failed to find evidence of implicit supernatural beliefs among non-believers" (p. 268).[^launonen-2025] Kelemen sets as an open question whether children "would not independently generate explanations in terms of designing nonnatural agency without adult cultural influence" (p. 299).[^kelemen-2004]
- The case of former believers and seekers rests on their own testimony. Bradley and his co-authors write that their scale "is only capable of detecting those reasons that respondents are aware of".[^bradley-2018] The wanting item records neither effort nor outcome, so it cannot show that anyone wanted to believe and could not.
- The prehistoric case rests on what early humans did not believe. Braddock notes that "Beliefs don't fossilize" and that "The absence of archaeological evidence for high god concepts is not good evidence of their absence" (p. 175).[^braddock-2022]
- The claim does not say how common such unbelief is. Blanton argues it is "relatively common" (p. 547).[^blanton-2026] [[Elizabeth Jackson]] argues "there is no good reason to think that there are very many" (p. 86). She counts non-resistance as freedom from blame (p. 86 n. 2).[^jackson-2016]
