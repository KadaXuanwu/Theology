# Description
Some people fail to believe in God without resisting God. That covers people who looked and came out unconvinced, and people who never had the idea to accept or reject. Philosophy of religion calls this non-resistant non-belief, and the label describes how the unbelief came about, not the person's moral character.

# Origins
J. L. Schellenberg introduced the idea in *Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason*.[^schellenberg-1993] Daniel Howard-Snyder and Adam Green reconstruct his argument in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.[^sep-hiddenness]

The 1993 version is framed around culpability. The encyclopedia records that *The Wisdom to Doubt* swaps culpability for resistance, and quotes him in the 2015 book calling that earlier focus a mistake.[^schellenberg-2007] In *The Hiddenness Argument* the premise reads that at least one capable finite person was at some time nonresistantly in a state of nonbelief.[^schellenberg-2015] Andrew Blanton states the definition as unbelief that does not result from the affective or behavioural dispositions characteristic of resistance to God.[^blanton-2026]

The encyclopedia lists the cases in view: honest seekers, members of cultures with no idea of a personal God, hunter-gatherers before recorded history, and former believers. The definition is not what people fight over. The existence claim is.

# Based On
- The encyclopedia entry. It reports some holding there are not many such people (Jackson 2016) and others that everyone at least implicitly believes in God (Wainwright 2002), and says a denial has to be squared with the evidence marshalled on their behalf. Widely granted, not universally.
- Lari Launonen tests how much of the suppression thesis survives.[^launonen-2025] He grants it some support from cognitive science, the territory of [[Humans Are Primed to See Agents and Purpose]], and concedes it is hard to see what would motivate self-deception about God's existence. He concludes that the non-belief of lifelong seekers and former believers is "very hard to explain away as the result of self-deceptive suppression", and that "the universal suppression thesis seems hopeless", while holding a strengthened partial thesis could still answer claims that God allows this much of it.
- Pew Research Center asked "nones" why they are nonreligious (n = 11,201 US adults, margin ±1.4 points).[^pew-2024] 67% of "nones" gave questioning religious teachings or not believing in God as an extremely or very important reason for being nonreligious, and 91% of atheists did. A related survey (n = 8,937) found that 51% of those who became "nones" gave "stopped believing in the religion's teachings" as extremely or very important.[^pew-2025]
- One study measures the wanting directly. Bradley asked respondents, whatever they believe, how much they want a god or gods to exist, on a 0 to 100 slider. In a sample screened to exclude believers the mean was 34.48, standard deviation 32.70. That is Study 1, n = 520, a paid online convenience sample.[^bradley-2014] The item sits alongside the RANGES scale rather than inside it.

# Carries
- It supplies the existence premise the hiddenness problem needs and reaches no further. Blanton notes the argument needs only one such person.
- What follows from it is contested by people who grant it. Michael Rea's *The Hiddenness of God* goes after the assumption that divine love must resemble idealised human love.[^rea-2018] Charity Anderson's review reports his line as divine love being perfect love without being ideal love.[^anderson-2019]
- If some unbelief is not a refusal, treating every case as a refusal misdescribes those people, which is where this meets [[No Finite Life Deserves Infinite Punishment]].

# Disputed By
- Stephen Napier argues the hiddenness argument runs on a false premise: such non-belief does not occur, or where it does it is not the sort a good and loving God would be obliged to prevent.[^napier-2023] His corollary is that someone arguing for atheism from hiddenness cannot satisfy the criterion.
- Alvin Plantinga, in chapter 7 of *Warranted Christian Belief*, holds that without sin and its effects God's presence and glory would be as obvious to us all as other minds and physical objects,[^plantinga-wcb] and that the deliverances of the sensus divinitatis, muffled already, can be suppressed by turning attention away from them. He holds the sense itself is damaged by sin without being obliterated. He does not hold that the damage in a given person is that person's own fault, which [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] sets out.
- Douglas V. Henry argues that adequate investigation is the exception rather than the rule, and that where the marks of honest inquiry are genuinely present they produce continued seeking rather than a settled resting in non-belief.[^henry-2001] Schellenberg replied at 22:3 (2005) and Henry answered again at 25:3 (2008).

# Limits
- The encyclopedia's own summary is that no single reply offers a total explanation of nonresistant nonbelief, though several partial ones might add up to one. It adds the harder point that it may be difficult to tell whether a person possesses or lacks the motivations at issue, so nobody may be well placed to say there are such non-believers.
- None of the sources here asks whether a non-believer wanted to believe and could not. Pew records how people describe themselves, and Bradley's item measures the wanting, not whether it went unmet.
- Nothing here settles how common such non-belief is. Blanton argues that it is relatively common, resting on a distinction between responsibility for acquiring a belief and responsibility for keeping it.[^blanton-2026] Jackson holds there are not many.
- The claim does not say all unbelief is non-resistant. Launonen's partial suppression thesis and Napier's benign category both stay on the table.
