# Description
Belief in God is spread very unevenly across countries and follows upbringing. The claim is that the resistance and sin that theistic accounts of unbelief appeal to are not spread that way. If so, the pattern is less surprising if there is no God than if there is. [[Stephen Maitzen]] assumes that people with such motives "do not cluster by country or culture", any more than deafness does (2006, p. 184).[^maitzen-2006] His conclusion is comparative: the lopsided distribution of theistic belief "is less surprising on naturalistic explanations than on theistic ones" (2008, p. 477).[^maitzen-2008]

# Origins
[[Michel de Montaigne]] wrote that "We are Christians by the same title that we are Perigordians or Germans" (II.12), aiming at faith that rests on custom.[^montaigne-essays] [[John Stuart Mill]] wrote in 1859 that "the same causes which make him a Churchman in London, would have made him a Buddhist or a Confucian in Pekin".[^mill-on-liberty] [[T. J. Mawson]] reads Mill's point as one about religious knowledge, which cuts against the irreligious too.[^mawson-2009] [[John Hick]] wrote that a person's religion almost always depends on "the accidents of birth", naming Thailand and Saudi Arabia among his examples (p. 2).[^hick-1989]

Maitzen set Saudi Arabia, "at least 95 per cent theistic", against Thailand, "at most 5 per cent theistic" (p. 179). Both figures are inferred from religious affiliation in the CIA World Factbook (n. 8). In 2006 he wrote that no theistic reply "has overcome" the uneven distribution and suggested none "could overcome" it, while naturalistic explanations "seem more promising" (p. 177).[^maitzen-2006] [[Max Baker-Hytch]] notes that [[Daniel Howard-Snyder]] calls the phenomenon "cultural nonbelief", citing a 1996 article (2025, p. 654).[^baker-hytch-2025] [[Adam Green]] calls Maitzen's "the second most prominent hiddenness argument", after [[J. L. Schellenberg]]'s (p. 617).[^green-2025]

# Based On
- Direct questions about belief, not affiliation, still show a wide spread. Among people born 1971 to 1981 in the 2008 International Social Survey Programme, about 30 per cent of the variation in belief in God lay between countries.[^ishii-2024] A 22-country survey, mostly from 2023, put belief in one God, several gods or an impersonal force at 20 per cent in Japan and 100 per cent in Egypt.[^aglozo-2025] In 2022, 48 per cent of Thai and 44 per cent of Cambodian adults told Pew they believe in God, against 99 per cent in Indonesia. That is far above the 5 per cent Maitzen allowed for Thailand. Pew notes that the question is geared to monotheistic religions.[^pew-2023-asia] In Saudi Arabia the World Values Survey of 2003 asked "Do you believe in God?", and 1,485 of 1,487 valid answers were yes.[^wvs-2003]
- Across 117 countries, 91 per cent of adults aged 18 to 54 keep the religion, or lack of one, they were raised in. Of those raised with no religion, 93 per cent still have none.[^pew-2025-switching]
- What a child sees at home predicts belief. In preregistered analyses of a nationally representative sample of 1,417 US adults, having seen caregivers act on their faith less often was the strongest predictor of disbelief tested.[^gervais-2021] Across 43 countries and regions, a mother's and a father's weekly attendance at services in childhood each went with higher odds of believing in God.[^ishii-2024]
- Maitzen gives the mechanism: attitudes to the divine are "highly plastic and subject to local conditions", with parents the obvious route (2008, p. 475).[^maitzen-2008]
- The motive premise is shared in print. Baker-Hytch states it as an objection to sin-based accounts of unbelief (2025, p. 660). He writes that "an orthodox Christian cannot embrace" original sin being greater in some populations than in others (p. 661).[^baker-hytch-2025] [[The Suppression Thesis Rests on Possibility]] carries his conclusion on those accounts.
- [[Andrew Blanton]] argues that where few believe, as in Vietnam, a child's non-belief is "a likely, if not nearly inevitable, outcome" and not resistance (2026, pp. 556–557).[^blanton-2026] That is [[Non-resistant non-belief|non-resistant non-belief]] at the scale of a country, the case [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]] makes.

# Carries
- It gives the direction of the evidence, not its size. Blanton notes that even if theism starts out more probable, the argument "might still render naturalism more probable than it otherwise would be" (2025, n. 27).[^blanton-2025]
- It removes universal sin and resistance accounts as an answer to the pattern and leaves partial ones standing, as Baker-Hytch concludes (2025, p. 662).[^baker-hytch-2025]
- It presses on [[General Revelation Leaves People Without Excuse]] where that reads [[John Calvin]]'s inborn sense of God as universal. Maitzen argues that inborn capacities such as hearing are spread evenly across the species, and belief in God is not (2006, pp. 186–187).[^maitzen-2006]

# Disputed By
- The strongest reply is Baker-Hytch's of 2016. People who rely on one another's testimony will cluster in their religious beliefs whether or not God exists, and God has reason to make people that way. He names three trade-offs: trust against being proof against deception, sharing responsibility for others' knowledge against self-reliance, and chances to grow intellectual virtue against freedom from challenge.[^sep-hiddenness][^blanton-2025] He claims a favourable balance and aims to cast doubt on Maitzen's claim, not to refute it.[^baker-hytch-2016] It assumes destiny is not fixed by belief at death.[^sep-hiddenness]
- Blanton answers that God could secure belief in himself in each person while people still depend on one another for much else, keeping all three goods (2025, pp. 703–705). If belief before death were needed to avoid damnation, as [[Restrictive exclusivism|restrictive exclusivism]] holds, he says the reply "could hardly get off the ground". The argument itself runs "without any appeal to afterlife considerations" (p. 709).[^blanton-2025] Schellenberg makes the point as type and token: dependence on testimony could hold as a type even if everyone believed in God.[^schellenberg-2017]
- [[Jason Marsh]] replies from [[Middle knowledge|middle knowledge]]. He supposes there are "some persons who would refuse to love God no matter what geographic circumstance they found themselves in". God places them among non-believers "to keep these individuals innocent for a later time" (pp. 467–468).[^marsh-2008] Maitzen answers that parents passing on their religion is the likelier cause, and that a plausible explanation needs "more than logical possibility" (2008, pp. 475–476).[^maitzen-2008] Baker-Hytch writes that a related middle knowledge move, which he traces to [[William Lane Craig]], "will strike many as being too much to stomach" (2025, p. 656).[^baker-hytch-2025]
- Mawson offers three stories that fit the pattern: God respecting freedom while culture shapes receptivity, a deadline for belief at the Last Judgment rather than death, and election.[^mawson-2012][^sep-hiddenness] The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* finds the middle knowledge and election stories, at first glance, "more than a little bit offensive".[^sep-hiddenness]
- Baker-Hytch weighs sin built into whole cultures, which can cluster but cannot make each unbeliever's unbelief his own sin. Faith that saves without explicit belief may be "far more evenly distributed", but then something besides sin must explain the missing belief (pp. 661–662).[^baker-hytch-2025] Maitzen asks why whole countries must "get by with the substitute" of implicit belief (2006, p. 185).[^maitzen-2006]
- Green questions what the surveys count as belief. Folk religion, he argues, differs less between Riyadh and Bangkok than the reflective religion of lay people, which in turn differs less than official doctrine (pp. 622–628). How strong the evidence is then "depends on which kind of cognition one thinks God would be more invested in guaranteeing" (p. 628).[^green-2025]
- The reply that humans cannot judge what God would permit "leaves the demographics of theism totally unexplained", Maitzen answers (2006, p. 186).[^maitzen-2006]

# Limits
- The claim stands or falls on one question. Would a perfectly loving God, able to give each person belief in himself while people learn everything else from one another, leave that belief to testimony too? Baker-Hytch argues that trust and shared responsibility for knowledge give him reason to. Blanton argues those goods survive if God secures the belief himself. No survey reaches this. A published case for a good that needs belief in God itself to pass by testimony would move it.
- The motive half is assumed, not measured. Maitzen says the objection "assumes, plausibly" that the motives do not cluster, and cites no study (2006, p. 184).[^maitzen-2006] The Understanding Unbelief survey of 2019 asked atheists and agnostics in six countries about identity, upbringing, confidence and values, not about resistance to God.[^bullivant-2019] The exposure studies did not measure motive, and reflective thinking, an individual trait, also predicted disbelief among people raised with little religious example.[^gervais-2021] Stated reasons for leaving vary by country. Among people who stopped identifying with a religion, majorities in Spain (74 per cent) and Italy (60 per cent) cite scandals involving religious institutions.[^pew-2018-europe] Reasons people give are not causes, and scandal is not the resistance these accounts mean.
- It reaches accounts that trace each unbeliever's unbelief to his own motives, and older ones that promised belief to all who did not resist. [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] held that someone raised in the woods who followed natural reason would be given what is needed for believing, if nothing on his side impeded it.[^aquinas-de-veritate] [[Stephen Bullivant|Bullivant]] notes that this assumed the gospel had already been preached everywhere.[^bullivant-2011] *Lumen Gentium* 16 (1964) speaks of those who, without blame, lack explicit knowledge of God and with his grace strive to live a good life. It says providence does not deny them the helps needed for salvation. It also says people are "often" deceived.[^lumen-gentium] [[Invincible Ignorance Excuses Unbelief]] sets out that teaching.
- The figures are rough. The 22-country item counts several gods and an impersonal force as belief, wider than Maitzen's "supreme, personal God" (2006, p. 179).[^aglozo-2025][^maitzen-2006] In the 2008 international survey, about 70 per cent of the variation in belief lay within countries.[^ishii-2024] The Saudi figure is from 2003 and was asked face to face. In Saudi law, conversion from Islam is grounds for a charge of apostasy, legally punishable by death, though courts have not carried out such a sentence in many years.[^usdos-2024] Retention measures identity, and some countries, Egypt among them, penalise leaving Islam by law.[^pew-2025-switching]
- It says which view the evidence favours, not which is true. Maitzen says it does not show theistic belief irrational (2008, p. 477), and Blanton that it gives no verdict on theism all things considered (2025, n. 3).[^maitzen-2008][^blanton-2025]
- It concerns present countries. Maitzen sets the spread of belief over time aside (n. 1).[^maitzen-2006] Belief among foragers and early humans is a separate question, with its own evidence in [[Peoples, Duda & Marlowe 2016]].
