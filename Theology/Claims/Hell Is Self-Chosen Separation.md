# Description
Hell is the state of living apart from God's presence, which is what a person who rejects him has chosen, not a torture chamber God sends people to. [[C. S. Lewis|Lewis]] summed it up by saying the doors of hell are locked from the inside: no one is dragged there against their will, they receive the separation from God they insisted on. Since heaven simply is life in God's presence, someone who genuinely does not want God would not want heaven either. On this view God is not coercing love or punishing honest doubt. He is respecting a person's freedom enough to let them have the life without him that they chose.

# Origins
The doors locked from the inside image is Lewis's, in *The Problem of Pain* (1940), ch. 8, and *The Great Divorce* (1945). It is Lewis rather than scripture and should be cited as his. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* calls hell "definitive self-exclusion from communion with God".[^catechism] [[Jerry Walls]] and [[Richard Swinburne]] are the usual philosophical names for hell as a freely embraced condition rather than an imposed punishment.

Needs sources. Lewis's two books and Walls's *Hell: The Logic of Damnation* were not opened for this node.

- [Matthew 25:41,46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2025:41,46&version=NIV) eternal separation/punishment.

- [Matthew 7:23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207:23&version=NIV) "I never knew you. Away from me."
- [Luke 13:25,27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2013:25,27&version=NIV) the owner shuts the door and says he doesn't know them.
- [Revelation 3:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation%203:20&version=NIV) God knocks; the human chooses to open the door.
- [[God Can't Accept Sin]] gives the reason the separation exists at all.

# Carries
- Hell is not a penalty God adds to refusal. It is what refusal is. So an objection built on the size of the penalty has to argue instead that the choice was not free or not informed.
- It makes what is judged a settled orientation of the will rather than a verdict on a question someone got wrong.
- It removes the sanction from the picture. If there is no penalty added, a step toward God is not taken under threat, and an objection that fear of hell compels belief loses its target.

# Disputed By
- The penal reading of the same texts. The New Testament uses active punishment language, fire, weeping, and in [Matthew 25:41](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2025:41,46&version=NIV) a place prepared, and Swinburne's own account keeps the pain of hell as a threat God leaves in place.[^swinburne-1998]
- [[No Finite Life Deserves Infinite Punishment]] presses the size of the outcome. This claim answers by denying it is a penalty at all, which only works if the texts allow the reading.
- [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]] someone who never rejected God, only failed to be convinced, has not chosen anything. A choice made without adequate information is not the informed refusal this claim describes.
- Needs sources. The universalist objection that a fully informed and free person could not choose separation. [[Thomas Talbott]] is the usual name and was not opened for this node.

# Limits
- The separation reading has to treat the punishment language as metaphor while treating the separation language as literal, and it does not have an independent rule for which is which.
- "Chose it" only works for someone who understood what they were refusing. It does not cover the honest doubter, see [[Some Unbelief Isn't Resistance]], which is the case the claim is most often used against.
- A choice made under an eternal cost is not obviously a free one. If refusing God carries the loss of everything, the freedom this claim rests on is under pressure from the cost itself.
- The doors locked from the inside line is Lewis's image, not a biblical statement, and it does more work in popular use than the texts support.

[^catechism]: *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, §1033.
[^swinburne-1998]: Richard Swinburne, *Providence and the Problem of Evil* (Clarendon Press, 1998).
