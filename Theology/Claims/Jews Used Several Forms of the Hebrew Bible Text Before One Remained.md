# Description
Before the first century AD the Hebrew text of the Torah and of several other biblical books existed in more than one form, and different Jewish groups used different forms.[^tov-2016][^tov-2017-essence] On [[Emanuel Tov]]'s account one of them, the forerunner of the [[Masoretic Text]], was the text of the Temple circles and the Pharisees.[^tov-2009][^tov-2017-essence] Its copies from about 50 BC on agree almost completely in their consonants with the medieval text, and Tov concludes that it was copied with great precision.[^tov-2017-essence] By the end of the first century AD it was the only Hebrew text left in Jewish use.[^tov-2017-essence][^tov-2009][^tov-2017-socio]

# Origins
Before the [[Dead Sea Scrolls|Qumran]] finds, many scholars divided the texts of the Hebrew Bible into three types: the Masoretic Text (MT), the [[Samaritan Pentateuch]] and the [[Septuagint]].[^tov-2017-outside] Tov reports that [[Paul Kahle]], in 1915, traced the three to revisions of three base texts of the Torah.[^tov-2016] In Tov's words, Kahle saw the Samaritan text as a "popularizing vulgar recension" and the Masoretic text as "an official and reliable recension".[^tov-2016] A theory of local texts, which Tov cites from articles of 1955 and 1975, tied the Masoretic Text to Babylon, the Septuagint to Egypt and the Samaritan Pentateuch to Palestine.[^tov-2016][^cross-1975] The 1955 article placed the first in Babylonia and the Hebrew behind the Septuagint in Egypt.[^albright-1955] It held that a scroll of Exodus close to the Samaritan text sprang "from the proto-Massoretic of Qumran".[^albright-1955] In 1982, by his own account, Tov proposed instead "an endless number of texts in each of the biblical books".[^tov-2016]

# Based On
- **Book by book.** Tov counts "ten to twelve different textual branches" of the Torah from the third century BC on, against one, two or three for the other books.[^tov-2016] He finds a single tradition in Judges, Job, Ruth, Qohelet and Lamentations.[^tov-2016] Jeremiah circulated in two versions, and Hebrew fragments of both survive from Qumran.[^schiffman-1994-jeremiah]
- **Beyond the Qumran group.** Tov holds that the Greek Torah "was a Jewish enterprise" and "was based on a different Hebrew text".[^tov-2017-other] Texts of the Samaritan type, without the Samaritans' few sectarian readings, were found at Qumran.[^tov-2009] From them Tov infers that the type was held as "an authoritative Jewish text".[^tov-2017-other] The Nash Papyrus, a liturgical sheet from Egypt of the second century BC, has a wording "closer to that of the Septuagint".[^cambridge-nash][^eshel-2015]
- **Qumran as a sample.** Tov estimates that "probably not more than thirty percent" of the Qumran texts were copied by the community's own scribes, the rest being brought from outside.[^tov-2008-texts] Their coexistence, he writes, "shows that no fixed text or textual family had been accepted as the central text for the country as a whole".[^tov-2008-texts] He adds that this "may be misleading", since in temple circles the Masoretic family "probably was the only acceptable text".[^tov-2008-texts] Tov reads the contrast with Masada in the same decades as one between groups, not periods.[^tov-2017-socio]
- **The one line.** Twenty-five biblical texts from Judean Desert sites outside Qumran, copied from about 50 BC to AD 115, agree almost completely in their consonants with the [[Leningrad Codex]].[^tov-2017-essence]
- **Before and after AD 70.** On Tov's account the line was "the most frequently used text in ancient Israel" from the third century BC.[^tov-2009] Before the destruction of the Temple it was still "one of the main texts used but not the only one".[^tov-2009] All Jewish streams accepted its consonantal text "probably in the 1st century CE".[^tov-2017-essence]

# Carries
- It dates the consonants of the medieval Masoretic Text to around the turn of the era.[^tov-2017-outside]
- It makes the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Qumran scrolls witnesses to Hebrew texts that Jews once used.[^tov-2017-other][^tov-2009]

# Disputed By
- **One standard text all along.** [[Peter J. Gentry]] argues in the *Southern Baptist Journal of Theology* (2020) that Qumran gives "the evidence of one sect". "In the larger picture," he writes, "there is a central stream dominated by the proto-Masoretic texts".[^gentry-2020] Research on the scribes' handwriting, he writes, "has shown that the same scribe produced both types of manuscripts", conservative and facilitating (p. 74). For him the two models are therefore "complementary", and the facilitating texts "presuppose a standard".[^gentry-2020] On his account the text only looks standardised after AD 70 because updating it for its readers moved from Hebrew copies into the Aramaic [[Targum|Targums]].[^gentry-2020] He concludes that "the consensus view that the text was standardized in the first century AD is wrong" (p. 76). "There was a standard text all along," he writes (p. 77).[^gentry-2020]
- **One type dominant by the revolt.** [[Lawrence H. Schiffman]] writes in *Reclaiming the Dead Sea Scrolls* (1994) that at Qumran "The proto-Masoretic text type was dominant".[^schiffman-1994-psalms] Beside it, he writes, stood "texts of sectarian-type as well as a few proto-Samaritan or Septuagintal-type texts".[^schiffman-1994-psalms] From the Masada finds he concludes that the Masoretic text "had essentially become the only recognized biblical text by the period of the revolt".[^schiffman-1994-masada]
- **One text by the late first century.** [[Ian Young]] questions in *Dead Sea Discoveries* (2002) the usual date at which the Qumran scrolls were put in the caves.[^young-2002][^young-2004][^tov-2008-texts] He finds "the arguments for textual diversity in the first century to explain the Masada scrolls" unconvincing.[^young-2004] He holds that "all the various groups we know of in Judaism (not the Samaritans) shared a common Biblical text by the late first century". "Different sects don't have to have different Bibles," he adds.[^young-2004] He puts the end of stabilisation "between the mid-first century BCE (Qumran) and the late first century CE (Masada)".[^young-2004]

# Limits
- The claim turns on whether Jewish groups other than the Qumran community used Hebrew texts of another type before the first century AD. Tov holds that they did.[^tov-2017-essence] He also writes that Jews began to see the Greek as problematic before Christianity, "since it did not reflect the proto-MT text current in Palestine".[^tov-2017-other] The other Judean Desert sites begin about 50 BC and hold only the forerunner.[^tov-2017-essence][^tov-2017-socio] For earlier decades the sources cite one Hebrew text of another type from outside Qumran: the Nash Papyrus, a liturgical sheet bought from an Egyptian dealer in 1902.[^eshel-2015][^cambridge-nash] Of similar liturgical texts at Qumran, Tov writes that they "carried authority as liturgical texts, but not as Scripture texts" (p. 18).[^tov-2016][^eshel-2015] The rest is inferred from the Greek and Samaritan texts and from Qumran, where each scroll's origin "remains hypothetical".[^tov-2008-synagogue] Biblical manuscripts from other Jewish sites, copied before 50 BC, would settle it.
- The precision is shown for the consonants, on what survives. Vowel signs, accents and marginal notes were written down only from the ninth century on.[^tov-2017-essence] Only "possibly five percent" of the line survives from the Judean Desert, and Tov infers that the rest matched.[^tov-2017-outside] Earlier care is also his inference: it "probably preceded the time of our earliest evidence, namely the third century BCE" (p. 24).[^tov-2017-essence]
- Exact copying is not fidelity to an original. The scribes "copied their scrolls faithfully, but these scrolls inherited an earlier tradition that was not always precise or consistent".[^tov-2017-socio]
- Nothing here shows that the other lines were copied less carefully. The agreement test "can be examined for the MT group and not for most non-MT texts".[^tov-2017-scribes]
- Why this line alone remained is disputed. Tov writes that "Grosso modo, MT is an excellent text, and it is therefore no coincidence that this text has become the central text of Judaism" (p. 24).[^tov-2017-essence] Elsewhere he holds that after AD 70 "nascent rabbinic Judaism was the only surviving form of Judaism".[^tov-2009] He reports that "many, if not most, scholars" see "a conscious and organized effort to stabilize the text of Scripture".[^tov-2017-socio]
- The one text held only within Judaism: on Tov's account the Samaritans and Christians, who kept the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Septuagint, had left it by then.[^tov-2017-socio]
- The claim concerns the text of each book, not [[The Bible's Core Was in Use Early and Its Edges Stayed Open for Centuries|which books belonged]].
- Counts of forms depend on the classification. Schiffman, and Tov in 2008, call the Qumran texts close to the medieval text "proto-Masoretic", and Tov then called them "the largest group at Qumran".[^schiffman-1994-psalms][^tov-2008-synagogue] His 2017 articles call them "MT-like" and find no proto-MT at Qumran.[^tov-2017-socio] For the Torah, Tov in 2016 finds "more texts of block II than of block I at Qumran", block I being the Masoretic line.[^tov-2016]
- Tov also rejects an organised standardisation: textual stability "should not be confused with an organized process of stabilization".[^tov-2017-socio] Among the sources found, the view that one standard text held all along, with Qumran one sect, appears only in confessional journals. Gentry states it in a 2020 article and in a 2008 address to the Evangelical Theological Society, printed in its journal in 2009.[^gentry-2020][^gentry-2009] No statement of the view outside a confessional venue was found.
