# Description
A comparative study of religious traits across 33 hunter-gatherer societies, coding which beliefs are present in each and reconstructing which came first. Reported frequencies: animism 100%, afterlife belief 79%, shamanism 79%, ancestor worship 45%, high gods 39%, high gods active in human affairs 15%. "High gods" is coded as a single all-powerful creator, so roughly six in ten of the sampled societies had no creator concept at all. The authors argue animism is the base state and everything else is layered on later.

# Shows
- A creator deity is not the default output of the human mind. Spirits and animate nature are.
- The gap between "people reach for agents" and "people arrive at one creator" is real and measurable, which is the gap [[Creation Alone Doesn't Point to God]] turns on.
- Anything claiming a creator is plain to everyone from nature has to account for the 61%.

# Limits
- Ethnographic coding of 33 recent societies, not a reconstruction of prehistory. These are contemporary or recently documented foragers, all of whom have had some contact with outside cultures.
- The paper itself reports high gods as the worst-fitting trait on its phylogeny and highly changeable, so the 39% is the least stable number in the set.
- "High god" is a coding category. Societies with a vague distant creator figure may be counted either way depending on the ethnographer.
- Matthew Braddock, [Natural Nonbelief in God](https://philpapers.org/rec/BRANNI), argues the empirical jury is still out on what prehistoric humans believed. Do not use this study to settle that question.

# Source
Hervey C. Peoples, Pavel Duda and Frank W. Marlowe, [Hunter-Gatherers and the Origins of Religion](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12110-016-9260-0.pdf), *Human Nature* 27 (2016), 261–282.
