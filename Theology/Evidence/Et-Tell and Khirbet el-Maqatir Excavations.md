# Description
Et-Tell and Khirbet el-Maqatir are two mounds about a kilometre apart in the hills north of Jerusalem.[^wood-2012][^kennedy-2011] Each has been proposed as the Ai of [Joshua 7–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%207-8&version=NIV), which [Joshua 7:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%207:2&version=NIV) places near Beth Aven, east of Bethel. The archaeologist William Albright identified Ai with et-Tell in 1924, and most scholars followed him.[^bimson-2025]

Judith Marquet-Krause, who directed the excavation of 1933 to 1935, gives the mound as 10 hectares.[^marquet-krause-1935] John Garstang had dug test trenches there in 1928, mostly outside the city walls, and Joseph Callaway dug there from 1964 to 1972.[^kennedy-2011] Marquet-Krause found a walled city of the Early Bronze Age. Its palace lay under ash up to 1.2 metres thick, and she dated the fire to about 2000 BCE.[^marquet-krause-1935] Above it she found [[Iron Age|Iron I]] remains on the acropolis and east of it, over about 0.25 hectare. She dated them to 100 or 150 years around 1200 to 1050 BCE. The Iron I builders reused standing walls of the palace. She reports no [[Middle Bronze Age|Middle Bronze]] or [[Late Bronze Age|Late Bronze]] remains.[^marquet-krause-1935]

Callaway wrote in 1968 that the earliest occupation after the Early Bronze destruction was Iron I and that there was no evidence of Late Bronze occupation.[^kennedy-2011] In the figures [[Titus Kennedy]] quotes from him, the Early Bronze city covered 27.5 acres and the Iron I village 2.75 acres.[^kennedy-2011] Garstang's trenches of 1928 gave Late Bronze I sherds, among them a Cypriot handle, and no local ware later than 1400 BCE, as he reported it.[^kennedy-2011] Marquet-Krause's full report appeared in 1949.[^kennedy-2011]

Khirbet el-Maqatir lies 1.5 kilometres south-east of Beitin.[^kennedy-2011] [[Israel Finkelstein]] surveyed it on 13 December 1981 and was the first to report Middle Bronze and Iron I remains there.[^stripling-hassler-2023][^wood-2012] In 1994 [[Bryant G. Wood]] investigated it for the Associates for Biblical Research as an alternative to et-Tell, and excavation began on 20 May 1995.[^stripling-hassler-2023] Wood directed until 2013 and Scott Stripling for the last three years. Fifteen seasons ran over 22 years, with none from 2001 to 2008.[^stripling-hassler-2023]

The site is about two hectares.[^stripling-hassler-2023] The excavators' table runs a Bronze Age stratum (Stratum 7) from Middle Bronze IIIA to Late Bronze IB, about 1650 to 1406 BCE. An abandonment follows, from about 1406 to 1187 BCE, and then an Iron Age stratum (Stratum 6) from Iron I to Iron IIB, about 1187 to 701 BCE.[^stripling-hassler-2023]

Wood's 2012 summary describes a fortress of about one hectare on the south-east slope, built on bedrock. The foundations of its north and west walls are four metres wide, and half of a two-chambered gate was found.[^wood-2012] An infant jar burial beside one of the walls inside the gate is dated to about 1500 BCE. The summary says the fortress ended in a fire, shown by reddened and fragmented bedrock in the gate passage, burned stones, ash deposits and refired Late Bronze IB pottery. It dates that end to about 1400 BCE from the latest pottery.[^wood-2012] His 2014 report adds a scarab found in 2013 beside four refired Late Bronze I sherds. He assigns it most likely to the Egyptian king Amenhotep II, about 1455 to 1418 BCE.[^wood-2014]

Kennedy supervised two seasons at Maqatir. He describes the burning as "a few small patches of ash up to 10 cm in thickness" with burned or refired Late Bronze I pottery. He reports no well preserved layer showing a fire.[^kennedy-2011] The final report is in two volumes. Volume 2, on the later periods, appeared in 2023, and Volume 1, on the Bronze and Iron Ages, has not appeared.[^stripling-hassler-2023][^bimson-2025]

# Shows
- Et-Tell held a walled Early Bronze city that burned. Its excavators of the 1930s and 1960s report no Middle or Late Bronze town in the areas they dug. The next settlement is a small Iron I one from about 1200 BCE.[^marquet-krause-1935][^kennedy-2011]
- [[Amihai Mazar]] puts the Early Bronze destruction "more than one thousand years" before the Iron I village was built above it.[^finkelstein-mazar-2007]
- Maqatir held a Bronze Age fortress with walls four metres wide on the north and west. On its excavators' table the site stood empty from about 1406 to 1187 BCE.[^wood-2012][^stripling-hassler-2023]
- Ash and refired pottery lie in the fortress's Late Bronze I contexts. Wood reads them as the fire that destroyed the fortress. Kennedy writes that burning from later periods and kitchen pits are alternative explanations and that the cause is not yet determined.[^wood-2012][^kennedy-2011]
- Robert Mullins, in a 2015 chapter on Israel's emergence, reads the fortress pottery Wood has published as late Middle Bronze, as John Bimson quotes him. Bimson places the conquest at the end of the Middle Bronze Age. He takes this reading to put the fortress's end in the same context as the fall of other Middle Bronze cities. His chapter treats [[Jericho Excavations|Jericho]], Ai and [[Hazor Excavations|Hazor]] in that order.[^bimson-2025]
- Mazar describes et-Tell as "the only prominent site east of Bethel" in setting out its identification with Ai. Wood holds that Maqatir meets all the geographic and archaeological requirements for Ai.[^finkelstein-mazar-2007][^wood-2012] The 2023 volume calls et-Tell the likely site of the Ai of the patriarchal narratives and Maqatir the best candidate for the Ai of the Late Bronze Age.[^stripling-hassler-2023]

# Limits
- Which site is Ai is assumed either way. Mazar states his verdict on et-Tell as conditional on the identification. Wood's case for Maqatir needs Bethel at el-Bireh. Kennedy concludes that Ai and its conquest "must remain an open question", since no inscription from the region names the site.[^finkelstein-mazar-2007][^bimson-2025][^kennedy-2011]
- The geography turns on where Bethel stood. Beitin is generally taken for Bethel and suits et-Tell, and el-Bireh, which David Livingston and Wood favour, suits Maqatir.[^kennedy-2011][^bimson-2025] No excavation at el-Bireh is reported. Surveys of its acropolis found Early Bronze, Middle Bronze, Iron I, Iron II and later pottery. Kennedy notes that none was Late Bronze, and Bimson takes the finds to raise the odds that it was Bethel.[^kennedy-2011][^bimson-2025]
- Neither mound is fully dug. The mound of et-Tell covers 10 hectares. Marquet-Krause's 1934 report counts about 0.45 hectare of the lower city and 0.25 hectare of Iron Age remains cleared, besides the palace and a sanctuary.[^marquet-krause-1935] Callaway's total was not found.
- Kennedy sees reason, which he calls small, to think that a Middle Bronze and a Late Bronze I settlement stood at or near et-Tell, perhaps on its slope. He knows of no digging there beyond Garstang's trenches, and he reads the sherds from the main excavations as probably residual.[^kennedy-2011]
- Yeivin, a member of Marquet-Krause's staff, wrote later that rim sherds first called Middle Bronze I belong to the transition from Early to Middle Bronze. One sherd he took for Late Bronze I may belong there too, as Kennedy quotes him. Kennedy also cites her 1949 report for Middle Bronze I sherds.[^kennedy-2011] She herself found Iron I sherds mixed with Early Bronze ones.[^marquet-krause-1935]
- Marquet-Krause's dates are from 1935. She put the fire about 2000 BCE and the gap to the Iron I village at 800 years, against Mazar's "more than one thousand years".[^marquet-krause-1935][^finkelstein-mazar-2007] Needs sources: Callaway's own absolute dates, in his 1980 volume and in his 1993 entry in *The New Encyclopedia of Archaeological Excavations in the Holy Land*. A search snippet gives about 2400 BCE for the Early Bronze end and about 1220 BCE for the Iron I village. His 1968 article, as Kennedy quotes it, names the destruction Early Bronze IIIA.
- Needs sources: the re-excavation of et-Tell reported by Hani Nur el-Din for the seasons of 2003, 2005 and 2007. He reported it at the 6th International Congress on the Archaeology of the Ancient Near East in Rome in 2008. Only a bibliography entry was seen.
- Maqatir's Bronze Age remains lie near the surface and were damaged by ploughing and probing, by the excavators' own account.[^stripling-hassler-2023] Wood adds robbing, farming and erosion, and Kennedy notes that sherds of several periods often lie in one locus.[^wood-2012][^kennedy-2011]
- The excavators' phase tables differ. Wood's 2012 table gives the Middle Bronze as pottery only, about 1600 to 1500 BCE. It gives the fortress as Late Bronze I on about one hectare, and Iron I squatters until about 1100 BCE.[^wood-2012] The 2023 volume gives a small fortress from Middle Bronze III and a Bronze Age stratum from about 1650 BCE. It gives a site of two hectares and an Iron Age stratum to 701 BCE.[^stripling-hassler-2023]
- The fire date is a pottery date. None of the Maqatir texts read here gives a radiocarbon date for the burning, and Kennedy notes that no thermoluminescence test had been made.[^wood-2012][^stripling-hassler-2023][^kennedy-2011] Wood's 2014 report adopts 1406 to 1400 BCE as the end of Late Bronze I. He derives that conquest date from the Bible's own chronology.[^wood-2014] The 2023 table ends the Bronze Age stratum at 1406.[^stripling-hassler-2023]
- Kennedy reports that several Israeli archaeologists who visited the site thought the proposed gate a Byzantine wine press, and he judges it likely that no gate has been found. Wood reads the remains as the west half of a two-chambered gate. The 2023 volume calls them the Late Bronze gate, reused as a wine press in the Late Hellenistic and Early Roman periods.[^kennedy-2011][^wood-2012][^stripling-hassler-2023]
- Wood dates the scarab to Amenhotep II. Bimson calls that attribution tentative at best and reports that the excavation's consultant Daphna Ben-Tor dates it more widely, to 1550 to 1450 BCE.[^bimson-2025]
- Mullins's reading is known here only as Bimson quotes it, and Bimson defends a conquest at the end of the Middle Bronze Age. He writes that a full independent assessment of the fortress pottery and stratigraphy must await the final report, which is Volume 1.[^bimson-2025]
- The pottery date, the fortress's extent and its phases come from the excavators, who dug the site to explore alternatives to et-Tell for Ai.[^stripling-hassler-2023] Needs sources: an assessment of the Maqatir fortress by archaeologists outside the Associates for Biblical Research, and Mullins 2015 in its own words.
