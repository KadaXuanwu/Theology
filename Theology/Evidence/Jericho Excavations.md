# Description
Tell es-Sultan, the mound of ancient Jericho, has a walled [[Middle Bronze Age|Middle Bronze]] city that ended in a fire. Radiocarbon on charred grain dates that fire to the late 17th or the 16th century BCE.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995][^nigro-yasine-2024] Charles Warren made exploratory excavations there in 1867 and 1868.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] An Austro-German expedition under Ernst Sellin and Carl Watzinger dug in 1907 to 1909 and in 1911, and published in 1913.[^sellin-watzinger-1913] John Garstang dug from 1930 to 1936 and reported in the *Palestine Exploration Fund Quarterly Statement* and the *Liverpool Annals of Archaeology and Anthropology*.[^garstang-1930] Kathleen Kenyon dug from 1952 to 1958. She published *Digging Up Jericho* in 1957 and a volume on the architecture and stratigraphy of the tell in 1981.[^kenyon-1957][^kenyon-holland-1981] A joint expedition of Sapienza University of Rome and the Palestinian Department of Antiquities and Cultural Heritage dug four seasons from 1997 to 2000.[^nigro-1997-2000] Its interim report on the 15th to 19th seasons, 2019 to 2023, is by Lorenzo Nigro of Sapienza and Jehad Yasine of the Palestinian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities.[^nigro-yasine-2024]

The final Middle Bronze city is the last phase of Garstang's City IV. The current team calls it Sultan IVc and dates it to 1650 to 1550 BCE.[^nigro-yasine-2024][^windle-2026] Hendrik Bruins of Ben-Gurion University of the Negev and Johannes van der Plicht of the University of Groningen dated samples from its final destruction debris in Kenyon's Area H. They measured 16 samples, 18 dates in all, at Groningen in 1991 and 1992.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] Six samples of short lived charred grain gave a weighted average of 3306 ± 7 radiocarbon years BP. Charcoal from the same debris averaged 3370 ± 6 BP once one outlying date was set aside.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] The authors called their calibration preliminary. On the 1993 decadal curve with the program OxCal, the grain date falls with equal probability in 1601 to 1566 or in 1561 to 1524 BCE at one standard deviation. They conclude that the final destruction fell in the late 17th or the 16th century BCE.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995]

The current team's latest samples from House A3, at the southern foot of the tell, date the final destruction of the Middle Bronze city to 1550 to 1525 BCE. Charred seeds from jars smashed in the collapse of the earlier Sultan IVb palace in Area G give 1635 BCE for that palace's end. Samples from the destruction layers over the rebuilt palace, which Garstang named the Hyksos Palace, suggest about 1530 BCE.[^nigro-yasine-2024]

Garstang dated the end of City IV to the [[Late Bronze Age]], at about 1400 BCE, and tied it to the biblical conquest.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995][^dickin-2024][^kennedy-2011] He argued from 18th Dynasty scarabs, among them two of Amenhotep III in a tomb, and from the absence of 14th century Mycenaean pottery. On that evidence he held that the city stood until about 1400 BCE.[^dickin-2024][^kennedy-2011] Watzinger had concluded in 1926 that the city fell by 1600 BCE at the latest.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] Kenyon assigned City IV to the Middle Bronze Age and, on Bruins and van der Plicht's account, ended it about 1580 BCE.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995]

Garstang named a building on the Spring Hill the Middle Building and found Late Bronze painted pottery in and around it, and a cuneiform tablet.[^nigro-yasine-2024][^windle-2026] Kenyon wrote in 1957 that no fragment of the walls of the Late Bronze city survives.[^kenyon-1957] In Area G the current team excavated parts of a Late Bronze building over the burnt Hyksos Palace, with walls W.2303 and W.2333 and floor L.2312. It dug two rooms that it takes to be annexes of Garstang's Middle Building. It dates the building by its pottery to the 15th century BCE. A fragment of a Cypriot White Slip II milk bowl is placed in the second half of that century. The report gives no date for the building's end. It says a later [[Iron Age|Iron I]] house was built on wall W.2333 and reused burnt Middle and Late Bronze bricks. Its section heading calls the building Late Bronze I to II, and a later section labels W.2333 Sultan Vb, Late Bronze II.[^nigro-yasine-2024] The 2019 and 2020 season pages say the building was destroyed and reused in the Early Iron Age, and give no date for the destruction.[^nigro-2019]

In Areas A and E at the southern foot of the tell the team found orange mudbricks on top of the Cyclopean Wall. That stone wall supported the Middle Bronze III rampart. Some of the bricks were in place and some had fallen. The team assigns them to Sultan Va, Late Bronze I, 1550 to 1400 BCE, after the final destruction of Sultan IVc, and calls them scanty. A footnote lists Sellin and Watzinger, Garstang and Kenyon as earlier excavators who identified a Late Bronze mudbrick city wall.[^nigro-yasine-2024] Windle quotes a 2023 chapter by Nigro on a Late Bronze II wall 2 m wide. In that quotation the wall is set back 20 to 30 cm from the front edge of the Cyclopean Wall.[^windle-2026][^nigro-2023-lb]

[[Bryant G. Wood|Wood]] writes that no 13th century pottery has been found at Jericho, in tombs or on the tell. He cites a Spring Hill sequence with a gap of about 1320 to 1100 BCE.[^wood-2005][^marchetti-2003] The expedition's report on its 1997 to 2000 seasons says it found no Late Bronze pottery on the tell. It adds that later razing in Areas G and F had removed all strata down to the Middle or even the Early Bronze Age.[^nigro-1997-2000] Nigro's 2023 overview describes the last century of the Late Bronze Age as a "dramatic draught of archaeological evidence".[^nigro-2023-icaane]

# Shows
The walled Middle Bronze city on the mound burned in the late 17th or the 16th century BCE. Short lived grain from Kenyon's excavation gives that range, and the current team's own samples give 1550 to 1525 BCE.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995][^nigro-yasine-2024] Bruins and van der Plicht state that the fortified city was not destroyed by about 1400 BCE, which was Wood's date for it in 1990. They add that their dates do not support a low 15th century end for the Middle Bronze Age.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995]

Garstang, Wood and [[Titus Kennedy|Kennedy]] read the end of the same city at about 1400 BCE. Wood argued in 1990 from pottery and from a British Museum radiocarbon date for a Kenyon sample, BM-1790, which he reported as 1410 ± 40 BCE.[^wood-1990][^kennedy-2011] Kennedy holds the date in a 2011 thesis, from pottery and Egyptian scarabs.[^kennedy-2011] A 2023 paper by Kennedy reassesses the pottery, scarabs, a cuneiform tablet and radiocarbon samples. Windle reads it as defending Sultan IV as the last Canaanite occupation.[^kennedy-2023][^windle-2026]

Jericho was occupied in the Late Bronze Age. The current team's finds are a 15th century building over the burnt palace and scanty Late Bronze I mudbrick on the Cyclopean Wall.[^nigro-yasine-2024] No report from the current team dates a destruction of the Late Bronze town.[^nigro-yasine-2024][^nigro-2023-icaane]

How large and how defended that town was is read two ways. Piotr Bienkowski, who studied the Late Bronze remains for a 1986 monograph, concluded that it was a small unwalled settlement. He limited it to the area of the Middle Building and dated it from about 1425 to about 1275 BCE.[^bienkowski-1986] Nigro describes a town with weaker fortifications than the Middle Bronze city, a mudbrick wall on top of the Cyclopean Wall.[^nigro-2023-icaane]

Little excavated at Jericho dates to the 13th century BCE. Wood reports none, and Nigro's 2023 overview describes the last century of the Late Bronze Age as a dramatic draught of evidence.[^wood-2005][^nigro-2023-icaane]

# Limits
- The end of the Late Bronze town is undated. The 2024 interim report dates the building by pottery to the 15th century and gives no date for its end.[^nigro-yasine-2024] Windle quotes a 2021 talk by Nigro that calls the end of the Late Bronze "some kind of conflagration", not clearly documented. He writes that Nigro has not dated it.[^windle-2026] Windle argues that this town is Joshua's Jericho and infers a fire in the 14th century. He reports that Nigro's 2025 book puts the fall of the town's walls in the second half of the 14th century and blames an earthquake.[^windle-2026][^nigro-2025] He also cites a layer of burnt debris that Garstang drew at the foot of the Cyclopean Wall. He cites a destruction phase in a Late Bronze house in Kenyon's Area H, which he says Kenyon dated to the 14th century.[^windle-2026] Needs sources. Nigro 2025, Kenyon and Holland 1981 and Garstang 1930 were not opened, so what each says of the date, the cause and any fire is unchecked.
- The radiocarbon dates carry their own limits. Bruins and van der Plicht call the calibration preliminary. Their grain date sits on a flat stretch of every curve they used, so a precise BP date becomes a spread of decades in calendar years.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] The link between the samples and the final destruction rests on Kenyon's phase assignments in her 1981 volume.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] The 2024 report prints no laboratory numbers or calibrated ranges for the House A3, palace and Hyksos Palace samples, and no radiocarbon date for the Late Bronze building.[^nigro-yasine-2024] Nigro's 2023 overview cites Bruins and van der Plicht for 1550 to 1520 BCE, a range that does not appear in the text read.[^nigro-2023-icaane] Southern Levant plants show a radiocarbon offset from the standard curve that fluctuates by a few years to several decades.[^manning-2018] Needs sources. A recalibration of the 1995 grain average on a current curve. Kennedy 2023 reportedly gives one for single samples and was not opened.
- Kennedy argues that pottery is the primary and most trusted dating method. He adds that charcoal in a burn layer can come from wood cut more than a century earlier.[^kennedy-2011] The charcoal here averages 64 radiocarbon years older than the grain.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] Kennedy reports that BM-1790 was later reissued as 3300 ± 110 BP after a laboratory fault. He reports two samples from washed down debris, tested by the expedition in 2000, at 1347 ± 85 and 1597 ± 91 BCE. He holds that the Jericho radiocarbon results cannot decide between about 1550 and about 1400.[^kennedy-2011] Needs sources. Bowman, Ambers and Leese 1990 and Marchetti and Nigro 2000 for those figures, not opened.
- The pottery dates rest on Egyptian chronology. Bruins and van der Plicht write that Kenyon's date was not based on firm chronological evidence but on an assumed link with the start of Dynasty XVIII. They add that this date has moved from about 1580 to 1539 BCE.[^bruins-vanderplicht-1995] The current team's Late Bronze divisions are the conventional pottery periods, and the radiocarbon its Table 1 cites concerns the Early Bronze Age.[^nigro-yasine-2024]
- The date of the Middle Building has moved. Kennedy reports that Garstang, Kenyon and Bienkowski dated it to Late Bronze II, about the late 14th or early 13th century BCE. He reports that Garstang in 1941 said it was not linked to the period of City IV.[^kennedy-2011] The current team dates the pottery from its annexes to the 15th century and says the same of Garstang's tablet and most of his pottery. Its White Slip II bowl, in the second half of that century, falls inside the team's own Late Bronze IB, 1470 to 1400 BCE.[^nigro-yasine-2024] Windle reports Nigro 2020 as dating the Middle Building's use to Late Bronze IIB and as admitting a scarcity of 13th century material.[^windle-2026] Needs sources. Nigro 2020 and Bienkowski 1986 for the building's range.
- The cause of the Middle Bronze fire is not settled by the excavation. The expedition's 1997 to 2000 report says there is no available evidence to attribute the destruction to an enemy and that the city was abandoned for several centuries.[^nigro-1997-2000] Nigro's 2023 overview says the city was destroyed by an enemy attack, possibly Ahmose or Amenhotep I, and that it became a town again from about 1500 BCE.[^nigro-2023-icaane] Windle puts the gap at 100 to 150 years.[^windle-2026]
- The Late Bronze wall rests on a reading of earlier sections. Kenyon's statement of 1957 that no Late Bronze city wall survives sits against the 2024 report. That report cites her 1981 volume for a Late Bronze mudbrick wall in Trenches I to III.[^kenyon-1957][^nigro-yasine-2024] Windle writes that Kenyon dated those remains to the Middle Bronze city and that Nigro in 2023 redated them.[^windle-2026] Needs sources. Kenyon 1957, read here only as search snippets, and Kenyon and Holland 1981.
- The size of the Late Bronze town is an extrapolation. Windle quotes Nigro 2023 for a wall circuit that "should be" about 850 m, from the earlier excavators' reports, and an area that "could be around 4.5 hectares".[^windle-2026] Bienkowski's reading dates from 1986, before the current team's building and wall remains.[^bienkowski-1986] Needs sources. Bienkowski 1986, section 7.8 and the section on the town's extent, read only as snippets, and Nigro 2023 itself.
- Erosion and razing limit what survives. Kenyon wrote that very little wash from the Late Bronze town has been found, and gave the Middle Bronze rampart crowning the crest as the probable reason.[^kenyon-1957] [[Kenneth Kitchen|Kitchen]], as Wood quotes him, holds that anything from 1275 to 1220 BCE has "long, long since gone". Wood answers that pottery washes to the base of a tell and would be found.[^wood-2005] The 1997 to 2000 report says later razing removed strata in Areas G and F down to the Middle or Early Bronze Age.[^nigro-1997-2000] That report is undated, and the Late Bronze building in Area G was excavated from 2019.[^nigro-yasine-2024] Needs sources. Kenyon 1957 for the sentence on the wash, which was seen as a snippet that breaks off.
- No source read states what fraction of the mound has been dug. Kennedy writes that Kenyon used extremely narrow trenches and dug only a very small percentage of the tell compared with Garstang.[^kennedy-2011] A find of nothing means nothing in the places dug. The 2024 report is interim, and Windle writes that Nigro has not published a final report.[^windle-2026]
- Windle's paper is a short communication by an author who argues for a conquest between 1426 and 1346 BCE. It is the only source seen for Nigro 2025.[^windle-2026]
