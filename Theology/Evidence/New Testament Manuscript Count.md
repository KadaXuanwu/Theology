# Description
The Institute for New Testament Textual Research in Münster keeps the standard register of Greek New Testament manuscripts, the Liste. It held about 5,800 entries as of 2017, from scraps of papyrus to complete medieval codices. The earliest, [[Papyrus P52]], is a fragment. Beyond the Greek there are roughly 10,000 Latin manuscripts and further thousands in Syriac, Coptic, Armenian and other versions. For comparison, Clay Jones's 2012 recount of the manuscripts of Caesar's *Bellum Gallicum*, written between 51 and 46 BC, reached 251, the earliest from the 9th century AD.

# Shows
- No other text from Greek or Roman antiquity survives in anything close to this number of copies.
- The gap between composition and the earliest surviving copy is far shorter for the New Testament, roughly a century, than for Caesar, roughly nine hundred years.

# Limits
- The count measures how often a text was copied, which for the New Testament is a function of a church that grew and kept copying for fifteen centuries. Most of the 5,800 are medieval minuscules.
- More copies means more variants, not fewer. Bart Ehrman's figure of several hundred thousand variant readings across the manuscripts is the same fact read from the other end, and both figures are true at once.
- Copies establish what the text said. They establish nothing about whether what it said is true.
- Roman history does not rest mainly on literary manuscripts. Inscriptions, coins, papyri and public documents carry much of it, and the Gospel events have no comparable documentary record.
