**Verdict:** Confirmed from the author's own copy, https://drive.google.com/file/d/10jGyT9-5BVseQaszKas_QaWFBthi_Or2/view, linked from Cutter's Google Sites page and read in full. It has 27 numbered pages and no version label, and the pages below are its own. The node does not cite Cutter. The earlier snippet's account holds, with one precision. Cutter sets out the objection as six premises, the third being that no one's earthly deeds merit infinite punishment (p. 6), and says his responses target the second, the step from everlasting suffering as punishment to an infinite punishment (pp. 6, 8). His two lines are that everlasting suffering does not entail infinite suffering (section 3), and that infinite suffering does not entail infinite negative well-being (section 4, pp. 1–2). He does not argue for the third premise so much as leave it standing. He notes a culpability difficulty for the reply that offences against a God of infinite dignity merit infinite punishment (p. 7, citing Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* Ia2ae q. 87 a. 4, and Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo* I.21). On p. 25 he sets that reply aside, along with other replies that ask us to revise our ordinary intuitions about what people deserve. On p. 25 he also concedes that, taking desert intuitions at face value, a hell of permanent extreme agony should be rejected, and claims only that a punishment involving everlasting suffering need not violate such constraints (pp. 25–26). On p. 2 he says the paper accepts that the sufferings of hell are properly construed as retributive punishment. Change for the node, not yet made: under Disputed By, add the bullet `- Brian Cutter does not dispute that no earthly deeds merit an infinite punishment. He denies instead that everlasting suffering must be an infinitely severe punishment, since it need not add up to infinite suffering, and infinite suffering need not mean infinitely negative well-being.[^cutter-forthcoming] He sets aside the reply that offences against a God of infinite dignity merit infinite punishment, and grants that, on ordinary intuitions about desert, a hell of permanent extreme agony should be rejected.` and add the footnote `[^cutter-forthcoming]: Brian Cutter, "Hell and Proportionality", *Oxford Studies in Philosophy of Religion* (forthcoming); author's draft, https://drive.google.com/file/d/10jGyT9-5BVseQaszKas_QaWFBthi_Or2/view, pp. 1–2, 6–8, 25–26.` The footnote follows citation style rule 8.
